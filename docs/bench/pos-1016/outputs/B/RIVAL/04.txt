I can't give you a number or confirm readiness, and any figure I made up would end up in a written commitment to procurement. The error rate has to come from your own beta data. Two weeks is enough time to measure it.

**Get the number from beta data**

1. Define the error before you count. For procurement, I'd track these separately:
   - Wrong person assigned (misattribution).
   - Item assigned to someone who wasn't on the call.
   - Hallucinated item that nobody committed to.
   - Missed item.
   - Wrong content or due date.

   "Action-item assignment error rate" could mean any of these, and they differ a lot in harm. Wrong-person and invented items are the dangerous ones because they land in task lists and inboxes.
2. Pull a random sample of consented calls from the beta workspaces, stratified by audio quality, number of speakers, accents, cross-talk, and workspace. Have two people label the correct action items and owners independently, and resolve disagreements. Aim for a few hundred items, not dozens. As a rule of thumb, even zero observed errors in 100 items only supports "under about 3%" at 95% confidence.
3. Report the rate with a confidence interval, broken out by error type and by audio condition. Use the upper bound or the worst-case segment in anything external, not the average.
4. Check the sample for bias. Twelve friendly beta workspaces over two months may not resemble a large client's calls, such as long multi-party calls, jargon, or poor conference-room audio. Say so in the response.

Since the vendor model is locked, errors have to be reduced in your own layers: prompts, speaker-to-attendee mapping, and confidence thresholds.

**Risks I'd resolve before launch**

- Auto-assigning and auto-emailing everyone on the invite turns a model error into a visible mistake, possibly to external people. For agencies and consultancies, the invite often includes their clients. A summary that leaks internal discussion or blames the wrong person is a real incident. I'd launch with a review step, where the organizer confirms items before assignment and email, at least for the new client. If that isn't possible, hold items below a confidence threshold for review.
- Transcripts are untrusted input. Someone on a call can say "assign this to the CEO" or "email the summary to this address," and the model may act on it. Test for this, including injection via shared documents or screen text if you ingest them. Also test that the model only assigns to actual invitees, and that email recipients come from the invite, never from model output.
- Check cross-workspace and cross-attendee boundaries. Confirm a summary or task can't reach someone outside the meeting or the tenant, and that name collisions (two Alexes) don't misroute.
- Check state and failure handling. If extraction fails halfway, make sure nothing partial gets assigned or emailed, and that a correction or undo exists for wrongly assigned items.

I'd treat all of these as hypotheses to test with synthetic calls where the right answer is known, not as findings. I haven't run anything.

**What to say to procurement in the meantime**

Don't commit to a single figure before the measurement exists. Something like:

> "We are measuring action-item assignment accuracy on a labeled sample of [N] items from [M] workspaces and will provide results, with error types and confidence intervals, by [date]. Assignments can be reviewed by the meeting organizer before they are sent."

Once measured, state the figure with its scope: sample size, conditions, error definition, and interval. Launch-page copy should be softer than the contract language. "Suggested action items, reviewed by you" is accurate and defensible. "X% accurate" is a claim you will be held to.
