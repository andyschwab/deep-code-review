Ship the languages that pass a gate, and don't commit to all six by flu season. Spanish performance tells you little about languages with fewer resources, and the cost of an error is high. A mistranslated "do not drink" or a wrong clinic time can cause real harm.

**1. Tier the content by risk**

Decide this with the health departments, not just engineering.
- **Tier 1:** outbreak alerts, water advisories, anything with "do/don't" safety instructions. Highest harm.
- **Tier 2:** clinic times, locations, deadlines. Errors waste people's time and erode trust, but are usually recoverable.
- **Tier 3:** general information and reminders.

**2. Use reviewed templates to shrink the review burden**

Your scarcest resource is fluent reviewers, so spend their time where it counts. Most notices are recurring types. For each language, have reviewers approve a library of templates once (boil-water advisory, clinic announcement, and so on). Fill the variable parts (dates, times, addresses, phone numbers) deterministically from structured data, not through free-form translation. This covers much of your volume with a small, one-time review cost. Free-form machine translation is then reserved for novel content.

**3. Build automated checks, but treat them as triage**

- Verify that numbers, dates, times, URLs, phone numbers and place names survive translation exactly.
- Run language and script identification, and check against a reviewed glossary of key terms.
- Back-translate with a different model and flag meaning drift, especially negations and conditionals.
- Check SMS encoding. Non-Latin scripts use UCS-2, which splits messages sooner, so test segment counts and truncation.

Back-translation and model-based quality scores are weaker in low-resource languages and can look fine when the translation is wrong. They can route items to review, but they can't certify safety.

**4. Evaluate on meaning errors, not fluency scores**

For each language, build a test set of roughly 50 to 100 realistic notices that includes hard cases: negation, conditionals, units, date formats, dialect variation, and terms with no direct equivalent. Have reviewers label errors as critical (changes the action a reader takes), major, or minor. Be honest about statistics: zero critical errors in 60 samples only bounds the true rate at about 5%. That is too weak to justify unreviewed Tier 1 publishing, and it's another reason to lean on templates.

**5. Find reviewers without a translator budget**

Ask the counties first. Many have language-access obligations and may have bilingual staff, interpreter contracts, or small honorarium budgets. Other sources are community organizations, refugee and immigrant services, university language programs, and hospital interpreter services. Aim for two independent reviewers on Tier 1 templates and one for the rest. Treat volunteer capacity as unreliable and ask for specific time commitments in writing.

**6. Set publish rules per language and tier**

- **Auto-publish:** only reviewed templates with deterministically filled, verified fields, in languages that passed evaluation.
- **Human review before publishing:** free-form content, and everything in Tier 1 for languages without a passing evaluation.
- **No qualified reviewer available:** this is the hard case. Waiting can also hurt people. Agree on a policy in advance with each health officer. Options include publishing English plus a short reviewed core message with an interpreter-line or 211 pointer, or publishing a clearly labeled machine translation with the officer's explicit sign-off. Engineering shouldn't make that tradeoff in the moment.

**7. Operate safely after launch**

- Label translations as automatically generated, in the target language.
- Give readers an easy way to report errors, and monitor those reports.
- Build a kill switch and a correction and retraction process, including re-sending corrected SMS.
- Pin the model version and re-run the evaluation before any change.
- Log source, translation, checks, reviewer, and approval for each publication.
- Run shadow mode (generate but don't publish) before launch to see real-world error rates.
- Consider whether some communities are better served by audio or low-literacy formats.

**8. Eight-week sketch**

- **Weeks 1-2:** confirm reviewer commitments per language, finalize tiers and fallback policy with officers, and rank languages by reviewer availability.
- **Weeks 2-4:** build templates, glossaries, and automated checks.
- **Weeks 3-5:** run reviewer evaluations and fix issues.
- **Weeks 5-7:** shadow mode in the first languages that pass.
- **Week 8:** limited launch. Languages without reviewers get the fallback policy, not unreviewed auto-publish.
