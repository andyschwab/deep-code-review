# Pre-launch review: fraud-score payment holds

I haven't seen your data, so everything below is a plan to run, not a finding. Agree the go/no-go criteria before anyone looks at the results, so the business deadline can't quietly move them.

## 1. What to measure

**Start with capacity, since it's your hardest constraint.**
- Using the three months of shadow scores, calculate how many claims would have been held per week at candidate thresholds. Include your worst weeks, such as storm-season surges.
- Compare that to what eight investigators can clear. Set the threshold from capacity and precision together, not from a score that looks "high".
- Estimate queue age: how long would the median and 90th-percentile hold last? A hold that sits for weeks is a harm in its own right.

**Then model quality, with an honest view of the labels.**
- Your outcome labels are probably contaminated. Past fraud findings reflect which claims investigators chose to look at, so the model may just be learning past referral habits. Say so in the review.
- Measure precision, recall and calibration at the proposed threshold against the best available outcomes (confirmed fraud, denials, recoveries). Report uncertainty intervals, not single numbers.
- Draw a random sample of low-scoring claims for investigator review. That is the only way to estimate what the model misses.
- Compare against your current process. The question is whether the model beats the baseline, not whether it's good in the abstract.

**Look at who bears the false positives.**
- Break down hold rates and false-positive rates by line of business, claim type, region, tenure, claim size, language and any available proxies for protected characteristics. Check for disparate impact and involve compliance or counsel in interpreting it.
- Estimate the cost of a wrongly held claim: days of delay, complaints, litigation exposure and customer loss. Weigh that against the fraud dollars you expect to catch.

**Check stability.** Look at score drift week to week, behaviour during a catastrophe event, and sensitivity to missing or late data fields.

## 2. How to treat flagged claimants

- **A flag is a reason to look, not a finding.** Holds should route to a human who makes the decision. Nobody should be denied or accused because of a score alone.
- **Cap hold duration.** Set a maximum review time, tied to your state prompt-payment and unfair-claims-practice rules (check these with legal). Decide what happens at expiry: release payment, or escalate to a named manager. Don't let claims sit indefinitely.
- **Protect urgent needs.** Consider paying undisputed portions or time-critical items, such as emergency repairs and temporary accommodation, while the rest is reviewed. Decide this explicitly rather than leaving it to chance.
- **Communicate neutrally.** Use plain language that says the claim needs additional review and gives a timeframe and a contact. Don't hint at fraud. Have legal approve the templates.
- **Give investigators the tools to overturn the model.** Show the main drivers of the score, record every release or confirm decision, and treat a quick, well-reasoned release as a normal outcome.
- **Offer a route to challenge.** A claimant should be able to ask for a review and reach a person.
- **Ramp up gradually.** Start with a high threshold or a fraction of claims, ideally with a randomly held-out group that isn't held, so you can measure the real effect on outcomes and delays. Add a kill switch with named owners, and pre-agreed triggers for pausing (for example, queue age beyond X days or complaint spikes).

## 3. What to document before go-live

- **Intended use and limits:** what the score is for, what it must not be used for, and who owns it.
- **Validation:** data, label definitions and their known biases, results with uncertainty, subgroup analyses, and the threshold rationale linked to investigator capacity.
- **Operating procedure:** decision rights, SLAs, escalation paths, surge plan, and what investigators must record for each case.
- **Claimant-facing material:** communication templates, appeal route, and the regulatory timelines you've mapped (with legal sign-off).
- **Monitoring plan:** weekly metrics (hold volume, queue age, release rate, complaints, subgroup rates), drift checks, a periodic audit sample, and a retraining and change-control process.
- **Rollback criteria and kill-switch procedure**, with who can invoke it.
- **Audit trail:** for every hold, the score, features or drivers, reviewer, decision and timestamps, so you can reconstruct any case later.
- **Known gaps:** anything unverified or deferred, written down rather than omitted.
- **Sign-offs:** claims leadership, investigations, compliance and legal, and a go/no-go record.

## A practical timeline

With roughly eleven weeks until next quarter, a sensible sequence is: finish the backtest and subgroup analysis, run the audit sample of low-scoring claims, draft the procedures and templates, then do a small staged launch at the quarter start. Full rollout should wait until the queue and complaint metrics hold up.

If the results show the model can't meet your capacity at acceptable precision, the right answer may be a narrower launch, such as only high-value claims or only the highest-scoring band, rather than the full deployment the business is expecting. It's better to say that now than after launch.
