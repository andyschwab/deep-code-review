Seven weeks is tight but workable if you treat the launch as a staged, reversible rollout rather than a single switch-on. Everything below is general guidance; I haven't seen your data or shadow results, so adjust to what you find.

**1. Decide what the shadow test must show (weeks 1-2)**

Shadow mode shows what the model would have charged, not how riders and drivers would react. Before launch, write down pass/fail criteria for:
- Price distribution versus current tiers: median, tails, and how often the model exceeds the old maximum.
- Stability: how much a quote changes for the same trip over a few minutes. Jumpy prices erode trust.
- Behavior in rare conditions: concerts, storms, outages, and data feed failures. Replay or simulate these, since shadow data may not contain them.
- Driver earnings: would the model have paid drivers more, less, or more unevenly than today, by city, hour, and zone?

**2. Fairness and rider-history risk**

Rider history is your highest-risk input. Check whether features like past acceptance of high fares, ride frequency, or trip origin act as proxies for willingness to pay or for protected characteristics. Compare average prices across neighborhoods grouped by demographic data (census-level is fine) after controlling for distance, time, and demand. Consider dropping or constraining history features for launch. Also have your lawyer review pricing rules on discrimination, consumer protection, and any algorithmic pricing disclosure requirements in your four cities. Price-gouging rules during declared emergencies can cap fares regardless of what the model says.

**3. Guardrails in the serving path**

- Hard price cap and floor, and a limit on how fast a fare can rise.
- A fallback to the old surge tiers if the model errors, times out, or inputs look stale or out of range.
- A kill switch the engineer can flip without a deploy, with a named person on call who has authority to use it.
- Model and feature version stamped on every quote.
- Fare caps for emergencies, ideally tied to a manual toggle.

**4. Logging and retention (do this first, because the clock is running)**

With 30-day trip retention and limited logging, you won't be able to investigate a complaint or compare holiday behavior to baseline. Start now:
- Log every quote: timestamp, zone, model version, input feature values, quoted fare, what the old tiers would have charged, whether the rider accepted, and the driver payout.
- Keep a privacy-reviewed, pseudonymized or aggregated version of this for much longer, at least through the holiday season plus a few months. Ask legal what you're allowed to hold and for how long.
- Confirm that logs are sufficient to reproduce any individual price after the fact.

**5. Rollout design**

- Launch in the two cities with the most stable, best-understood demand, and avoid your most holiday-volatile city.
- Start with a small share of trips, or off-peak hours, then widen in steps with explicit go/no-go checks.
- Keep a holdout group on the old tiers so you can measure real impact. Because riders and drivers share a marketplace, randomizing by city, zone, or time block usually works better than by individual rider.
- Avoid ramping up during the biggest holiday peaks. If you can't be fully ramped by a set date, push the scale-up past the holiday rather than compressing testing.

**6. Monitoring once live**

Build a dashboard and alerts before launch for:
- Average and 95th/99th percentile fares versus the old tiers.
- Conversion (quote to booking), cancellations, and ride completion.
- Driver earnings per hour, driver supply, and acceptance.
- Price disparities across zones.
- Complaint and support-ticket volume by topic.

Decide who reviews it daily during the first weeks and what thresholds trigger a rollback.

**7. Communication and support**

Riders should see a clear fare before they confirm, and support agents need a script for "why did my price change?" Tell drivers how earnings are calculated and what's changing; surprises in pay are a faster route to churn and press than rider price complaints.

**Rough timeline**

- Weeks 1-2: logging fixes, success criteria, legal and fairness review kickoff.
- Weeks 3-4: guardrails, fallback, kill switch, replay of stress scenarios.
- Week 5: dry run of rollback and on-call process, final legal sign-off.
- Weeks 6-7: small-share launch in both cities, daily review, scale only if checks pass.

If any of the legal, fairness, or logging items aren't done by week 5, I'd tell leadership the honest options are a smaller launch (fewer trips, no rider-history features) or a delay. That is cheaper than a pricing incident during the holidays.
