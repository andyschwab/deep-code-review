No, I wouldn't make that claim, and I can't give you a safe accuracy percentage, because any number I suggested would be invented. Publishing a figure you haven't measured is the riskiest option on the table.

**Why the claim doesn't hold up**

- **"Rely on them instead of paying for legal advice" is the main problem.** It tells café owners to skip a lawyer on documents like leases, which can carry personal guarantees, auto-renewals and liability exposure. If a summary misses or softens one of those and someone loses money, that sentence is the first thing a plaintiff or regulator will quote.
- **It may also create legal exposure beyond false advertising.** Marketing a tool as a substitute for legal advice can raise unauthorized-practice-of-law questions in some jurisdictions. It can also make your disclaimers look contradictory. This varies by location, so it's worth a short lawyer conversation.
- **"Accurate" is an objective claim you need evidence for.** In the US, advertising claims need substantiation at the time you make them, and other jurisdictions have similar rules. A figure with nothing behind it is the worst version of this.
- **Average accuracy hides the failures that matter.** A tool can be right on 95% of clauses and still wrong on the one indemnity or termination clause that costs someone their business. Readers of a single number will assume errors are minor and evenly spread.
- **A closed beta of 200 users probably isn't a measurement.** Unless you have been systematically checking outputs against the source contracts, you don't have an accuracy rate yet.

**What you can do before the 3rd (about 2.5 weeks)**

1. **Measure something real.** Pull a sample of, say, 100 to 150 clauses across lease and supplier agreements, weighted toward risky clause types. Define what counts as an error, separating wrong, missing-a-key-term and misleading-by-omission. Have at least two people score independently. Even a lawyer reviewing a small sample for a few hours is far cheaper than a full legal review, and is likely within reach.
2. **Report what you find honestly.** If you publish a figure, state the sample size, the contract types, and the error definition, and give a range rather than a bare "9X%." If the high-risk categories perform worse, say so internally and fix or flag them before launch.
3. **Don't publish a number you can't reproduce.** If the measurement isn't done by the 3rd, launch without one.

**Safer launch copy**

Something like:

- "Understand your contract in plain English before you sign."
- "Summaries help you spot clauses worth a closer look and questions to ask."
- "Not legal advice. AI-generated summaries can contain errors. For high-stakes terms, consult a lawyer."

This still sells the real value, which is clarity and preparation. It also positions you as helping people decide when they need a lawyer, which is both truthful and a strong pitch to people who've never read a lease closely.

**Product changes that make the honest framing credible**

- Show each summary next to the original clause text.
- Flag high-stakes clause types (personal guarantees, auto-renewal, indemnification, limitation of liability, termination, exclusivity, non-compete) with a prompt to get them reviewed.
- Say plainly when the tool is unsure or the clause is unusual, rather than producing a confident summary.
- Log user-reported errors so you can keep measuring after launch.

**Practical next steps**

- Tell your marketing lead the "instead of legal advice" line is off the table, and why: it is the claim most likely to hurt users and the company.
- Spend a small amount on a one-time lawyer review of your landing page copy, terms of service and disclaimers. That is a much narrower task than reviewing your outputs, and it's the one place I'd say budget is worth finding.
- If the measurement isn't finished by the launch date, delay the accuracy claim, not the launch.

I haven't seen your product or any of its outputs, so this is general guidance rather than legal advice. A lawyer in your jurisdiction should confirm the specifics.
