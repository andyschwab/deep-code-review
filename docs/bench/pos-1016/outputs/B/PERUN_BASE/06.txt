# Pre-launch review: fraud-score payment holds

**Bottom line:** I can't tell you the holds are safe or fair, and no test will. This plan gives you a harm inventory, a measurement plan, and gates. The launch decision belongs to your accountable owner, and I'd name that person in writing now. One risk stands out. With eight investigators, a hold can become a de facto denial through delay, even if no claim is ever formally denied.

## Harm inventory (who is hurt, worst case)

- **Unfair outcomes:** certain groups (by age, postcode, language, claim type, disability, first-time claimants) are held more often. Worst case: systematically delayed payouts for people already in crisis.
- **Delay harm:** a held claimant waits for a house repair or a replacement car. Worst case: financial distress or abandoned claims.
- **Automation bias:** investigators treat the score as a verdict.
- **Opacity:** claimants don't know why they are waiting or how to respond.
- **Excess agency:** the hold fires with no human decision. Treat it as a gated, consequential action.

## What to measure

Replace "test for bias" with one test row per harm: probe set, metric, owner-set threshold, and stop condition. I won't suggest numeric thresholds. Set them yourselves, and for any automatic denial (which should not exist) tolerance should be zero.

1. **Hold volume vs capacity.** Using the three months of shadow scores, count how many holds each candidate threshold would have created per week, and compare that with what eight people can review. Then pick the threshold from capacity, not the reverse. Stop condition: projected queue growth that can't clear within your target review time.
2. **Time-in-hold.** Measure the distribution of review time, especially the tail. Set a maximum hold duration and an escalation path. Stop condition: breaching it triggers auto-release or a threshold change.
3. **Subgroup disparity.** Slice hold rates and, where outcomes are known, false-positive rates by segment. Include a known-good control set (claims later confirmed legitimate) and a known-bad one (confirmed fraud). Beware proxies such as postcode. Stop condition: a gap above your set tolerance blocks launch for that segment.
4. **Label validity.** Shadow-mode data is skewed. Claims that were paid unexamined only reveal fraud if someone caught it later, and past investigations reflect past investigator attention. Any precision or recall figure is **UNVERIFIED** until you check the labels' provenance. Don't quote a rate externally until then.
5. **Investigator agreement.** Run a blind sample where investigators review claims without seeing the score, then compare against the score to check for anchoring.
6. **Robustness and data hygiene.** Test for score drift, inputs from claimants' free text (injection or odd formatting if text features are used), and leakage of other claimants' data into investigator views.

## How to treat flagged claimants

- **Human gate:** a high score creates a *review task*, not a payment stop that nobody owns. If you keep the automatic hold, it needs a clock, an owner, and an automatic release path.
- **Staged rollout:** start with a threshold that the capacity analysis says you can clear, covering a limited slice of claims. Widen only after the metrics above hold. A small cohort also limits the harm from mistakes.
- **Triage by need:** undisputed portions of a claim, urgent living costs, or emergency repairs should be payable while the rest is reviewed. Ask counsel and claims leadership whether partial payment is feasible.
- **Plain-language notice:** tell claimants their claim is under additional review, what they can do, and a realistic timeframe. Never say "flagged as fraud."
- **Appeal and override:** a named person can release a hold, and investigators can override the score with a reason code, which you then log.
- **Show the score as a defined tier**, such as Routine / Elevated / High / Referral-only, with each tier defined, never "87% fraud likely." Show investigators the contributing factors, not just a number.
- **Kill switch:** anyone on the team with authority can revert to shadow mode, and you've rehearsed doing so.

## Document before going live

- **Model card:** intended use and out-of-scope uses, training/eval data provenance, performance by subgroup, known limits, owner, model version. A metric you haven't measured is written as UNVERIFIED, never a filled cell.
- **Data card** for training and evaluation data, covering composition, consent/licensing, and known gaps.
- **Harm inventory and test rows** above, with results, residual risk, and who accepted it.
- **Operating procedure:** thresholds and why, hold time limits, escalation, override logging, and the review cadence. Re-review whenever the model, data, or threshold changes.
- **Monitoring plan:** weekly hold volume, backlog, time-in-hold, subgroup rates, and overrides, with named owners.

## Route to counsel / compliance (don't decide this yourselves)

Whether the holds fall under claims-handling timelines, anti-discrimination rules, or automated-decision or notice requirements in your jurisdiction is a legal question. Ask counsel which regimes apply, including any insurance-regulator guidance on AI use. For frameworks to name in that conversation: the NIST AI RMF, ISO/IEC 42001, and the EU AI Act (if relevant to you). Whether any of them binds you, and any deadlines, is for counsel to say. Also ask whether recommending disclosure of automated screening to claimants is advisable or required.

## On the timeline

If the capacity analysis shows eight people can't clear the projected volume, the honest options are a higher threshold, a narrower rollout, or delaying the start. Launching with a backlog turns your fraud control into a payout-delay mechanism, and that is the outcome this review exists to catch.
