# Pre-expansion safety plan: translated health notices

**Bottom line:** I can't tell you this is safe to expand, and no test will let anyone say that. What you can do in eight weeks is name the harms, measure them per language, and set a publishing rule that doesn't depend on reviewers you don't have. The ship decision belongs to you and the county departments.

## Harm inventory (who is affected, worst case)

- **Critical-field errors:** wrong time, date, address, dose, or phone number. A resident goes to the wrong clinic or misses one.
- **Meaning inversions and omissions:** "do not drink" becomes "drink," or a boil-water step is dropped. This is the worst realistic case, since people act on water advisories immediately.
- **Fabricated additions:** the model adds advice that isn't in the source.
- **Uneven quality across languages:** your Spanish pilot tells you nothing about the six new languages. The least-served communities get the worst output, which is an equity harm.
- **Register, dialect, and script problems:** wrong dialect, stilted or alarming tone, and SMS encoding problems such as truncation, split messages, or broken characters.
- **Irreversible publishing:** a text alert can't be recalled, so a bad one is corrected only by a second message.
- **Over-reliance and missing disclosure:** staff assume the translation is fine, and residents can't tell it's machine-generated or where to get the English or a human.

## Publishing rule: gate by content type, not by language

1. **Pre-approved templates.** Have fluent volunteers or bilingual staff verify, once per language, fixed templates for recurring notice types (clinic hours, boil-water, outbreak alert) with slots for variable details. Fill the slots with deterministic code, not the model. This is the best use of scarce reviewers.
2. **Free-text translation.** Machine-translate it, but a human reviews before publishing. If no reviewer is available in a language, it doesn't auto-publish. It goes out as English plus a short verified template pointing to a phone line or interpreter.
3. **Urgent, irreversible notices.** Water advisories and outbreak alerts never auto-publish unreviewed. Decide in advance who the on-call reviewer is and what the fallback is when nobody is reachable.
4. **Disclosure.** Label machine-translated text as such, state that the English is authoritative, and give a contact. Whether disclosure is legally required is a question for counsel.

Languages with no reviewer capacity should launch at tier 1 only, or not launch before flu season. That is a legitimate outcome.

## Measurement: one test row per harm

For each row, specify the probe set, a positive control, a negative control, slices by language and notice type, the metric, an owner-set threshold, and a stop condition.

- **Critical-field errors:** an automated check that every number, date, time, address, and phone number in the source appears correctly in the output. The positive control is a deliberately altered translation, and the negative control is a known-good one. Propose zero tolerance for any failure in a published urgent notice. The stop condition is blocking auto-publish for that language and notice type.
- **Meaning inversions and omissions:** a reviewer-scored sample of safety-critical sentences, including negations, "boil" and "do not drink" instructions, and warnings. Include planted negation-flip errors to confirm reviewers catch them. Back-translation through the same model is only a weak screen, because it can hide errors.
- **Fabrication:** compare output against source for added claims.
- **Uneven quality:** report results per language, never only in aggregate.
- **SMS rendering:** test real devices and carriers for each script and its segment count.
- **Process checks:** probe for injection or odd formatting in pasted notice text, and for phone-number leakage in logs.

Set a probe budget up front, such as how many items per language one reviewer can score in a few hours. Name who reviews failures. Set thresholds yourselves. I won't suggest a "typical" acceptable error rate, and any rate you haven't measured is **UNVERIFIED**.

## Reviewer shortage

Ask county staff, community health workers, community organizations, and university language programs for volunteers. Use a second reviewer on critical sentences only, and track reviewer agreement. Recruit now, since this is your critical path. Be honest in any documentation that review depth is limited.

## Before launch

- Write a model/data card per language: intended use, what was measured, reviewer coverage, known limitations, owner and version. Unmeasured items stay marked UNVERIFIED.
- Add a correction path: a fast way to pull a web translation and send a corrective text, plus a resident feedback channel.
- Name an owner for each language and re-review when the model or its prompts change.
- Language-access obligations, and whether these are regulated communications, are for counsel and each county's own attorneys. I can't assert them.

## Eight-week sequence

Weeks 1–2: recruit reviewers, build the harm inventory, and write the critical-field checker. Weeks 3–5: draft and verify templates and run evals per language. Weeks 6–7: dry run and rollback drill. Week 8: the departments decide, language by language, what launches and at which tier.
