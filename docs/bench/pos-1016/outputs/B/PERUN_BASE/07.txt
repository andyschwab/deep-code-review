I can't certify this safe, and nothing here has been run. What follows is a work list, and the ship decision stays with you. The main risk is that a model generates content live for a child, at night, with no parent present and no screen to catch problems.

**1. Harm inventory (write down who is affected and the worst realistic case for each)**
- Frightening, violent or otherwise age-inappropriate content, especially when the child steers toward it ("make the monster eat him").
- Fabricated facts a young child will take as true (animals, safety, "is it okay to...").
- Child disclosures mid-story (hurt, afraid, abuse, a medical emergency), and how the assistant responds.
- Attachment and over-reliance: the assistant as friend, stalling bedtime, "one more story" loops, engagement-driven design.
- Missing AI disclosure, to the child in age-appropriate terms and to the parent.
- Privacy: the child's name, family details and voice recordings flowing into prompts, logs or other families' stories.
- Prompt injection by voice, including TV audio, siblings or a child deliberately trying to jailbreak it.
- Excess agency: purchases, smart-home control, calls or sharing, if the device can do any of them.
- Stereotyped or exclusionary stories, and speech recognition that works worse for some accents, ages or speech patterns.

**2. Safeguards to decide**
- **Human gate.** No human is live at bedtime, so the gate is parent-set boundaries plus hard limits. I'd default any irreversible or consequential action (purchases, sharing or exporting recordings, changing settings, contacting anyone) to off or parent-confirmed in the app. Never let a child's voice authorise it.
- **Layered content control.** Don't rely on the system prompt alone. Add an independent output check before speech, and decide what the speaker says when the check fails or the model is slow: a calm redirect or a pre-written fallback story, never silence or an error tone.
- **Distress protocol.** Define what the assistant says and does when a child discloses harm. Have counsel and a child-safety specialist review it before launch.
- **Honest identity.** If a child asks "are you a person?", the answer is simple and true. Avoid claiming feelings or friendship.
- **Wind-down design.** Stories should end, with a story-count or time cap the parent can set. Avoid cliffhangers and nudges to continue.
- **Uncertainty.** Factual claims inside stories should be framed as story, not fact, and questions like "is that real?" should get a plain, honest answer.

**3. Testing: one row per harm**
Each row should have a probe set, a known-bad positive control (a prompt that must be blocked, to prove the test can fail), a known-good negative control (to catch over-refusal that ruins ordinary stories), the metric, an owner-set threshold, and a stop condition that blocks launch or triggers rollback.
- Zero tolerance is the sensible proposal for gated actions and for failure to handle a child-safety disclosure. Set every other threshold yourselves. I won't suggest a typical rate.
- Probes: child-style steering escalations, interruptions mid-sentence, tricky or silly requests, jailbreak and injection attempts, other-voice audio, and attempts to extract other families' data.
- Slice results by age band, accent and speech pattern, and story theme, not just in aggregate.
- Use real child-directed content review by people qualified to judge it, plus parent reviewers. Don't rely only on automated classifiers.
- Any rate you haven't measured is UNVERIFIED. Don't put "safe for kids" claims on packaging or pages without evidence.
- Use the 40-family pilot for this, with consent. It's too small to show rare harms are absent, so call it exploratory.

**4. Parent-facing decisions**
- Parents can't see transcripts on the device, so decide what the app offers: full transcripts, summaries, flagged-event alerts, or all three. Alerts are probably essential for blocked or distress events.
- Retention, deletion, and whether recordings are used to improve the model. Make these controls in the app, not buried in terms.
- Setup choices: age band, banned themes, name and detail use, story limits, quiet hours.
- Plain disclosure of what the assistant is, what it stores, and which third-party model providers see the data.
- A visible, simple way to stop the assistant: a physical mute and stop control.

**5. Legal routing (for counsel, not for me)**
Children's data and voice recordings, parental consent, any AI-disclosure duty, and whether this counts as a regulated or high-risk use are legal determinations. Ask counsel specifically about the child-privacy regimes in your sale markets, the EU AI Act if you sell there, and ISO/IEC 42001 if buyers ask for it. I'm naming them, not asserting that any applies or when. Retail partners may also impose their own requirements.

**6. Transparency and ownership**
- Write a model and data card: intended use, out-of-scope uses (for example, no advice, no companionship claims), measured performance by subgroup, known failure modes, model version and owner. Leave unmeasured cells marked UNVERIFIED.
- Name one owner per harm, and re-review whenever the model, prompt, safety filter or provider changes.

**Ten-week triage for a team of seven**
- Weeks 1–3: the harm inventory, the gated-action defaults, the distress protocol with outside review, and the fallback behaviour.
- Weeks 3–7: the test rows, the pilot review, and the parent app controls.
- Weeks 7–9: counsel sign-off, fixes, and the model card.
- Week 10: a go/no-go meeting against your own stop conditions. If the distress protocol or content checks aren't ready, shrink scope (preset story themes, no free steering) rather than shipping looser safeguards.
