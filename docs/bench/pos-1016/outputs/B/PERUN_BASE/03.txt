**Bottom line:** I can't tell you the model is fair or safe, and no pre-launch test could. What you can do in seven weeks is build a harm inventory, measure each harm, put hard guardrails and a human gate around the money-moving behavior, and give leadership a residual-risk report. The launch decision stays with your owner.

## 1. Harm inventory (finish this week)

For each, write who is affected and the worst realistic case.

- **Unfair outcomes for riders.** Rider history and trip details can proxy for protected traits (pickup neighborhood, trip patterns, device, payment type). The worst case is systematically higher fares for particular areas or groups.
- **Exploitative personalization.** Charging a rider more because the model infers they will tolerate it, such as someone with a long history who is stranded or in a hurry.
- **Driver harm.** Earnings can drop or become unpredictable for some drivers or zones, and drivers may not understand why.
- **Hallucination-as-fact analogue.** A quoted fare presented as a market price when it is a model artifact, such as a demand spike from a data glitch or an out-of-distribution input.
- **Deceptive patterns.** Urgency messaging, hidden fare changes between quote and charge, or hard-to-find fare explanations.
- **Missing disclosure.** Riders and drivers can't tell the price is algorithmic and individualized.
- **Unsafe automation.** Fares that are costly to riders, and hard to reverse once charged, with no human gate.

## 2. Measurement plan: one row per harm

Each row needs a probe set with a known-bad positive control and a known-good negative control, sliced by subgroup or zone. It also needs a metric, an owner-set threshold, and a stop condition. I'm not proposing thresholds. Your owner sets them, and I'd propose zero tolerance for any quote above the hard cap.

- **Rider fairness:** compare fare, and fare relative to demand, across neighborhood and demographic-proxy slices, controlling for trip distance and time. The positive control is a synthetic model that deliberately penalizes one zone, which your test must flag. Stop condition: a gap above the owner threshold that you can't explain by cost or demand.
- **Personalization:** hold the trip and conditions fixed and vary only rider history. Large fare differences from history alone are the finding.
- **Driver earnings:** compare earnings per hour and variance by zone and driver tenure, old tiers versus model.
- **Robustness:** replay stress inputs such as sensor or feed outages, event-end crowds, and weather. Holiday demand is probably outside what your shadow period saw, so treat it as unverified.
- **Quote integrity:** check that every charged fare equals the quote shown, with zero tolerance.
- **Security and leakage:** probe whether inputs (such as promo codes or location spoofing) can manipulate fares, and whether rider data leaks through explanations or driver-facing views.

Shadow mode shows what the model would do, not how riders and drivers react. Say so in the report.

## 3. Logging and the 30-day retention limit

This is your biggest gap. With 30 days of trip-level data you can't audit a complaint from week six, compare holiday behavior to baseline, or show a regulator or reviewer what happened. Before launch:

- Log every quote with model version, input features, output, applicable cap, and whether a guardrail fired.
- Keep aggregated, privacy-minimized subgroup metrics well beyond 30 days.
- Decide the retention period deliberately with counsel, since rider-history data carries privacy obligations. Don't just extend it by default.

## 4. Controls (the floor)

- **Hard fare ceiling and a rate-of-change limit** that the model can't override.
- **Emergency/disaster override** that freezes or caps fares, since price-gouging rules in some places apply.
- **Kill switch** to revert to fixed tiers, with a named on-call person and a tested rollback.
- **Human approval** for changes to caps or model versions.
- **Staged ramp:** a small share of trips first, with explicit stop conditions.
- **Rider-facing explanation** that the fare is dynamic, plus a fare-review or complaint path.
- **Driver-facing transparency** about how earnings are affected.
- **Confidence or uncertainty:** if you show estimates, use defined coarse labels, not false-precision numbers.

## 5. Route to counsel

Whether personalized or algorithmic pricing is regulated in your cities, whether disclosure is legally required, and which anti-discrimination, consumer-protection, price-gouging, and driver-pay rules apply are legal questions. I'm not asserting any of those duties, so get counsel to name the regimes before launch.

## 6. Governance and timeline

- Write down an owner for each output harm. On a four-person team that may be one person, but the name still goes on paper.
- Re-review when the model, features, or data change.
- Publish an internal model card covering intended use, limitations, and subgroup results. Anything you haven't measured is marked **UNVERIFIED**, not filled in.

**Seven weeks, roughly:** weeks 1–2 for the inventory, logging, and guardrails; weeks 3–5 for the test rows and counsel review; week 6 for a go/no-go on the evidence; week 7 for a small ramp in one city. If the holiday-demand test or the logging fix slips, launch later or cap more tightly. Don't skip the gates.
