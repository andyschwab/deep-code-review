**Bottom line:** I can't tell you the model is safe or unbiased, and no review can. This plan gives you what to measure, how to treat claimants, and what to write down. The launch decision is yours. The biggest issue is that eight investigators set the real hold capacity, not the model.

**Harm inventory (who is hurt, worst case)**
- **Wrongful holds:** honest claimants in hardship (a burned-out home, a totaled car) go unpaid for weeks. This is the main harm.
- **Biased flagging:** certain groups, areas, languages or first-time claimants get held more often. Features such as ZIP or claim channel can act as proxies.
- **Queue overflow:** if holds exceed 8 people's capacity, the hold becomes indefinite. A storm event will spike volume and correlated flags.
- **Anchoring:** investigators treat a high score as proof.
- **Opaque or accusatory handling:** claimants get no explanation, or are told they're suspected of fraud.
- **Gaming and leakage:** fraudsters probe the score. If free-text notes or documents feed the model, planted content can manipulate it. Claimant data could also leak into notes or letters.

**What to measure (one test row per harm)**
Every row needs a probe set with a known-bad and a known-good control, a metric, an owner-set threshold, and a stop condition. I'm not proposing typical rates, so thresholds are yours to set.
1. **Wrongful holds:** your three months of shadow scores were never investigated, so you have no fraud labels for them. Have investigators blind-review a random sample of flagged claims, plus a sample of unflagged ones. Labels taken from past investigations are selection-biased. Zero errors in n reviews only bounds the rate near 3/n at 95%, so size n to your tolerance.
2. **Bias:** flag rate and false-positive rate by slice (age band, language, ZIP or region, claim type, tenure, catastrophe-event claims). Check proxy features. Stop condition: a gap above your threshold blocks the stage.
3. **Capacity:** simulate holds per week against investigator hours, including a storm-spike scenario. Measure time-to-decision. Zero tolerance for any hold with no assigned reviewer or past its deadline.
4. **Anchoring:** have investigators review with and without the score, and compare outcomes.
5. **Gaming, injection, leakage:** test with fixture claims and accounts, never real claimants.

**How to treat flagged claimants**
- The score should create a review task, not deny or accuse. A payment hold is costly to the claimant, so a human confirms it, and your investigators are that gate.
- Enforce limits in the claims service, not in the model or a document: a daily hold cap tied to staffing, a maximum hold duration, and required reviewer assignment. Decide, with counsel, what happens at the deadline. Releasing payment by default is the safer design.
- Consider paying undisputed portions and exempting declared catastrophe events from automatic holds.
- Show investigators a defined tier (for example Routine / Elevated / High / Needs-review-now, each with a written definition), not a raw score or percentage. Add a one-line reason and an easy override.
- Give claimants neutral notice of the review, a contact, and a path to appeal. Counsel should confirm the wording.

**Rollout, assuming a calendar-quarter start of about 1 January (about 11 weeks)**
- **Weeks 1–2:** brief counsel, name the owners, and build the hold cap, deadline and kill switch in code. The engineer is the bottleneck, so do this first.
- **Weeks 2–6:** run the blind review samples and the bias slices. Investigators' hours are the scarce resource, so budget them now.
- **Weeks 6–8:** extend shadow mode, now logging proposed holds and what the investigators would have done.
- **Weeks 9–11:** staged launch. Start with the top tier only, one claim type, and a low daily cap, then widen only if the stop conditions hold.
- **After launch:** monitor the same metrics under a named owner.

If time runs out, ship narrower, with the top tier only or review-task-only with no holds. Don't compress the evidence steps.

**Documentation before go-live**
- A **model card** covering intended use and out-of-scope uses, subgroup performance, limitations and failure modes, model version, and owner. Any metric you haven't measured is marked UNVERIFIED, never filled in.
- A **data card** covering training data composition, provenance, known gaps, and the label-bias caveat above.
- The harm inventory with the test results and the residual risk.
- The hold policy: caps, deadlines, override and appeal paths, and kill-switch criteria.
- A re-review trigger for any change to the model, the data, or the claim mix.

**Questions for counsel** (applicability and deadlines are theirs to decide)
- Do state claims-handling and prompt-payment rules limit hold duration?
- Do insurance unfair-discrimination rules or regulator guidance on AI and external data apply?
- Are adverse-action or notice duties triggered?
- Do privacy or third-party data rules apply?
- Do any AI-governance regimes (EU AI Act, ISO/IEC 42001, NIST AI RMF) bind you?

Facts to give them: the use case, the features, the hold mechanics, and the planned launch date, which is time-sensitive.
