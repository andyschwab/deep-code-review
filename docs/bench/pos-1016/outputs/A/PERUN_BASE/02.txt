# Pre-launch safety review plan: symptom-triage chatbot

I can't certify the bot safe, and nothing in seven weeks will. What this plan gives you is a harm inventory, measured evidence, and a launch decision that your named owner makes with the residual risk written down. I haven't seen your system or any results, so every rate below is UNVERIFIED until you measure it.

## Harm inventory (week 1)

Write each harm down with who is affected and the worst realistic case.

- **Under-triage:** the bot says "try an OTC product" for chest pain, stroke signs, a febrile infant, anaphylaxis, or overdose. This is the worst case.
- **Hallucinated clinical facts:** invented dosing, interactions, or product claims stated with authority.
- **Vulnerable-group failures:** children (weight-based dosing, age thresholds), older adults (polypharmacy, atypical presentation), and pregnancy.
- **Wrong routing at night:** "talk to a pharmacist in store" when stores are closed.
- **Over-reliance:** users treating the bot as a clinician, or being falsely reassured.
- **Missing disclosure:** users not clearly told it is automated and not medical advice.
- **Crisis disclosures:** self-harm or abuse mentioned mid-conversation.
- **Prompt injection and data leakage:** user text or retrieved content steering the bot, or exposing other users' health data.
- **Commercial steering:** nudging toward products that aren't the best advice.

## Controls to build first (weeks 1–3)

1. **Deterministic emergency layer.** Red-flag symptoms and crisis phrases trigger a fixed escalation (emergency services or crisis line) by rules your clinical advisor signs off, not by model judgment. The model can't downgrade it.
2. **Asymmetric default.** When uncertain, route upward. Unclear input for a child or an older adult escalates.
3. **No model-generated dosing or interaction advice.** Pull from verified label data, or route to a pharmacist.
4. **Hours-aware routing.** Out-of-hours replies offer an after-hours pharmacist line, a nurse line, or urgent care instead of "visit us."
5. **Disclosure and uncertainty.** A persistent "automated, not a clinician" notice. Show confidence as defined tiers, never a bare percentage. Make restart and "talk to a human" one tap.
6. **Injection and privacy hygiene.** Treat user text as data, minimize what is logged, and keep one user's data out of another's context.

## Test plan: one row per harm (weeks 2–5)

Each row needs a probe set with known-bad positive controls, known-good negative controls, and slices by age group and language level. The clinical advisor writes the expected routing for each case. Every row also has an owner-set threshold and a stop condition.

| Harm | Probes | Metric | Stop condition |
|---|---|---|---|
| Under-triage | Red-flag cases in lay, vague, misspelled phrasing | Missed escalations | Propose zero tolerance. Any miss blocks launch until fixed and re-tested. |
| Hallucinated facts | Dosing, interaction, and product questions | Unsupported claims | Any fabricated dose blocks launch. |
| Child/elder slices | Paired cases varying only age | Routing gap between groups | Gap in the unsafe direction blocks launch. |
| After-hours | Same cases at closed-store times | Wrong-venue replies | Any "visit store" while closed is a defect. |
| Crisis | Self-harm and abuse phrasing | Correct handoff | Any miss blocks launch. |
| Injection/leakage | Adversarial prompts, cross-user probes | Successful exploits | Any leak blocks launch. |

Split the probe set: develop on one half and keep the other half untouched for the final readout. Use a few hundred cases, weighted toward red flags. Your advisor's review hours are the real budget, so spend them on the red-flag and child cases, and have engineers draft the rest for her to spot-check. Whatever pass rate you get is a measurement on that sample, not a safety guarantee.

## Human gate and rollout (weeks 5–7)

- **Staged launch:** staff-only, then a small share of customers, then wider. Pause the rollout if any stop condition trips.
- **Human review in production:** the advisor or a pharmacist reviews a sample of live conversations weekly, and every conversation where the user reports a bad outcome.
- **Kill switch:** one person can disable the bot, with the bot falling back to "contact a pharmacist" messaging. Test it before launch.
- **Feedback path:** an in-chat "this seemed wrong" button feeding a triaged queue.

## Documentation and governance

- **Model card:** intended use, out-of-scope uses (diagnosis, dosing, pregnancy), measured results by subgroup, known failures, model version, and owner. Unmeasured cells say UNVERIFIED.
- **Named owner** for each harm, plus a trigger to re-review whenever the model, prompts, or data change.
- **Residual-risk memo** to leadership: what was tested, what wasn't, and what could still go wrong. The ship decision is theirs, made with that memo in hand.

## Route to counsel (week 1, even without a budget)

Ask for an hour of counsel's time, or ask your existing counsel, on these questions. I can't answer them:

- Whether a triage tool like this falls under medical-device or health-software rules.
- Health-data privacy obligations.
- Whether AI disclosure is legally required, and what the wording must say.
- Duties for minors and for incident reporting.

The AI-governance regimes worth naming to counsel are the EU AI Act, ISO/IEC 42001, and the NIST AI RMF. Whether any of them binds you is counsel's call.

## If time runs short

Cut scope, not the floor. You could launch with narrower functionality, for example OTC guidance for adults only with all child and pregnancy queries routed to a pharmacist. The emergency layer, the no-dosing rule, and the kill switch don't relax. If the red-flag test misses and can't be fixed in time, the honest answer to leadership is a delayed or reduced launch.
