The main risk is a fluent description that says something the spec sheet doesn't. Examples are "supports a healthy pregnancy," "safe for teens," a changed dose, a dropped allergen, or an implied cure. For these shoppers, a wrong statement about safety, dosage, allergens, or interactions is the harm that matters. Tone and copy quality are secondary.

**Harms to inventory, with who's affected**
- Hallucinated or inflated health claims (chronic-condition shoppers, older adults).
- Wrong or missing dose, serving, allergen, or warning text (allergic shoppers, parents buying for teens).
- Implied suitability for pregnancy, children, or medication users that the source doesn't support.
- Over-trust: a polished page reads as authoritative.
- Unmarked AI-written content.

**Design: shrink what the AI can say, then spend human time where harm concentrates**

1. **Remove safety-critical fields from generation.** Pull dose, serving size, ingredient list, allergens, and supplier warnings from the spec sheet by deterministic code and display them verbatim. The AI writes only the narrative around them. This removes the worst failures without any review effort.

2. **Tighten the prompt.** Use only facts in the supplied sheet. No condition, disease, or "supports/boosts/treats/prevents" claims unless the sheet states them. Never mention pregnancy, children, or medication suitability. Say "not stated" rather than guess.

3. **Run automated checks on all 1,400 pages** (cheap, no editor needed):
   - A flagged-phrase list: cure, treat, prevent, detox, "safe for," pregnancy, children, "clinically proven," and similar.
   - A diff confirming every number, ingredient, and unit in the description appears in the source.
   - Any page that fails goes to human review or reverts to plain spec-sheet text.

4. **Tier the human review rather than sampling uniformly:**
   - **Tier A, review 100%:** herbals and botanicals, anything with stimulants or interaction potential, products aimed at teens or children, anything flagged by the checks. Two or three people can each read one page quickly because the checks narrow what they look for.
   - **Tier B, sample:** vitamins and protein products with clean checks. Sample size is yours to set. Start high and lower it only if the first batches show no safety-relevant errors.
   - **Fallback:** a page that is not reviewed or does not pass ships with spec-sheet text only, not AI prose. Missing the sale deadline for some pages is better than publishing unreviewed health content.

**Use your 50-page test batch as the measurement set**
Read all 50 against their source sheets and log each error by type (invented claim, wrong number, omitted warning, suitability implication). Don't treat the result as a rate for the whole catalog. It tells you which failure types occur and whether your checks catch them. Before trusting the checks, plant a handful of known-bad descriptions (a fake "treats anxiety" line, an altered dose) and confirm they get flagged. Also run a few clean pages to see how many false alarms the checks raise. Any error rate or "accuracy" figure is UNVERIFIED until measured this way, and I can't supply one.

**Stop conditions (set by you)**
- Zero tolerance: if review finds a wrong dose, missing allergen, or unsupported pregnancy/child/medication statement on a page, pause publishing, find the cause, fix the prompt or check, and re-check that batch.
- Define a threshold for other error types before you start, so you aren't deciding under sale pressure.

**Four-week plan**
- Week 1: lock down field extraction, prompt, and checks; read the 50 pages; run the planted-error test.
- Week 2: regenerate and check everything; review Tier A.
- Week 3: finish Tier A, sample Tier B, fix and re-check failures.
- Week 4: publish in waves (lowest risk first), watch for problems, keep the fallback ready. Don't change prompt or model in the final days without re-checking.

**After launch**
- Add a "report a problem with this page" link and have someone own the inbox.
- Re-review when the model, prompt, or supplier sheets change.
- Keep a one-page record: intended use, what the AI can't say, check results, known gaps, owner.
- Add a plain note that descriptions are AI-assisted and not medical advice. I'm recommending that as a trust control.

**Route to counsel**
Supplement marketing has its own rules on health and structure/function claims, and whether an AI disclosure is legally required is a legal question. Ask a lawyer to name the applicable rules and approve your flagged-phrase list and standard disclaimer. A single lawyer review of the template is far cheaper than reviewing 1,400 pages.

I can't certify the descriptions are accurate or safe. This process lowers risk and shows you where residual risk sits. The decision to publish is yours, and the clearest sign it's not ready is a Tier A page you haven't been able to review.
