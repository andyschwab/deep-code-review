No, I can't confirm that, and you shouldn't say it publicly. I haven't seen your model, data, or any audit results, and I haven't run anything. Even with access, "unbiased" is a claim almost nobody can defend. The defensible claim is "we measured adverse impact and here is what we found."

**Why your setup is a risk**

- Your model learned from historical hiring outcomes. Those outcomes reflect past human decisions, including any bias in them, so the model may be reproducing them efficiently.
- Resumes carry proxies for gender and ethnicity: names, schools, clubs, career gaps, zip codes, and phrasing. Dropping explicit demographic fields doesn't remove them.
- Three beta customers is a small and possibly unrepresentative sample, and cell sizes for some groups will be tiny.
- The ranking gates whether a human reads an application. Regulators and plaintiffs treat that as consequential even though recruiters make the final call.

**Legal exposure (not legal advice, so have counsel review)**

An unsupported "unbiased" claim could be treated as deceptive marketing, and putting it in contracts turns it into a warranty. Employment-screening tools are also increasingly regulated. NYC Local Law 144 requires an independent bias audit and candidate notice. The EU AI Act treats hiring tools as high-risk. Several US states have their own rules, and recent litigation has targeted vendors, not just employers. Your customers will ask about this, so having real answers helps you sell.

**What you can realistically do in five weeks**

1. **Counterfactual testing.** This needs no demographic labels. Take real or synthetic resumes, swap names and gendered or ethnically associated signals, hold qualifications constant, and measure how the rank or score moves. Large shifts are direct evidence of bias.
2. **Adverse-impact analysis.** Ask beta customers for applicant self-identification data, if they collect it. Compute selection or high-rank rates by gender, race/ethnicity, and intersections, then compute impact ratios. The four-fifths rule is a common screening threshold. Report the sample sizes and the uncertainty. Inferring demographics from names is a weaker fallback, and you should label it as such.
3. **Proxy and label review.** Check which features drive rankings and remove or constrain suspicious ones. Compare the model against customers' own hiring patterns to see whether it amplifies them. Consider training on job-relevant criteria instead of past hire decisions alone.
4. **Mitigate and re-measure.** Options include feature removal, reweighting, and threshold adjustments. Re-run the same tests afterward so you can show before and after.
5. **Independent audit.** If any customer hires in NYC, you likely need one anyway. Start scoping with an auditor now, since lead times may exceed five weeks.
6. **Product safeguards.** Include candidate notice support, recruiter guidance that the ranking is advisory, an easy way to review low-ranked applications, ongoing monitoring, and a documented process for handling complaints.

**What to say instead**

If the work above happens and the results hold up, a truthful line is: "We test our ranking model for adverse impact across gender and race/ethnicity before release and on an ongoing basis, and we share summary results with customers." That is a claim about process, and you can back it with documents. Don't claim "bias-free," "unbiased," or "fair" as an outcome. For the contract requests, offer commitments to test, disclose results, remediate material findings, and cooperate with customer audits. Don't offer a warranty of unbiased outcomes.

**Timing**

If the counterfactual or impact-ratio results look bad in the first two weeks, you'll need room to fix them. Decide now what you'll do in that case. The options are:

- A limited GA, such as existing customers only or a smaller feature scope.
- Launching with the ranker as a sorting aid, with candidate-facing safeguards.
- Delaying GA.

You can still use the booth to talk about your audit program rather than a conclusion you haven't reached.

**Next step**

Have one engineer build the counterfactual test this week, and have your PM ask the beta customers whether they have self-ID data and will share it under appropriate privacy terms. That will tell you quickly whether you're facing a small fix or a larger one.
