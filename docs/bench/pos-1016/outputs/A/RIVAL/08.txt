**Removing human review for permanent bans**

I wouldn't do it on this evidence. A month of shadow mode shows what the system would have done, but it only tells you whether it's right if you have trustworthy ground truth. Check these first:

- **What the system was compared against.** If you measured agreement with moderators, but moderators only saw a subset (the backlog guarantees this), you have selection bias. Agreement with a rushed or inconsistent reviewer isn't accuracy either.
- **Per-segment performance.** An overall figure can hide poor results on non-English communities, slang and in-group jargon, and hobby contexts where "kill," "shoot," or "sell" are normal. Business pages carry extra harm, since a wrong ban costs someone their income.
- **Irreversibility.** Permanent bans are the costliest error type. Suspensions with fast appeal are much more forgiving.
- **Legal exposure.** Depending on where your users are (the EU's DSA, for example), you may owe statements of reasons and an appeals process with human involvement. Ask counsel before enforcement turns on.

A middle path fits your constraint better than all or nothing:

- Automate only categories where audited precision is very high, such as spam, and where you have strong signals like known-bad hashes.
- Let automation issue temporary suspensions with a quick appeal path, but keep a human check before any permanent ban.
- Always route business accounts and low-resource-language communities to a human until you've measured them separately.
- Use the model to prioritize the backlog by severity and confidence, so moderator hours go where they matter most. That may help more than removing review.
- Roll out gradually (a share of traffic or a few communities), with a kill switch, an appeal-overturn alert, and a set appeal turnaround time.

**A wrongful-ban rate for the transparency page**

I can't give you a number, and I'd be wary of anyone who does without an audit. What I can do is define how to get one:

1. **Define the metric.** Wrongful bans divided by what? Candidates are bans the system would have issued, or bans issued per month. State both numerator and denominator on the page.
2. **Don't use appeals alone.** Many wrongly banned users never appeal, so overturn rate understates the true rate. Treat it as a floor.
3. **Run a blind audit.** Draw a random sample of the system's would-be bans, stratified by language, community type, violation category, and business versus personal accounts. Have two independent reviewers label each one, with a tiebreaker. Don't let them see the model's decision.
4. **Report an interval.** Zero errors in 300 samples still only supports "under about 1%" at 95% confidence (roughly 3 divided by n). Small language strata will need more samples than you think, or you report that they're unmeasured.
5. **Scale it.** Even a 0.5% error rate across your monthly ban volume could mean many real people. Work out that number before launch.

**The transparency page**

Don't let the launch date force a figure you haven't measured. A published number that's later revised upward costs far more trust than a candid methodology note. Better options:

- Publish what you measured, how you sampled it, the confidence interval, and which segments are not yet covered.
- State that permanent bans retain human review (if you adopt the middle path), and give the appeal process and its target turnaround.
- Commit to a regular update schedule.

**Three-week plan**

- Week 1: Pull shadow-mode decisions, define the audit sample, and brief counsel.
- Week 2: Run the blind audit with your existing moderators. It's smaller than clearing the backlog. Break results out by segment.
- Week 3: Decide the enforcement tiers from the audit, set up the staged rollout and monitoring, and write the page from the measured results.
