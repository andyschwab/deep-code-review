**Short answer:** probably yes on specific reasons, and I can't give you an accuracy figure. Any number I offered would be invented. I'm also not a lawyer, and this assumes you lend to US consumers, so confirm with counsel before the translation handoff.

**Are specific reasons required?**

Likely, in many situations:

- **ECOA / Regulation B:** If the score leads to a denial, or to worse terms than requested, that is generally "adverse action." The creditor must give a notice with the specific principal reasons (or the right to request them). CFPB guidance has said that using a complex algorithm doesn't excuse this, and that generic checklist reasons are inadequate if they don't reflect the real drivers of the decision.
- **FCRA:** If a consumer report feeds the score, you likely owe an adverse action notice with the credit-score disclosure, key factors, and dispute rights.
- **"Pre-approval" is not automatically exempt.** Whether a pre-approval request counts as an "application" depends on how you handle it. If it's evaluated and can end in a decline, regulators may treat it as one. Counsel should decide this question first.
- **State law and fair lending:** Some states add requirements. Thin-file applicants also raise disparate-impact and UDAAP exposure, so test the score for differing outcomes across protected groups.
- **If you serve anyone outside the US:** The EU, for example, has separate rules on automated decisions and credit scoring.

**The bigger risk is the AI-written paragraph.**

If the paragraph is generated by an LLM, it may not match the factors that actually drove the score. A fluent but wrong explanation could be worse than none, because it can become the "reason" the applicant relies on, and it may not satisfy the notice rule. Questions to settle:

- Are the reasons derived from the model's real attributions, with the LLM only rephrasing them? That is much safer than letting it infer the reasons.
- Does a low score always map to compliant, specific reasons? Would those be the same reasons stated in a formal adverse action notice?
- Who owns the notice, and does the paragraph conflict with it?

**The accuracy figure**

I have no pilot data, so I can't state one, and you shouldn't publish a number without a defensible basis. If you want to publish one, here's a way to get it:

1. Define "accurate." The useful measure is faithfulness: does each stated reason correspond to a factor that truly drove that applicant's score, and is anything stated that didn't?
2. Take a sample from the pilot, say a few hundred cases, stratified to include thin-file applicants and low scores.
3. Have two reviewers who understand the model grade each one against the actual attributions, and resolve disagreements.
4. Report the rate with sample size and a confidence interval, and have compliance approve the wording.

If that can't be done in ten days, don't wait on it. A FAQ is a public claim, and an unsupported accuracy claim can itself create UDAAP/deceptive-practice exposure.

**What to do before translation**

- **Send counsel two questions now:** does this flow trigger adverse action notices, and which regulator wording applies to the FAQ?
- **Replace the accuracy placeholder with a claim you can support today.** For example: "Your score is an estimate based on the information you provide. The explanation describes the main factors that influenced it. It is not a final credit decision, and you can ask us to review your information." Avoid words like "accurate," "guaranteed," or "fair" unless you've verified them.
- **Replace the reasons placeholder** with whatever counsel approves, such as how applicants can request specific reasons and how to dispute or correct their data.
- **Ask your data scientist for the attribution-to-text pipeline description.** That tells you whether explanations are grounded or free-generated.
- **Ask for a "can we launch" gate.** If the faithfulness review isn't done by launch, consider shipping the score with templated, attribution-driven reasons rather than free-form AI text, and add the generated paragraph later.
