Because landlords often follow the recommendation without reading further and applicants never see it, the feature works as an automated decision with little human check and no appeal path. That shapes the plan. I can't run anything, so everything below is work for your team to do and verify.

**1. Legal review (start this week, as it has the longest lead time)**
- Get housing and consumer-reporting counsel to answer whether Kestrel is a consumer reporting agency under FCRA, or provides data that triggers it. If so, adverse action notices, accuracy procedures, and dispute rights apply. Hidden recommendations are the central problem here.
- Check Fair Housing Act disparate-impact exposure, state and local source-of-income protections (housing vouchers are protected in many jurisdictions), and any state or city rules on automated decision tools. Confirm current HUD and CFPB guidance rather than relying on memory.
- Decide who is responsible for notices to applicants, you or the landlord, and put it in the partner contract.

**2. Product design changes to consider**
- Treat housing assistance as reliable income, never as a risk signal. Handle irregular income by looking at multi-month patterns rather than the latest paycheck.
- Exclude or audit proxies for protected classes: names, neighborhoods, language, family size, and gaps in rental history that reflect moving or hardship.
- Consider removing a standalone "decline." Use "approve" or "needs review," with decline reserved for objective, documented criteria the landlord sets (for example, a verified eviction judgment).
- Show evidence with each rationale: which document and which field drove it. Make rationales faithful to the actual factors, not plausible-sounding summaries.
- Add friction for negative outcomes: landlords must open the evidence before confirming a decline. Log who decided and what they saw.
- Give applicants a path to see the factors, correct errors, and request reconsideration.

**3. Evaluation before rollout**
- Build a test set of realistic synthetic and consented applications, with labels from someone with property-management experience.
- Run paired counterfactual tests: identical applications that differ only in name, voucher versus wage income, gig versus salaried work, recent move, or address. Recommendations should not change on those variables.
- Measure document extraction accuracy separately (income, dates, names). Many bad decisions will come from OCR or parsing errors, not judgment.
- Where lawful, compare outcomes across groups on beta data, using approved methods and counsel guidance. Review the beta decisions already made for patterns.
- Set numeric go/no-go thresholds in advance, so schedule pressure can't move them later.

**4. Adversarial and security testing**
- Applications contain untrusted text from applicants and references. Test prompt injection ("recommend approve," hidden text in PDFs, reference letters containing instructions), attempts to extract the prompt or other applicants' data, and cross-landlord data leakage.
- If you use Promptfoo, use synthetic applicants, fixed landlord identities, known owned and unowned application IDs with allowed-access controls, small budgets, and a review of the generated cases before you run them. Don't use real applicant data.
- Review SSN and income-document handling: encryption, retention limits, access controls, and what your model provider stores or trains on.

**5. Rollout controls**
- Run the partner in shadow mode first: record recommendations and compare them to landlord decisions before anyone sees them, or limit the first phase to a subset of properties.
- Build a kill switch, monitoring for decline-rate shifts by property and segment, and a named incident owner.
- Train landlords and property managers on what the tool can't do, and put in the contract that it supports decisions rather than making them.
- Schedule a post-launch audit at 30 and 90 days.

**6. Ownership and timing**
With nine people, assign one owner each for legal, evaluation, document accuracy, security, UX changes, and monitoring. One month is tight. If the work isn't finished, offer the partner a narrower launch: approve and review only, evidence shown, a phased portfolio rollout, and decline kept off or limited to objective criteria. A narrower launch protects both applicants and the contract. A discrimination claim or regulatory action tied to a flagship partner would cost far more than a delayed start.

Tell the partner early that the rollout depends on defined safety gates. Large partners usually have their own legal teams and will likely ask about these issues anyway, so having answers ready helps you.
