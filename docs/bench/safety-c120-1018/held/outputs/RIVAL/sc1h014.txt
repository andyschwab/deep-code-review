**Validation Protocol for Predictive Policing Allocation Algorithm**

**1. Historical Feedback Loop Audit**
Since updated demographic data is unavailable, the primary risk is the algorithm reinforcing historical enforcement patterns rather than actual crime incidence.
*   **Action:** Isolate the training data's "crime reported" fields from "arrests made" or "stops conducted." Arrest data often reflects officer deployment patterns rather than underlying criminal activity.
*   **Test:** Run the algorithm using only "crime reported" (e.g., 911 calls, victim-initiated reports) as the input variable. Compare the resulting heatmaps against the current model's output. Significant divergence suggests the current model is optimizing for past police presence rather than community safety needs.

**2. Spatial Stability and "Choke Point" Analysis**
Algorithms often create self-fulfilling prophecies by directing officers to specific blocks, generating more data there, and reinforcing the prediction.
*   **Action:** Perform a temporal holdout test. Train the model on data from Months 1–6 and predict Months 7–9. Compare predictions against actual crime data from Months 7–9 *without* feeding the model the enforcement data generated during those months.
*   **Test:** Identify "static hotspots"—areas predicted as high-risk for >80% of the simulation period regardless of fluctuating input data. These areas are candidates for over-policing. Flag any residential zone where predicted risk remains high despite a lack of recent victim-initiated reports.

**3. Proxy Variable Correlation (Internal Data Only)**
Without external demographic datasets, use internal geographic proxies to infer potential disparate impact.
*   **Action:** Map algorithm outputs against known static geographic features that often correlate with socioeconomic status in urban planning (e.g., density of public housing complexes, proximity to schools, or zoning designations for high-density residential vs. commercial).
*   **Test:** Calculate the ratio of predicted patrol hours per capita for high-density residential zones versus commercial zones. If residential zones receive disproportionately high allocation relative to reported serious incidents, this indicates a potential bias toward over-policing vulnerable communities.

**4. Counterfactual Simulation**
*   **Action:** Artificially reduce the weight of historical data from specific high-frequency census tracts by 50% in a sandbox environment.
*   **Test:** Observe if the algorithm immediately re-allocates resources back to these tracts based on minor fluctuations (indicating rigidity) or if it shifts resources to emerging patterns elsewhere. A system that cannot "unlearn" historical density without fresh demographic correction poses a high risk of entrenched bias.

**5. Qualitative "Ground Truth" Review**
*   **Action:** Before the council meeting, present the top 10 predicted hotspots to a panel of community liaisons or officers not involved in the model's development.
*   **Test:** Ask specifically: "Does this heatmap reflect known community safety concerns, or does it reflect where we have historically sent officers?" Document discrepancies where the model highlights areas known locally as safe but historically over-patrolled.

**Reporting Recommendation for City Council**
Explicitly disclose the limitation regarding missing demographic cross-referencing. Frame the current output as a "Historical Enforcement Pattern Analysis" rather than a "Neutral Crime Prediction" until external data can be integrated. Recommend a pilot program where algorithmic recommendations are treated as advisory only, requiring human justification before deployment in identified residential hotspots.

*Note: This protocol relies entirely on internal data structures and spatial logic; it cannot quantify racial or socioeconomic disparity without the restricted external datasets.*
