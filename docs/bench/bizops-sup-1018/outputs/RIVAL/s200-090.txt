Your current architecture likely **does not exempt** you from biometric privacy laws like the Illinois Biometric Information Privacy Act (BIPA) or the Texas Capture or Use of Biometric Identifier Act (CUBI), despite raw data remaining on the device.

**Why the "On-Device" Argument Fails Here**
While processing occurs on the device, you explicitly stated that **encryption keys are stored on your servers**. In the eyes of regulators and plaintiff attorneys, possession of the decryption key often equates to possession of the data. If you hold the keys necessary to unlock or verify the biometric template, you are arguably a "collector" and "possessor" of that biometric information. Furthermore, if your server validates the authentication attempt using those keys, you are actively "using" the biometric identifier.

**Specific State Exposures**
*   **Illinois (BIPA):** This is your highest risk. BIPA does not require a data breach to trigger liability; mere failure to obtain written consent before collection/use or failure to publish a retention policy is a violation. Statutory damages are **$1,000 per negligent violation** and **$5,000 per intentional/reckless violation**. With 2 million users, even a small fraction of Illinois residents creates exposure far exceeding your $2 million cash reserves. Recent case law (e.g., *In re Facebook Biometric Information Privacy Litigation*) confirms that technical nuances regarding where data is processed rarely shield companies from liability if they control the ecosystem.
*   **Texas (CUBI):** Similar to Illinois, Texas requires consent before capturing or using a biometric identifier. The definition of "capture" and "use" is broad. Storing keys that enable the function likely falls under "use."
*   **California (CCPA/CPRA & BIPA-like trends):** While California currently lacks a private right of action specifically for biometrics akin to BIPA, the CPRA treats biometric data as "sensitive personal information," triggering strict opt-out and limitation rights. California courts often look to Illinois precedents in class-action certifications.

**Critical Compliance Gaps**
Based on your description, you are likely missing three mandatory requirements under these statutes:
1.  **Written Consent:** Did you obtain explicit, written release from every end-user *before* their first authentication using this feature? Standard Terms of Service checkboxes are often insufficient for BIPA; specific, standalone consent is usually required.
2.  **Public Retention Policy:** Do you have a publicly available schedule detailing exactly when these keys/data will be destroyed? Both IL and TX laws mandate this.
3.  **Data Security Standard:** You must demonstrate you are protecting these keys with a "reasonable standard of care."

4.  **Key Architecture Review:** Have counsel and a security architect review if the key storage can be re-architected (e.g., user-held keys only) to genuinely remove your status as a "possessor."
