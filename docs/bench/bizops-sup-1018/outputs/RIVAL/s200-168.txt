You cannot accurately model a "Lifetime Value" for CodeCamp Kids by forcing a recurring SaaS formula onto a seasonal, one-time event. Doing so creates a misleading metric that investors will immediately spot as flawed. Your business is not a subscription; it is a high-ticket, low-frequency transaction model similar to a private school or a luxury camp.

To present a realistic value proposition to investors, you must shift your metric from **LTV (Lifetime Value)** to **TPV (Total Permissible Value)** or **Fully Loaded Contribution Margin**.

Here is the correct way to model your unit economics:

**1. Reject the Standard LTV Formula**
Standard LTV formulas assume recurring revenue over time. Since your churn is effectively 100% after six weeks, your "Lifetime" is one session. Therefore, your raw LTV is simply **$2,500**.
*   **Current Ratio:** $2,500 (LTV) / $450 (CAC) = **5.5x**.
*   **Reality Check:** A 5.5x ratio is actually excellent for many businesses, *if* your margins support it. If investors think you look unprofitable, the issue is likely your gross margins (instructor pay, facility costs), not the LTV calculation itself. Do not artificially inflate LTV with speculative referral values yet.

**2. The "Fully Loaded" Value Model**
Instead of stretching the timeline, expand the definition of value captured within that single summer. Calculate the **Total Contribution per Student**:
*   **Base Tuition:** $2,500
*   **Add-ons:** Merchandise, extended care, specialized workshops (e.g., +$200)
*   **Referral Credit:** Calculate the *actual* cash value of referrals. If 10% of students refer a friend who pays full price, and you offer a $100 credit, the net value of that referral behavior is real revenue minus the discount.
*   **Formula:** `(Tuition + Add-ons + Net Referral Revenue) - Variable Costs = Contribution Margin`.

Present this as: **"One-Time Customer Contribution."** This tells investors exactly how much cash you make on day one of the camp, which is the only money that matters for a seasonal business.

**3. Addressing the "One-and-Done" Problem**
Investors are worried about your growth ceiling, not just the math. If 90% never return, your revenue relies entirely on finding *new* customers every year, which is expensive and risky. To fix the business model (not just the spreadsheet):
*   **Create a Multi-Year Pathway:** Rebrand from a "Summer Camp" to a "Year-Round Academy." Offer fall/spring weekend courses or online modules. This turns a one-time $2,500 event into a recurring relationship, legitimately increasing LTV.
*   **The "Alumni" Tier:** Offer a discounted "returning student" rate for a second summer or a specialized advanced track. Even a 20% return rate drastically changes your unit economics.
*   **Referral Engine:** Since you rely on new blood, make referrals your primary growth channel. If a parent refers two friends, give them a significant incentive. This lowers your blended CAC below the $450 ad spend.

**Conclusion for Investors**
Do not try to fake a subscription model. Present your metrics as:
1.  **CAC:** $450
2.  **Average Transaction Value (ATV):** $2,500+
3.  **Contribution Margin:** (Revenue - Variable Costs)
4.  **Payback Period:** Immediate (since payment is upfront).

Argue that your model is capital-efficient because you collect cash upfront before delivering the service. The goal is not to extend the "lifetime" mathematically, but to increase the "value" extracted per student and lower acquisition costs through referrals.

*Note: Adding speculative merchandise sales to LTV without historical data will hurt your credibility; only include confirmed add-on revenue.*
*Focus your pitch on the immediacy of cash flow and the potential to convert the 90% one-timers into multi-year academy students.*
