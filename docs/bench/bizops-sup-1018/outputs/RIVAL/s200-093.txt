Your current TAM calculation is significantly inflated and will likely be challenged by institutional investors. The core issue is a mismatch between your **value metric** (what you charge for) and your **market definition** (who you count).

**The Flaw in Your Current Approach**
You are counting **developers** (2.5M) but pricing per **seat** ($40/mo), while acknowledging that 30% of users utilize multiple stacks. If your tool charges per developer regardless of how many languages they use, counting them once is correct, and the "overlap" is a feature, not a bug. However, if your pricing or value delivery scales with the number of repositories, languages, or organizations connected, counting unique individuals underestimates your revenue potential per user but overestimates the total number of *paying entities* if you intend to sell per-project.

More critically, TAM for B2B SaaS is rarely calculated by counting individual developers in North America unless you are a pure prosumer tool with no enterprise sales motion. Investors expect TAM to reflect **budget-holding entities**, not end-users. A company with 500 developers is one customer, not 500 separate market entries, even if you charge per seat. Your current method assumes every developer is an independent buyer, ignoring that procurement happens at the organization level.

**Industry Standard Methodology**
To avoid inflation and align with investor expectations, shift your TAM calculation from "Active Developers" to "Software Development Organizations."

1.  **Define the Unit of Sale:** Determine if you sell to the individual developer, the team lead, or the CTO/VP of Engineering. For a code coverage tool, the buyer is typically the organization ensuring quality standards.
2.  **Count Organizations, Not Individuals:** Instead of 2.5M developers, estimate the number of software companies, digital agencies, and enterprise IT departments in North America. If there are ~200,000 relevant organizations, and the average seat count per organization is 20 developers, your math changes:
    *   *Old TAM:* 2.5M users × $480/yr = $1.2B
    *   *New TAM:* 200k orgs × (20 seats × $480/yr) = $1.92B (Top-down) OR 200k orgs × Average Contract Value (ACV).
3.  **Address the Multi-Stack Overlap:** Do not deduct the 30% overlap. If a developer uses Python and Go, and your tool covers both, that increases the **value** and justifies the price; it does not mean they are two different customers. The "double counting" fear only applies if you were counting "Python Developers" + "Go Developers" as separate market segments. Since you are counting "Active Developers" as a unified pool, the overlap is already neutralized. The real risk is assuming 100% of those developers are reachable buyers.

**Recommended Adjustment for Your Pitch**
Recalculate TAM using a **Bottom-Up** approach, which investors prefer for seed rounds:
*   **Target:** Number of software organizations in North America with >10 engineers.
*   **Penetration:** Estimate realistic capture (e.g., 5% of these orgs).
*   **ACV:** Use your actual data. With 5,000 customers, calculate your true Average Contract Value. If your $40/mo is per seat, and the average customer has 10 seats, your ACV is $4,800, not $480.
*   **Formula:** (Total Target Orgs) × (Estimated Penetration Rate) × (Your Actual ACV).

This removes the ambiguity of individual developer overlaps and focuses on the economic reality of B2B contracting. Your 30% multi-stack statistic should be used in your **value proposition** ("Developers use us across multiple languages, increasing stickiness"), not as a deduction in your market size.

*Note: Ensure your $40/mo price point is clearly defined as "per seat" vs "per user" in the deck to prevent confusion on ACV calculations.*
