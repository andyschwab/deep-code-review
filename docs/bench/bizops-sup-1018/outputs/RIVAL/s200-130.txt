**Compliance Risk Evaluation for SoberPath Dynamic Pricing**

Implementing dynamic pricing for referral fees in the sober living sector carries significant legal exposure under the Fair Housing Act (FHA) and California's Fair Employment and Housing Act (FEHA). While you are charging the homes (businesses) rather than the tenants (individuals), regulators and courts often look at the **economic effect** on protected classes. If your algorithm causes homes in specific neighborhoods to pay higher fees, and those homes subsequently raise rents or reject tenants to offset costs, you may be liable for facilitating discrimination.

**How Algorithms Create Disparate Impact**
Disparate impact occurs when a facially neutral policy (like dynamic pricing) disproportionately harms a protected group, even without discriminatory intent. In your model, risk arises through correlation:
1.  **Geographic Proxying:** If "high-demand areas" correlate with affluent, predominantly white neighborhoods, and your algorithm charges lower fees there while charging higher fees in lower-income, minority-heavy areas, you are effectively penalizing homes that serve protected classes.
2.  **Cost Pass-Through:** Homes facing an $80 referral fee instead of $30 may raise rents to maintain margins. If this price hike disproportionately excludes tenants based on race, disability, or source of income, your pricing algorithm is the catalyst for the violation.
3.  **Inventory Distortion:** If high fees force homes in specific demographics to delist from your platform, you reduce housing options for those communities, creating a systemic barrier.

**Data Points to Strictly Avoid**
To minimize risk, your pricing logic must never ingest, infer, or correlate with the following, either directly or via proxy:
*   **Protected Class Demographics:** Race, color, national origin, religion, sex, familial status, or disability status of the neighborhood or tenant base.
*   **Geographic Proxies for Race/Income:** Zip codes, census tracts, or neighborhood names that historically correlate with segregation or redlining. Using "demand" metrics derived from these areas is dangerous.
*   **Source of Income:** Data indicating whether tenants use Section 8 vouchers or other government assistance.
*   **Disability Status:** Any data regarding the type of recovery or disability of the typical tenant in a specific home.
*   **Historical Occupancy Data:** If past tenant data reflects biased admission practices, using it to predict "value" or "demand" perpetuates that bias.

**Mandatory Legal Audit Requirements**
You must engage a fair housing legal expert to audit the following **before** writing a single line of code or deploying the algorithm:
1.  **The Variable Selection Audit:** A formal review of every input variable proposed for the algorithm to ensure no direct or proxy correlation with protected classes. This includes testing historical data to see if "demand" or "location" variables produce skewed outcomes against protected groups.
2.  **The Disparate Impact Simulation:** A statistical stress test where the proposed pricing model is run against historical data to model whether the resulting fee structure would disproportionately burden homes serving protected classes.
3.  **The Business Necessity Defense Documentation:** Legal counsel must help document the specific, non-discriminatory business necessity for each pricing factor (e.g., proving that higher fees are strictly tied to operational costs, not demographic value) to prepare a defense if challenged.

Do not proceed with dynamic pricing until these three components are signed off by counsel specializing in housing law.

*Note: California laws (FEHA) are often stricter than federal FHA; state-specific counsel is essential.*
