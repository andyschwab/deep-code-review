### Pricing Model Analysis for NexusUI

**Option A: Per-Seat Pricing ($49/developer)**
*Pros:* Low friction for initial adoption; teams can onboard without procurement hurdles for small pilots. It scales naturally with team growth.
*Cons:* Revenue is capped by headcount, not value. Large enterprises often have thousands of developers; capping seats or negotiating enterprise discounts becomes a bottleneck. It fails to capture the value of the framework enabling hundreds of applications.
*Financial Impact:* At your current scale, this yields ~$9,800/mo for a 200-dev/50-app customer. This leaves significant value on the table given your $35k burn rate.

**Option B: Per-Instance Pricing ($500/application)**
*Pros:* Directly aligns price with business value (number of shipped products). It scales infinitely with customer success; as they build more micro-frontends, your revenue grows without needing more seats. It discourages "shelfware" and encourages consolidation onto your platform.
*Cons:* High friction for new internal tools. Teams may hesitate to spin up a new experimental app if it incurs a hard $500/mo cost immediately. This can slow down the "land and expand" motion crucial for enterprise dev tools.
*Financial Impact:* Yields $25,000/mo for the same customer. This single deal nearly covers your entire burn rate, drastically improving runway.

**Recommendation:**
For a micro-frontend framework targeting large enterprises, **Per-Instance (or a Hybrid) is superior for Lifetime Value (LTV).** Enterprise value comes from the complexity and number of applications managed, not just the number of coders. However, a pure $500/app model risks stifling innovation.

**Proposed Hybrid Strategy:**
Adopt a **Base Platform Fee + Usage Tier**. Charge a lower per-seat fee (e.g., $29) to cover collaboration/tooling costs, plus a per-instance fee that decreases at volume (e.g., $500 for the first 10 apps, $250 for the next 40). This lowers the barrier for new experiments while capturing massive value from mature deployments.

**Critical Data to Gather Before Deciding:**

1.  **Expansion Velocity:** Ask your 12 pilot customers: "How many new applications do you plan to deploy in the next 12 months?" If the answer is high, the instance model wins. If they are consolidating, the seat model might be safer.
2.  **Budget Owner Identification:** Determine who signs the check. Seat pricing is often approved by Engineering Managers (OpEx). Instance pricing often requires VP/Director approval (Project/CapEx). If your sales cycle is currently stuck waiting for higher approval, the seat model may be necessary for initial land, with a contract clause to renegotiate upon hitting instance thresholds.
3.  **Churn Sensitivity Simulation:** Model a scenario where a customer cuts 20% of their projects. Under the seat model, you lose 20% revenue. Under the instance model, you lose 20% revenue. However, model the *growth* scenario: if they double their apps but keep the same devs, seat revenue stays flat while instance revenue doubles. Which curve matches your product roadmap?
4.  **Pilot Willingness-to-Pay:** Conduct a Van Westendorp survey specifically with your two Fortune 500 pilots. Ask: "At what price per application would NexusUI be too expensive to use for side projects?" This identifies your friction point for the instance model.

**Immediate Action:** Do not switch models for existing pilots yet. Propose the Hybrid model to the two Fortune 500 customers as an "Enterprise Scale" option. If they accept, you have validation to shift the public pricing page.

*Note: Ensure your contract terms define an "instance" clearly to avoid billing disputes over dev/staging vs. production environments.*
