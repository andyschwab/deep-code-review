Yes, it is entirely possible to use FIFO effectively for your upcycled furniture business, and an auditor will accept it provided your record-keeping tracks the specific identity of each unique item. The misconception that FIFO fails with non-uniform stock stems from confusing "fungible goods" (like bushels of wheat) with "specific identification." Since you deal in one-off pieces where every chair or table has a distinct acquisition cost and date, you are actually in a stronger position to use **Specific Identification**, which is often preferred over FIFO for high-value, unique items, though FIFO remains perfectly valid if applied correctly.

To structure your records for audit acceptance, you must move away from averaging costs or grouping items by category. Instead, implement a **Unique Item Ledger**. For every piece acquired (e.g., the "Mid-Century Walnut Credenza" bought on March 12 for $450), assign a permanent ID tag. Your inventory log must link this ID to three critical data points: the exact acquisition date, the specific purchase price (including restoration costs if capitalized), and the sale date.

When you sell an item, you do not estimate cost based on a generic "first-in" pool; you simply match the sold item's ID to its specific acquisition record. If you strictly sell the oldest acquired items first, your flow matches FIFO naturally. However, because your items are distinct, auditors typically prefer you to use the **Specific Identification Method**. This method allows you to report the exact cost of the specific unit sold, eliminating the need to assume a flow of goods. It is more accurate than FIFO for your model and fully GAAP-compliant.

To satisfy your investor and prepare for an audit:
1.  **Tag Everything Immediately:** Ensure every item received from an estate sale gets a unique SKU or barcode linked to its invoice/receipt before it hits the showroom floor.
2.  **Digitize the Chain of Custody:** Use inventory software that allows you to scan an item out and automatically pulls its specific historical cost. Spreadsheets are risky for 450+ unique items; dedicated inventory management tools are essential for audit trails.
3.  **Separate Restoration Costs:** Clearly define if labor/materials for upcycling are expensed immediately or added to the inventory cost basis. Consistency here is vital for auditors.
4.  **Reconcile Physical to Digital:** Conduct monthly spot checks to ensure the physical item on the floor matches the "unsold" status in your ledger.

Your sporadic purchase intervals and varying costs are not obstacles; they are the exact reasons why tracking individual units is required. As long as you can demonstrate a clear, unbroken link between the specific dollar amount you paid for a specific table and the revenue generated when that exact table sold, your Cost of Goods Sold (COGS) calculations will be robust. The auditor's primary concern is not the method (FIFO vs. Specific ID) but the **verifiability** of the data. If your system proves that the $450 cost assigned to a sold item corresponds to the actual receipt from the estate sale, your valuation method will hold up.

Stop trying to force your unique inventory into a bulk-commodity mindset. Embrace the uniqueness of your stock as the justification for item-level tracking, which is the gold standard for audit reliability in your sector.

*Note: Consult a CPA to formally elect Specific Identification for tax purposes, as it requires strict documentation standards. Ensure your restoration labor accounting policy is documented and applied consistently.*
