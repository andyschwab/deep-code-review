**Regulatory Boundary: General Wellness vs. Medical Claims**

The line between a permissible general wellness claim and a regulated medical device claim hinges on **intended use**. The FDA determines intended use primarily through your labeling, advertising, and website copy. If your marketing suggests the product is designed to diagnose, cure, mitigate, treat, or prevent a disease or condition, it becomes a medical device requiring clearance (typically 510(k)) before sale.

**The Risk with "Allergy" Claims**
Mentioning "allergies" is high-risk. Allergies are considered a medical condition. Claims stating the product "helps allergy sufferers," "treats allergic reactions," or "prevents allergy symptoms" strongly imply a therapeutic intent. If the FDA determines your product is a medical device based on these claims, and you do not have clearance:
1.  **Enforcement Action:** The FDA can issue a Warning Letter demanding you cease sales and remove the claims immediately.
2.  **Seizure/Injunction:** In severe cases, they can seize products or seek an injunction to stop operations.
3.  **Halting Sales:** Yes, if the FDA classifies it as an unapproved medical device, you would likely be forced to halt sales of the specific marketed version until you obtain clearance or remove the offending claims.

**Safe Harbor: General Wellness Wording**
You can target the same customer segment without triggering medical device regulation by focusing on **environmental conditions** rather than **human health outcomes**. The goal is to describe what the machine does to the *air*, not what it does to the *person*.

**Recommended Wording Strategy:**
*   **Avoid:** "Helps allergy sufferers," "reduces allergy symptoms," "protects against pet dander allergies," "medical-grade filtration for asthma."
*   **Use Instead:** "Removes 99% of airborne pollen," "captures pet dander particles," "reduces common airborne irritants," "improves in-car air quality by filtering particulate matter."

**The Distinction:**
*   *Medical Claim:* "Removes pollen to stop sneezing and itchy eyes." (Focus: Treating a symptom/condition).
*   *Wellness/General Claim:* "Removes 99% of pollen particles from the air." (Focus: Mechanical performance/environmental change).

**Strategic Recommendation**
Do not mention "allergies" or "sufferers" explicitly. Instead, market the **removal of the allergen** (pollen/dander) as a feature of air purity. You can still target allergy sufferers through your ad imagery (e.g., showing someone driving comfortably) and audience targeting settings on ad platforms, but keep the *textual claims* strictly limited to particle removal efficiency.

If you wish to make explicit health claims, you must undergo the FDA 510(k) clearance process, which involves clinical data, significant costs ($10k–$100k+), and time (3–6 months minimum). Given your current stage (1,000 units sold, early revenue), the safest path is to refine your copy to focus on "clean air" and "particle reduction" rather than "health relief."

**Note:** The FDA monitors digital marketing and website content, not just physical packaging. Ensure your social media ads and landing pages align with this strategy immediately.
