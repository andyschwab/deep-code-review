### Financial Sustainability Analysis: TeachPrep Money-Back Guarantee

**Verdict:** The guarantee is **financially sustainable** and highly advisable, provided you manage cash flow timing. It will not wipe out your margins; instead, it will likely increase net profit by boosting conversion rates, assuming your current volume holds.

#### 1. Unit Economics Under Guarantee
Currently, you recognize revenue from every student. Under the new policy, you only retain revenue from those who pass (assuming 100% of failures claim refunds).

*   **Current Model (No Guarantee):**
    *   Revenue per student: $499
    *   Variable Cost per student: $120
    *   **Contribution Margin:** $379 per student
    *   **Quarterly Profit (500 students):** $189,500

*   **New Model (With Guarantee):**
    *   **Passing Students (78%):** You keep the $499 revenue.
        *   Margin: $499 - $120 = $379
    *   **Failing Students (22%):** You refund the $499 but have already incurred the $120 cost.
        *   Net Result: -$120 (Loss of variable cost only)
    *   **Weighted Average Margin per Enrolled Student:**
        *   (0.78 × $379) + (0.22 × -$120)
        *   $295.62 - $26.40 = **$269.22**

*   **Projected Quarterly Profit (500 students):**
    *   500 × $269.22 = **$134,610**

**Impact:** Your quarterly profit drops from $189,500 to $134,610, a reduction of **$54,890**. While significant, you remain highly profitable. You are effectively trading ~29% of your profit potential for a massive marketing advantage (the guarantee).

#### 2. Cash Reserve Stress Test
Your risk is not profitability, but liquidity. You must have cash on hand to issue refunds before new sales replenish the account.

*   **Worst-Case Scenario (All 500 students enroll, then 22% fail immediately):**
    *   Refund obligations: 110 students × $499 = **$54,890**
    *   Your Cash Reserve: **$40,000**
    *   **Shortfall:** $14,890

**Critical Finding:** Your current $40,000 reserve is **insufficient** to cover a worst-case quarter where all failures claim refunds simultaneously. If refunds are processed instantly upon exam results, you could face a cash flow crunch. However, since students take courses over time and exam dates are staggered, the *actual* simultaneous refund load will likely be lower than the theoretical maximum.

#### 3. Unknown Factors & Behavioral Risks

1.  **The "Moral Hazard" Effect:** Guarantees can inadvertently lower pass rates. Students may study less rigorously knowing their money is safe, potentially pushing your failure rate from 22% to 30%+. This would deepen losses.
2.  **Retake Friction:** You mentioned some might retake the course instead of refunding. If you offer a free retake rather than a refund, your cost structure changes. A free retake incurs *additional* variable costs (platform fees, instructor grading) without new revenue. You must define if the guarantee is "Money Back" or "Free Retake." A free retake is often cheaper for you than a full refund if the marginal cost of the second attempt is low.
3.  **Conversion Lift:** This analysis assumes volume stays at 500. In reality, a money-back guarantee often increases conversion rates by 20–40%. If enrollment jumps to 650 students due to reduced risk perception, your total profit could actually **exceed** the current model despite the lower margin per unit.

**Recommendation:** Proceed with the guarantee but adjust the reserve. Aim to hold at least $60,000 in liquid cash to safely cover a bad month. Consider structuring the offer as a "Free Retake" first, with a refund as a secondary option, to protect cash flow and encourage completion.

*Note: Ensure your terms clearly define "first attempt" and require proof of exam completion to prevent abuse.*
*Monitor the failure rate closely for the first two cohorts; a spike above 25% requires immediate curriculum review.*
