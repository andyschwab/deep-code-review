**Accounting Treatment Determination**

Based on the facts provided—specifically that the implementation services are **not distinct** from the software license—you **cannot** recognize the $75,000 implementation fees immediately upon signing. Under ASC 606 (the revenue recognition standard governing GAAP), these fees must be **amortized over the three-year contract term** alongside the license fee.

Your auditors are correct. The core issue lies in the definition of a "distinct" performance obligation. For the implementation to be treated as a separate obligation (allowing upfront recognition), it must meet two criteria:
1.  **Capable of being distinct:** The customer can benefit from the service on its own or with other readily available resources.
2.  **Distinct within the context of the contract:** The promise to transfer the service is separately identifiable from the promise to transfer the license.

Since you stated the services are *not distinct*, they fail the second criterion. This usually occurs when the implementation involves significant customization, configuration, or integration that is essential to make the software functional for the specific Fortune 500 client. In this scenario, the license and the implementation are viewed as a **single combined performance obligation**.

**The Calculation Impact**
Because they are a single obligation, the total contract value ($250,000 license + $75,000 implementation = $325,000) must be recognized ratably over the period the customer receives benefit (the three-year term).

*   **Total Contract Value:** $325,000
*   **Recognition Period:** 36 months
*   **Monthly Revenue Recognition:** ~$9,027
*   **Quarterly Revenue Recognition:** ~$27,083

If you were to recognize the $75,000 upfront, you would be overstating your revenue by approximately $62,500 in the first quarter alone ($75,000 cash vs. ~$12,500 earned).

**Strategic Implications for Series B**
While recognizing the fees upfront would artificially inflate your top-line growth to assist with your Series B round, doing so against auditor advice constitutes a material misstatement of financials. This creates severe risks:
1.  **Due Diligence Failure:** Sophisticated Series B investors will uncover this during financial due diligence. Discovering aggressive or non-compliant revenue recognition often kills deals instantly due to trust erosion and liability concerns.
2.  **Restatement Risk:** If you close the round and later force a restatement of financials to correct the revenue, it could trigger legal liabilities and valuation down-rounds.
3.  **Cash vs. Revenue:** You correctly identified the cash influx ($675,000). While this cannot be booked as *revenue*, it is recorded as **Deferred Revenue** (a liability) on the balance sheet. Investors analyze cash flow and deferred revenue growth closely; strong deferred revenue growth signals future predictable income without violating GAAP.

**Conclusion**
You cannot argue that the setup is a separate performance obligation if the services are inherently integrated with the license delivery. The fees must be amortized. Presenting the strong cash position and growing deferred revenue balance is the compliant and sustainable narrative for your Series B raise.
