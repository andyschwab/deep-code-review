**Pre-Order Treatment in Valuation**
Do not count pre-orders as full recognized revenue for valuation purposes. In hardware startups, pre-orders are treated as **committed demand** or **backlog**, not GAAP revenue, because you have not yet delivered the product or incurred the cost of goods sold (COGS). Investors typically apply a significant discount to this backlog when calculating valuation, often valuing it at 20–40% of the gross face value to account for cancellation risk, fulfillment costs, and execution risk.

With $995,000 in pre-orders ($199 × 5,000) and $110/unit COGS, your gross potential margin is $445,000. However, since you have only $25,000 cash and need $400,000 to manufacture, investors view this as a capital-intensive execution challenge, not a solved revenue stream. A standard approach is to value the company based on a multiple of the *net* potential profit from the backlog plus a premium for the team and IP, rather than a multiple of the top-line pre-order number. Expect investors to argue for a lower cap until units are shipped and churn/cancellation rates are proven.

**Securities Law Risks**
Presenting pre-orders as "traction" is standard and permissible, provided you clearly label them as "unfulfilled pre-orders" or "backlog" rather than "revenue." The legal risk arises if you imply these are guaranteed sales without disclosing cancellation policies or fulfillment risks. Misrepresenting backlog as recognized revenue can constitute fraud or misleading statements under securities laws. Always include a footnote in your pitch deck stating that pre-orders are subject to cancellation and that delivery timelines are estimates.

**Legal Counsel and SAFE Agreements**
You absolutely need a startup lawyer. Standard SAFE templates (like those from Y Combinator) do not contain specific language addressing large pre-order books, manufacturing liabilities, or the specific risks of hardware fulfillment. A lawyer is required to:
1.  Draft disclosures for the SAFE regarding the use of funds specifically for manufacturing these units.
2.  Ensure your pitch deck disclosures meet securities regulations to avoid future litigation from investors if fulfillment fails.
3.  Advise on whether the pre-order funds (if already collected) are held in escrow or commingled, which affects liability.

Do not attempt to customize a SAFE or navigate securities disclosures for a hardware raise with significant pre-order liability without legal counsel. The cost of a lawyer is negligible compared to the risk of an invalid raise or future lawsuit.

*Note: Pre-orders validate demand but do not replace the need for working capital to fulfill them; investors will focus heavily on your path to positive cash flow post-fulfillment.*
