**IP Ownership Audit Checklist for StreamSchema Acquisition Due Diligence**

To prepare your eight-engineer team and $50k reserves for a $12M acquisition, focus your legal audit on these specific high-risk areas rather than renegotiating every contract immediately. Standard early-stage agreements often fail rigorous due diligence in three specific areas:

**1. The "Prior Inventions" Disclosure Gap**
California law (and standard agreements) requires employees to list pre-existing IP they own that might overlap with company work.
*   **The Risk:** If your engineers did not formally attach a "Prior Inventions" list to their contracts, or if the list is blank/boilerplate, an acquirer may fear an engineer could later claim core schema logic was their pre-existing idea.
*   **Action:** Ask your attorney: "Do our signed contracts include executed exhibits listing prior inventions for all eight engineers? If missing, can we execute a 'Confirmation of No Prior Inventions' memo now to cure this defect without full renegotiation?"

**2. The Contractor "Work Made for Hire" Void**
You specifically mentioned short-term contractors. This is your highest liability.
*   **The Risk:** In the US, code written by contractors is *not* automatically owned by the company unless there is a written agreement explicitly stating it is a "work made for hire" AND assigning all rights. If you only have email chains or informal SOWs, you likely do not own that code. Acquirers will not accept "implied license" arguments.
*   **Action:** Ask your attorney: "Do we have signed IP assignment agreements specifically covering every line of code written by external contractors? If any are missing, what is the minimum cost to execute a retroactive assignment deed just for those specific individuals?"

**3. Moral Rights and Waivers**
While US copyright law has limited "moral rights" compared to Europe, software due diligence often flags the lack of explicit waivers.
*   **The Risk:** Some early contracts omit the specific waiver of moral rights (the right to be named as author or object to modifications). While rarely litigated in US SaaS, acquirers want clean title to modify/sell the code without theoretical obstruction.
*   **Action:** Ask: "Do our current assignments explicitly waive moral rights to the fullest extent permitted by law? If not, is a simple supplemental waiver sufficient for the eight employees?"

**Specific Questions for Your Corporate Attorney**
Present these exact questions to maximize your limited budget:

1.  "Based on a review of our eight employment files, is there any ambiguity regarding 'inventions made during employment' versus 'prior inventions' that would trigger a standard acquirer's representation warranty exception?"
2.  "For the contractors hired in years 1 and 2, does our documentation meet the strict 'work made for hire' statutory requirements under 17 U.S.C. § 101, or do we need retroactive assignments?"
3.  "Given our $50k cash constraint, can we draft a single 'IP Confirmation and Ratification Agreement' for all current staff to sign, acknowledging that all past work belongs to StreamSchema, rather than rewriting individual employment contracts?"
4.  "Are there any 'open source' contamination risks in our schema registry codebase that our current IP clauses fail to address regarding employee contributions from personal projects?"

**Strategy Note:** Do not renegotiate full employment contracts unless a specific gap is found; instead, seek targeted "ratification" or "confirmation" documents which are cheaper and less alarming to staff.

**Cost Note:** A focused audit and drafting of supplemental ratification agreements should fit within your $50k reserve, whereas full contract re-papering likely will not.
