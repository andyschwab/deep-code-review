Implementing a formalized sliding-scale pricing model based on income does not inherently jeopardize your current 501(c)(3) tax-exempt status; in fact, it often strengthens your charitable purpose argument by demonstrating a commitment to serving low-income communities. The primary compliance risk is not the discount itself, but the potential for "private benefit" if the criteria are applied inconsistently, arbitrarily, or if the program disproportionately benefits specific individuals rather than a charitable class.

To protect the organization during this transitional phase, you must treat the sliding scale as a formal charitable program, not a marketing tactic. The IRS requires that any benefit provided to individuals be qualified by a charitable class (e.g., those unable to afford care) and determined by an objective, written policy. If you apply discounts based on vague discretion or favor specific families without documented financial need, you risk an excess benefit transaction finding. However, if you establish clear income brackets (e.g., Federal Poverty Level percentages) and apply them uniformly to all applicants, you are effectively documenting your charitable distribution, which supports your exemption rather than threatening it.

Regarding your future conversion to a for-profit entity, introducing this model now creates no legal barrier to conversion, provided the non-profit does not sell assets (like your patient lists or brand) to the new for-profit entity at below-market rates. The risk lies in the conversion transaction itself, not the prior pricing strategy. Investors generally view a validated sliding-scale model as a de-risking asset because it proves unit economics across different demographic segments.

**Required Documentation to Mitigate Liability:**

1.  **Written Sliding-Scale Policy:** A board-approved document explicitly defining the charitable purpose of the program, the income eligibility criteria (tied to objective standards like FPL), and the specific discount tiers.
2.  **Uniform Application Records:** A log showing that every family requesting assistance was evaluated against the same criteria, regardless of their relationship to staff or board members.
3.  **Financial Need Verification:** Standardized forms collecting proof of income (tax returns, pay stubs) for every family receiving a discount, stored securely to prove the "charitable class" requirement was met.
4.  **Board Minutes:** Specific records of the board's discussion and approval of the policy, highlighting the intent to expand access to care for underserved populations in California.
5.  **Annual Impact Report:** A summary quantifying the total value of charity care provided (the difference between the $120 standard rate and the collected amount), which serves as evidence of your public benefit.

Offering scaled prices to 40% of your base does not create immediate liability if the above framework is followed. The 30% of clients currently paying nothing are already receiving charity care; formalizing the middle tier simply brings structure to your existing charitable activity. The key is consistency: if a family at 150% of the poverty line gets a 20% discount, every family at that level must receive the same treatment.

*Note: While this strategy aligns with general IRS principles for charitable organizations, you must have your specific policy draft reviewed by legal counsel specializing in California non-profit law before implementation.*
*Ensure your future conversion plan includes an independent valuation of assets to prevent any appearance of impermissible private inurement when transferring to the for-profit structure.*
