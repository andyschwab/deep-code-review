**Runway Calculation for FireBarrel Series A Pitch**

**Standard Cash Runway (Recommended for Pitch Deck)**
*   **Cash Balance:** $1,200,000
*   **Monthly Operating Burn:** $30,000
*   **Calculation:** $1,200,000 / $30,000
*   **Result:** **40 Months**

**"Economic" Runway (Including Opportunity Cost)**
*   **Inventory Value:** $500,000
*   **Annual Opportunity Cost (8%):** $40,000
*   **Monthly Implicit Cost:** ~$3,333
*   **Adjusted Monthly Burn:** $33,333 ($30k cash + $3.3k implicit)
*   **Calculation:** $1,200,000 / $33,333
*   **Result:** **36 Months**

**Strategic Recommendation**
Do **not** include the opportunity cost of aging inventory in your primary runway slide. Present the **40-month** figure as your official runway.

Including the $3,333 monthly implicit cost to claim a 36-month runway is counterproductive for a Series A pitch for three reasons:

1.  **Inventory is an Asset, Not a Burn:** In standard GAAP accounting and venture finance, cash spent on inventory converts to an asset on your balance sheet; it does not disappear as an expense until sold. Your cash burn is strictly the cash leaving the bank for operations ($30k). The sauce is not "idle capital"; it is active production. Without that $500k inventory, you would have no product to sell, rendering the business worthless.
2.  **Signaling Efficiency:** Deliberately shortening your runway by factoring in opportunity costs signals to investors that you view your core production process as a liability rather than a value-driver. It suggests you believe the capital *should* be sitting in a bond rather than building your product. Investors back companies that deploy capital into high-return activities (like aging premium sauce), not companies that hoard cash.
3.  **Confusion Over Sophistication:** While calculating opportunity cost shows financial literacy, mixing non-cash implicit costs with cash burn confuses the metric. Runway answers one specific question: "How many months until we run out of cash?" The answer is 40. Introducing theoretical costs muddies the water and may make you appear unsure of standard financial definitions.

**How to Demonstrate Sophistication Instead**
To show you understand working capital constraints without hurting your runway narrative, address the aging process in your **Unit Economics** or **Working Capital** slide, not the Runway slide.

Explicitly state: *"Our 18-month aging cycle creates a working capital lag, requiring $500k in continuous inventory investment. However, this allows us to command a premium price point ($25/bottle) with gross margins of X%, significantly higher than competitors with no aging process."*

This frames the $500k as a strategic moat that drives higher margins, rather than an inefficiency dragging down your cash position. It tells investors you understand the cost of capital but have priced your product to overcome it handsomely.

*Note: Ensure your inventory valuation is accurate and auditable, as investors will diligence the $500k figure heavily given its size relative to your burn.*
*If asked directly about "economic burn" in Q&A, you can verbally acknowledge the 8% cost but reiterate that your pricing strategy yields a return on inventory far exceeding that hurdle rate.*
