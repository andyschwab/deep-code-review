To stabilize your unit economics for investor reporting while managing real-world volatility, you should adopt a **Standardized Cost Basis** model for your P&L, treating currency variance as a separate financial line item rather than burying it in gross margin.

**1. Restructure Your Unit Economics Calculation**
Stop calculating gross margin using the actual spot rate at the time of payment. Instead, establish a **Standard Exchange Rate (SER)** for each fiscal quarter or academic year based on a conservative 12-month average or a forward rate available at the start of the booking cycle.
*   **Revenue:** £8,500 (fixed).
*   **COGS (Standardized):** Calculate foreign costs using the SER.
*   **Reported Gross Margin:** This will now appear stable to your board, reflecting operational performance rather than forex noise.
*   **Variance Line Item:** Create a specific line below Gross Margin called "Foreign Exchange Variance." This captures the difference between the SER and the actual realized cost.

This approach isolates the risk. Investors see a consistent ~22% operational margin, with the FX impact clearly visible and quantifiable below the line. It prevents the narrative from shifting from "strong agency model" to "volatile trading vehicle."

**2. Immediate Financial Defense: Hedging**
You must hedge. A projected net loss on booked students due to a 5% currency move indicates unhedged speculation, not a business model flaw.
*   **Action:** Implement forward contracts for known future outflows (tuition and housing) as soon as students commit. Since your revenue is fixed in GBP and costs are known in foreign currency 3–6 months in advance, you can lock in rates immediately upon booking.
*   **Result:** This effectively fixes your COGS in GBP terms, eliminating the variance line item for those specific cohorts. The cost of the hedge (the spread) becomes a predictable part of your standard COGS.

**3. Pricing Strategy: The FX Buffer**
Do not raise your base fee of £8,500 explicitly for "currency risk," as this creates friction in sales. Instead, build a **dynamic contingency buffer** into your internal cost modeling.
*   When setting the SER for the next cycle, inflate the estimated foreign costs by 3–5% above the current forward rate.
*   If currency remains stable, this buffer flows directly to your bottom line as extra profit.
*   If currency worsens within that range, the buffer absorbs the hit without triggering a loss.
*   Only if volatility exceeds this buffer does it hit your "Variance Line Item."

**Presentation to the Board**
Present two views:
1.  **Operational View:** Uses the Standard Exchange Rate and hedged costs. This shows the true health of the agency's core business (margins ~20%+).
2.  **Financial Reality View:** Includes the "FX Variance" line. Explain that this is a manageable financial risk being mitigated via forward contracts, not an operational inefficiency.

By separating operational execution from financial market movements, you demonstrate sophisticated financial management. You are not hiding the risk; you are ring-fencing it, which is exactly what investors want to see in an international business.
