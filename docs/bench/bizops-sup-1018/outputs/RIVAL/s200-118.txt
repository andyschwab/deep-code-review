### Analysis of UBIT Risk for AbilityHire

**1. The Core Test: Substantial Relationship**
The primary determinant of whether your $200/job listing fee triggers UBIT is not the *amount* of revenue, but the **nature of the activity**. The IRS applies a "substantial relationship" test: Does the commercial activity contribute importantly to the accomplishment of your exempt purpose (advocacy and employment for people with disabilities)?

*   **Argument for Exemption (Related Income):** If your job board is curated, vetted, and actively managed to ensure roles are accessible and suitable for candidates with disabilities, the activity directly furthers your mission. In this view, the fee is a recovery of costs for a mission-critical service, not a commercial enterprise. The fact that you have 1,500 active postings from corporate partners suggests an existing ecosystem aligned with your goals.
*   **Argument for UBIT (Unrelated Income):** If the board operates like a generic job site (e.g., anyone can post any job without vetting for disability inclusion), the IRS may view this as a commercial service competing with for-profit job boards (Indeed, LinkedIn). If the activity is "not substantially related" to the exempt purpose, the income is unrelated, regardless of how you spend the profit.

**2. Revenue Thresholds and "Regularly Carried On"**
There is **no specific percentage of revenue** that automatically triggers a penalty or loss of exempt status. However, two thresholds matter:
*   **Filing Threshold:** If your gross unrelated business income exceeds **$1,000** annually, you must file **Form 990-T** and potentially pay taxes (currently a flat 21% corporate rate) on the net profit. Your projected $100,000 revenue (minus direct expenses) would almost certainly exceed this, requiring a filing if deemed "unrelated."
*   **Substantiality Test for Exempt Status:** While no fixed percentage exists, IRS scrutiny increases if unrelated activities become a "substantial" part of your operations. Historically, courts have looked at both the percentage of total revenue (e.g., >20-25% often raises flags) and the time/effort staff devote to it. With $100k new revenue against $300k grants, this represents ~25% of total inflow, which is a significant enough portion to warrant careful structuring to protect your 501(c)(3) status.

**3. The "Fragmentation Rule"**
Be aware that the IRS applies the fragmentation rule: even if your overall organization is charitable, a specific activity (the job board) can be sliced out and taxed separately if it operates commercially.

**4. Strategic Recommendation: The Subsidiary Option**
Given the projected $100,000 revenue and the ambiguity of whether the IRS would view a generic fee-for-service model as "substantially related," establishing a **taxable subsidiary (C-Corp or LLC)** is a prudent risk-mitigation strategy.
*   **How it works:** The subsidiary runs the job board and pays UBIT/corporate tax on its profits. It then donates its net earnings to the non-profit parent as a qualified charitable contribution.
*   **Benefit:** This creates a firewall. If the IRS determines the job board is commercial, only the subsidiary is at risk, protecting the foundation's core exempt status and grant eligibility.

**Conclusion**
The $100,000 itself is not illegal, but if the activity is deemed "unrelated," you will owe taxes on the net profit. The risk to your *exempt status* arises if this commercial activity becomes a primary focus rather than a secondary support to your mission. Because the line between "mission-aligned service fee" and "commercial competition" is fact-specific, the safest path to financial sustainability without endangering your foundation is to house this revenue stream in a taxable subsidiary.
