For ModularLiving, the $350 last-mile logistics cost must be classified as **Cost of Goods Sold (COGS)**, not Sales and Marketing. This classification provides the truer picture of your scalability and profitability for the following reasons:

**1. Fulfillment of the Core Promise**
Your product is not just the sofa; it is "sofa + white-glove delivery." Since the $2,500 price point explicitly includes delivery, you cannot generate revenue without incurring this cost. In accounting standards (such as ASC 606 in the US), costs directly attributable to fulfilling a customer contract are part of the cost of revenue. Treating this as a marketing expense would artificially inflate your Gross Margin, hiding the fact that your fundamental unit economics are heavily burdened by fulfillment.

**2. Accurate Unit Economics for Scalability**
Investors and internal teams need to see the real contribution margin per unit to assess scalability.
*   **Current Reality:** Revenue ($2,500) - COGS (Materials + Labor + **$350 Delivery**) = True Gross Profit.
*   **If Misclassified:** If you move the $350 to Sales & Marketing, your Gross Margin looks $350 higher per unit. This creates a false signal that the business is more profitable at scale than it actually is. When you hire your next team or expand volume, that $350 variable cost remains tied to every single unit sold. Hiding it in overhead obscures the operational leverage (or lack thereof) in your model.

**3. The "Premium Service" Misconception**
You mentioned concern about distorting the perceived value. Classifying delivery as COGS does not diminish its value; it accurately reflects its cost. Your premium positioning allows you to charge $2,500 (likely higher than competitors charging $150 for freight), but the *cost* to deliver that premium experience is still a direct cost of the product sold. The "value" is captured in the higher price point you command, not in moving the cost to a different line item.

**Recommended Metric Structure**
For your investor updates and hiring plan, structure your unit economics as follows:

*   **Revenue per Unit:** $2,500
*   **Variable COGS:**
    *   Manufacturing/Materials: $X
    *   Inbound Freight: $Y
    *   **Last-Mile Delivery: $350**
*   **Gross Profit per Unit:** $2,500 - (Sum of above)
*   **Gross Margin %:** (Gross Profit / $2,500)

Use this **Gross Profit** figure to determine how much cash you have left to cover fixed costs (R&D, Marketing, Salaries) and contribute to runway. With only 9 months of runway, understanding that every sale consumes $350 in immediate fulfillment cash is critical for cash flow forecasting. If this cost were treated as marketing, you might erroneously believe you have more flexibility to spend on customer acquisition than you actually do.

**Note:** Separating the cost does not mean separating the value proposition; it ensures your margin reflects the true cost of your bundled offer. Consult a CPA to ensure specific compliance with ASC 606 regarding shipping and handling activities.
