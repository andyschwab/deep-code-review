**The Call:** A contract clause alone is insufficient to avoid money transmitter classification; regulators prioritize functional control over contractual disclaimers. You must technically architect your system to ensure you possess zero ability to initiate, authorize, or alter the movement of funds or the authoritative state of balances.

**The Numbers:**
*   **Licensing Cost:** $50,000/state × 3 states = **$150,000** (one-time).
*   **Surety Bond Requirement:** $200,000 × 3 states = **$600,000** (collateral/cost of carry).

**What Flips It, and The Trigger:**
The deciding factor is **functional custody**. If your database schema or API allows your system (or an admin) to write to balance fields based on anything other than a direct, immutable feed from a licensed bank or processor, you risk classification.
*   **Trigger:** If your engineering team implements *any* logic that calculates, adjusts, or reconciles balances internally before storing them, you cross the line.
*   **Rule of Thumb:** Data hosting records history; money transmission moves value. If you can "fix" a balance via a database update, you likely have custody.
*   **This Week's Step:** If your current architecture allows internal balance adjustments, consider engaging a fintech-specialized attorney to audit your data flow diagrams before signing any new enterprise contracts.

**Routing Note:**
Money transmitter laws are strictly jurisdiction-specific (NY DFS BitLicense, CA DFPI, TX DFI). You must route the final determination of your specific data flows and contract language to a licensed attorney in each target state. Do not rely on general rules; the definition of "custody" varies by regulator.

*Note: Technical prevention of write-access to balance fields (immutable logs only) is generally safer than contractual disclaimers, but only counsel can validate your specific implementation against state statutes.*
