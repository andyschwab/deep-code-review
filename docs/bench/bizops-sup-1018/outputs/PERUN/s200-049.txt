**The call:** Do not include the opportunity cost of aging inventory in your cash burn or runway calculation for your Series A deck; it artificially shortens your runway and misrepresents your liquidity. Your runway is strictly a function of cash on hand versus cash operating burn. Including implicit, non-cash costs like the time value of inventory will confuse investors and signal a misunderstanding of standard SaaS and CPG financial metrics, making you look less sophisticated, not more.

**The numbers:**
*   **Formula:** Runway (months) = Cash Balance / Monthly Net Burn.
*   **User Figures:** Cash Balance = $1,200,000; Monthly Operating Costs = $30,000.
*   **Assumption (Base Case):** Revenue is zero or negligible during the aging phase (conservative view), so Net Burn ≈ Operating Costs.
*   **Calculation (Standard):** $1,200,000 / $30,000 = **40 months**.
*   **Co-founder's Proposal (Opportunity Cost):**
    *   Inventory Value: $500,000.
    *   Opportunity Cost Rate: 8% annually.
    *   Annual Implicit Cost: $500,000 * 0.08 = $40,000.
    *   Monthly Implicit Cost: $40,000 / 12 ≈ $3,333.
    *   "Adjusted" Monthly Burn: $30,000 + $3,333 = $33,333.
    *   "Adjusted" Runway: $1,200,000 / $33,333 ≈ **36 months**.

**What flips it, and the trigger:**
The only input that changes the recommendation is if the $500,000 inventory requires immediate *cash* outflows (e.g., storage fees, insurance premiums) not already captured in the $30,000 operating costs. If those specific cash costs exceed $0, add them to the denominator. The trigger to revisit this is your next quarterly cash flow forecast; if storage costs rise above 5% of operating expenses, model them as a line item, not an abstract opportunity cost.
*Rule of thumb:* Investors buy equity based on cash efficiency, not accounting theory. A 4-month reduction in runway based on a non-cash charge looks like an error, not prudence.
*Step this week:* If your inventory carrying costs (insurance, warehousing) are significant, break them out as a specific line item in your P&L projection rather than blending them into a theoretical "cost of capital" adjustment.
