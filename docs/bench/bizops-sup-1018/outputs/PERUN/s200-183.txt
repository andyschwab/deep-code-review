**The Call**
Do not alter your fee structure, but immediately implement explicit, prominent disclosures separating payment from editorial decision-making; the risk is reputational and governed by self-regulatory codes (COPE, DOAJ) rather than a specific EU statute banning APCs, provided peer review remains blind to payment status.

**The Numbers**
Your current unit economics show a healthy surplus, but the "conflict cost" is binary (reputation loss vs. trust).
*   **Revenue:** 50 submissions/month × €1,200 APC = €60,000/month.
*   **Costs:** €25,000/month (fixed operating).
*   **Surplus:** €35,000/month.
*   **Assumption (ASSUMED):** Acceptance rate is independent of payment. If your acceptance rate exceeds 60% while charging APCs, industry benchmarks suggest scrutiny will increase regardless of disclosures.
*   **Break-even Volume:** €25,000 / €1,200 ≈ 21 submissions/month. You are well above survival mode, giving you runway to invest in robust disclosure mechanisms without financial distress.

**What Flips It, and the Trigger**
The single input that changes the call is **editorial access to payment data**. If editors see who has paid before making an acceptance decision, you move from a "perceived conflict" to an "actual integrity violation."
*   **Trigger:** If a single journal is flagged by the Directory of Open Access Journals (DOAJ) or Committee on Publication Ethics (COPE) for lacking separation of duties, you must immediately audit your submission workflow.
*   **Rule of Thumb:** In the EU, statutory law (like the German Act Against Unfair Competition) generally requires transparency, but research integrity is primarily enforced via indexing requirements. If you lose DOAJ indexing, your traffic and author submissions will likely drop by >50% within six months.
*   **Step this week:** If your submission system allows editors to view payment status, consider implementing a technical firewall that hides financial data until after the final editorial decision is logged.
