**The Call**
Use your specific historical failure rate (2%) as the base case for your financial model, but explicitly present a sensitivity scenario using the 5% industry average to demonstrate conservatism. Investors view company-specific data as more credible than generic benchmarks, provided you acknowledge the sample size limitation; relying solely on the higher industry rate without justification signals that you do not trust your own quality control or data.

**The Numbers**
Warranty Reserve = (Units Sold) × (Estimated Failure Rate) × (Cost Per Repair).

*Inputs:*
*   Units Sold: 10,000
*   Cost Per Repair: $100
*   Failure Rate A (Historical): 2% (0.02)
*   Failure Rate B (Industry): 5% (0.05) [ASSUMED: Industry benchmark applies to your specific product class]

*Computed Liability:*
*   **Base Case (Historical):** 10,000 × 0.02 × $100 = **$20,000**
*   **Conservative Case (Industry):** 10,000 × 0.05 × $100 = **$50,000**
*   **Variance Impact:** $30,000 difference in current liability, directly reducing working capital on the balance sheet.

**What Flips It, and The Trigger**
The deciding factor is **statistical significance and product maturity**. Your sample of 10,000 units is likely sufficient for a hardware startup, *unless* your "extreme weather" design is new and unproven compared to the legacy products comprising the industry average.
*   **Trigger:** If your current production batch uses new materials or manufacturing processes not present in the 10,000 historical units, the historical data becomes stale.
*   **Rule of Thumb:** If the new batch differs materially from the historical cohort, shift the base case toward the industry average (or a blended rate).
*   **Action Step:** This week, segment your 10,000 units by production date; if failure rates are trending down over time, present the weighted average to investors to prove quality improvement, rather than a flat 2% or 5%.

**Accounting Principles & Investor Credibility**
The guiding principle is **GAAP ASC 450 (Contingencies)**, which requires reserves to be based on the "best estimate" of probable losses, not the worst-case scenario.
1.  **Credibility:** Sophisticated hardware investors prefer a model grounded in your actual telemetry (2%) with a clear "what-if" buffer, rather than an inflated liability that artificially depresses valuation. Using the 5% rate blindly suggests you cannot distinguish your product performance from the market median.
2.  **Sample Size:** 10,000 units is generally statistically significant for early-stage hardware. The risk is not the number of units, but whether those units faced the same "extreme weather" conditions your marketing promises. If your historical sales were in mild climates, the 2% rate is not predictive of future extreme-weather performance.
3.  **Presentation:** In your investor deck, list the $20,000 reserve on the pro-forma balance sheet, but include a footnote or sidebar showing the $50,000 impact if failure rates match the industry average. This shows you understand the risk without penalizing your current valuation unnecessarily.
