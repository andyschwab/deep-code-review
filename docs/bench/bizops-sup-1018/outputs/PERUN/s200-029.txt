**The Call**
Structure your promotion as a post-sale manufacturer rebate; in California and most key markets, you must collect and remit sales tax on the full $110 selling price, not the net $85, because the tax base is fixed at the point of sale when the customer pays the full amount. Do not attempt to reduce your sales tax remittance based on the future rebate liability; the optimization lies in cash flow timing and clear consumer communication, not in lowering the tax base.

**The Numbers**
*   **Gross Revenue:** 3,000 units × $110 = $330,000 (Note: Your $80,000 projection implies only ~727 units sold; calculations below use your 3,000 unit volume for accuracy).
*   **Rebate Liability:** 3,000 units × $25 = $75,000 (Assumed 100% redemption rate; mark as ASSUMED).
*   **Net Revenue (GAAP):** $330,000 - $75,000 = $255,000.
*   **Sales Tax Base (CA):** $330,000 (Full price).
*   **Estimated Sales Tax (CA @ ~8.5% avg):** $330,000 × 0.085 = $28,050.
*   **Tax Savings if Discount Applied (Hypothetical):** $0 (Not permitted for post-sale rebates).
*   **Cash Retention Impact:** You hold the $25/unit cash float until redemption. If 20% of customers fail to redeem (industry standard ASSUMPTION), you retain an extra $15,000 in revenue, on which you have already paid sales tax.

**What Flips It, and the Trigger**
The single input that changes the tax treatment is the **mechanism of the price reduction**. If you change the promotion to an "instant discount" (selling the lantern for $85 at the register), the sales tax base drops to $85.
*   **Trigger:** If your cash flow allows absorbing the immediate price cut and you wish to lower the customer's upfront tax burden, switch to an instant discount.
*   **Rule of Thumb:** Post-sale rebates = Tax on Gross Price; Instant Discounts = Tax on Net Price.
*   **Action Step:** If redemption rates are historically below 80%, consider keeping the rebate model to capture breakage revenue, accepting the higher sales tax remittance as the cost of that potential upside.
