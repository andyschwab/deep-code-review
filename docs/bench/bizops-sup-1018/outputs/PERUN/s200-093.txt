**The Call**
Your current TAM is inflated; switch immediately from counting "active developers" to counting "billable seats" or "organizations," as institutional investors reject top-down headcount math that ignores multi-stack overlap and purchasing reality.

**The Numbers**
*Current Method (Flawed):*
Formula: `Total Developers × Annual Price`
Input: 2,500,000 developers × ($40 × 12 months)
Result: **$1.2 Billion TAM**
*Assumption:* Every developer is a unique, single-stack buyer. (Marked INVALID due to 30% overlap).

*Adjusted Method (Seat-Based):*
Formula: `(Total Developers × Overlap Factor) × Annual Price`
Assumption (Low): 20% overlap → 2.0M unique seats → **$960M TAM**
Assumption (Base): 30% overlap (your data) → 1.75M unique seats → **$840M TAM**
Assumption (High): 40% overlap (enterprise consolidation) → 1.5M unique seats → **$720M TAM**

*Unit Economics Reality Check:*
Formula: `LTV = (ARPA × Gross Margin) / Churn` vs `CAC`
Input: ARPA $480/yr, CAC $120.
Result: You need an LTV:CAC ratio >3:1. If churn exceeds 2.5% monthly, your unit economics break regardless of TAM size.

**What Flips It, and the Trigger**
The input that changes the investor's perception is the **definition of the buying unit**. If you present "developers" as the market, investors assume you must sell to every individual. If you present "seats" or "teams," you align with how budgets are approved.
*Trigger:* If your pilot data shows >25% of users connecting multiple stacks, stop using "North American Developer Count" entirely.
*Rule of Thumb:* Investors discount top-down TAM by 50% if the buying unit (individual) does not match the billing unit (seat/org).
*Step this week:* Re-calculate your TAM using a "Bottom-Up" approach: `(Target Organizations × Avg Developers per Org × Penetration Rate)`. If this number is <10% of your top-down figure, lead your deck with the bottom-up number only.
