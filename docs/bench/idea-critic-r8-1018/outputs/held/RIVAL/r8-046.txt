**Recommendation for Anna Weber:**
Proceed with the €30,000 pilot strictly as a **technical and behavioral proof-of-concept**, not a revenue generator. Before signing the full rollout contract, you must mandate a clause requiring the vendor to guarantee bandwidth performance for 50 concurrent users at no extra cost; if they cannot contractually guarantee this, do not deploy beyond the pilot. The current financial model is fragile because it assumes historical attendance from a different era without accounting for the hidden operational costs of staffing and marketing that sank the 2018 Vienna project's true ROI.

**Three Main Failure Points:**
1.  **Infrastructure Bottleneck:** Without known bandwidth requirements per user, the museum's existing network could collapse under load, causing latency that induces motion sickness and immediately halts visitor throughput, turning the attraction into a liability rather than a draw.
2.  **Operational Cost Blindness:** The budget allocates zero euros for the additional staff needed to manage headsets, hygiene, and user flow. If the 20-minute experience requires 5 minutes of staff setup/cleanup time, you will need significantly more personnel than currently planned, eroding the margin before the first ticket is sold.
3.  **Optimistic Throughput Assumptions:** Banking on 15,000 visitors based on a 20-minute cycle time ignores real-world friction (late arrivals, technical glitches, cleaning). If actual throughput drops by just 15% due to these factors, you will miss the break-even point entirely, leaving the €90,000 rollout as a sunk cost with no path to recovery.

**Kill/Pause Trigger:**
Pause the full rollout immediately if the pilot phase (Oct–Dec 2024) fails to achieve an average of **45 completed sessions per operating day** or if the vendor confirms that supporting more than 10 concurrent users requires hardware upgrades exceeding **€5,000**. These thresholds indicate that the projected 15,000 annual visitors are mathematically impossible under current constraints.
