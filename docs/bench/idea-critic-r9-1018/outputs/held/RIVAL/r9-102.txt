**Strongest Objection: The Math Doesn't Hold**
Your pilot spend calculation is incorrect, which undermines the financial credibility of the entire pitch. 150 devices at $350 each equals **$52,500**, not $55,000. While a $2,500 discrepancy seems minor, presenting inflated costs alongside a $12 million ARR projection signals sloppy unit economics. Furthermore, your ARR projection relies on a "hockey stick" conversion assumption: going from 15% conversion in a pilot to a scale that generates $12M implies acquiring roughly 83,000 *paying* subscribers (at $12/mo). If the pilot only proved 15% conversion among 150 users, you have no data validating you can acquire the ~550,000 total users required to hit that revenue target by Q4 2025. The jump from pilot validation to mass-market scale is the widest gap in your narrative.

**More Compelling Go-to-Market Alternative**
Pivot from a direct-to-consumer (DTC) subscription model to a **B2B2C "Team License" model**. Instead of chasing individual athlete subscriptions ($12/mo is low value for a hardware company given support costs), sell annual licenses directly to professional teams, university athletic departments, and high-performance training centers.
*   **Why:** It lowers customer acquisition costs (one contract vs. thousands of users), increases average contract value, and leverages the "pro endorsement" effect. If the pros use it, the amateurs will buy the hardware even without a subscription. This de-risks the churn assumption inherent in the DTC model.

**Pull-the-Plug Metrics (Kill Criteria)**
1.  **Regulatory:** If FDA 510(k) clearance is not received by **October 1, 2024**. (Pushing manufacturing start to July without clearance burns cash on inventory that cannot be sold; a 3-month slip here jeopardizes the Q2 2025 revenue target entirely).
2.  **Product-Market Fit:** If daily wear rate drops below **65%** in a follow-up study of 500 users. (80% in a small, highly motivated pilot of 150 is optimistic; if it dips below 65% at scale, the data continuity required for the "performance insights" value prop fails, and churn will spike).
3.  **Conversion:** If paid conversion trails below **5%** after the first 1,000 units. (Your model assumes 15%; if real-world friction cuts this by two-thirds, the CAC to acquire a paying user likely exceeds their Lifetime Value, breaking the unit economics).

**Missing Element**
You are missing a **hardware margin analysis**. You mention the $350 cost but not the sale price or gross margin. If you sell the device at a loss to drive subscription revenue, you need to explicitly state the "subsidy per user" and how much cash reserve is required to fund that hardware burn before subscription revenue kicks in. Investors will ask this immediately.
