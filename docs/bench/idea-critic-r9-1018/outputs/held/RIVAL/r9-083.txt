**First Red Flag: The Break-Even Math Ignores Permit Delays and Downtime**
Your 2-year break-even claim is fragile because it assumes immediate operation and 100% uptime. If the environmental permit takes 90 days (the upper bound you noted) instead of 30, you lose $45,000 in delayed savings ($180k/year ÷ 4). Furthermore, your model ignores the cost of downtime. If the treatment tanks go down for 5 days per incident just twice a year, you lose another $15,000 annually in unrealized savings. These combined risks could push your actual break-even point past 3 years, which may fail your internal hurdle rates for capital projects.

**Suggested Better Path: Stage the Investment**
Instead of committing the full $1.2 million upfront for the 2M liter/day capacity, propose a **Phase 1 Pilot** targeting 500kL/day (25% of the goal).
*   **Capital:** Likely ~$350k–$400k.
*   **Benefit:** Validates the electricity assumption ($0.08/kWh) and the actual maintenance downtime before scaling.
*   **Strategic Win:** You can begin operations sooner (smaller systems often face lighter permitting scrutiny), generating early cash flow to fund Phase 2. This reduces your "at-risk" capital while the 90-day permit uncertainty resolves.

**Scenarios to Abandon the Project**
Pull the plug immediately if any of the following occur:
1.  **Permit > 60 Days:** If the regulator hasn't approved by day 60, the delayed ROI likely destroys the project's Net Present Value (NPV).
2.  **Electricity Spikes:** If local energy rates rise above $0.11/kWh, the operational cost to treat the water may exceed the cost of buying fresh water, turning the system into a liability.
3.  **Downtime Frequency:** If the pilot or vendor data shows downtime exceeds 10 days per year, the reliability risk outweighs the 30% recycling benefit.
