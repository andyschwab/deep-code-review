**Top Three Failure Modes**

1.  **NPS Erosion Outweighs Revenue Gain:** The model assumes a static 78% load factor. If dynamic pricing is perceived as "gouging," loyal customers may defect to competitors. A 5% drop in NPS could cost more in lifetime value (LTV) than the $1.2M pilot gain, especially if it triggers negative social sentiment on our busiest routes.
2.  **Integration Scope Creep:** The $150k integration budget is likely underestimated. Connecting real-time pricing engines to legacy reservation systems often reveals data latency issues that require manual overrides, bloating operational costs and delaying the feedback loop needed to tune the algorithm.
3.  **Fuel Cost Sensitivity:** The plan assumes flat fuel costs. If fuel prices spike in Q2, the baseline cost structure changes. Dynamic pricing optimized for a low-fuel environment might suppress demand too aggressively when consumers are already price-sensitive due to broader economic factors, causing the revenue lift to vanish.

**Pull-the-Plug Metric**

**Trigger:** Halt the initiative immediately if **Net Revenue lift falls below 4% OR NPS drops by more than 3 points** in any rolling two-week window during the pilot.
*Reasoning:* Waiting a full month (as suggested) is too long; algorithmic pricing errors compound quickly. A two-week window provides enough data for statistical significance while limiting exposure. The 4% revenue threshold provides a buffer below the 8% target to account for normal variance, while the NPS clause protects the brand.

**Hidden Risks & Arithmetic Checks**

*   **Training Budget Absorption:** You noted staff training is "lumped into" the $150k integration line. This is risky. If integration hits technical snags (common), the training budget may be cannibalized to fix code, leaving staff unprepared to handle customer complaints about price volatility. Unbundle this: allocate a fixed $50k for training that cannot be touched by engineering.
*   **The "Flat Fuel" Fallacy:** The model treats fuel as a constant, but dynamic pricing interacts with consumer disposable income. If fuel rises, consumer discretionary income falls. The model may not account for this second-order effect, potentially overstating the 8% lift in a volatile macro environment.
*   **Pilot Route Selection Bias:** Running this on the "12 busiest routes" introduces selection bias. These routes often have inelastic demand (business travelers). The model's success here may not translate to the broader network where leisure travelers are more price-sensitive. Ensure Marta understands this pilot proves viability only for high-density corridors, not the whole domestic network.
