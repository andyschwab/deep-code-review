**Strongest Objection: The Financial Model Relies on Static Variables in a Volatile Policy Environment**

Your proposal presents a deterministic financial model ($12M cost, 5-year payback) based on assumptions that are currently unstable. The primary risk is not technical performance, but the fragility of the revenue stack. You are assuming the 30% state tax credit is guaranteed and that electricity prices will remain static relative to your fixed O&M budget. If the state legislature modifies the credit structure post-2026 as hinted, or if electricity prices rise slower than your implicit inflation assumptions, the payback period could extend from 5 years to 9+ years. Furthermore, budgeting $200k/year for O&M without an escalation clause ignores historical trends in labor and specialized component costs, creating a hidden liability that will erode net savings annually. The Riverdale College case study is dangerous precedent if their utility rate environment or incentive package differs materially from ours; a 25% bill reduction there does not guarantee equivalent performance here.

**Clearly Better Route: Phased Power Purchase Agreement (PPA)**

Instead of a capital-intensive upfront purchase, pursue a Power Purchase Agreement (PPA) with a third-party developer. This route eliminates the $12M upfront capital risk and transfers the performance and maintenance risk to the vendor. Crucially, a PPA allows you to lock in a fixed electricity rate for 15–20 years, providing immediate budget certainty without exposing the university to O&M inflation or technology obsolescence. Alternatively, execute a phased rollout: install 50% (1 acre) first to validate actual production data and policy stability before committing the remaining capital. This "real options" approach preserves capital and allows you to pivot if the 2026 legislative landscape shifts unfavorably.
