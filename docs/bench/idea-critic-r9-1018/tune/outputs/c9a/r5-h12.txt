**Recommendation: Go, conditional on a hard cap on parts-cost exposure.**
The program's projected $30M revenue stream is mathematically sound ($12k × 150 × 20 = $36M nominal; your $30M figure likely accounts for the 5% discount rate or conservative churn), providing a strong base to cover the $2.4M initial investment. However, the unmodeled 8% aluminum price hike introduces a structural margin risk that must be contained before launch.

**Three Biggest Failure Modes:**
1.  **Compounding Input Cost Volatility:** You identified a $200k/year risk from aluminum, but this is a floor, not a ceiling. If other raw materials (copper, silicon) correlate with aluminum or if logistics costs spike, the "fixed" maintenance fee becomes a loss leader. Without a price-adjustment clause in the contract, inflation directly erodes the 20-year margin.
2.  **The "Cliff" Attrition Risk:** You assumed <5% churn, yet admitted zero data on post-warranty behavior. The real failure isn't gradual churn; it's a mass exodus at Year 15 or 20 when customers realize the cost of a new system is lower than maintaining an aging one. If the average lifespan is 25 years, the final 5 years of your contract cover failing systems with high repair frequency, potentially flipping the unit economics negative in the very years you projected pure profit.
3.  **Labor Scalability Mismatch:** The $1.8M crew budget assumes linear scaling. If the 150 systems (or future growth) require non-linear support due to unforeseen technical faults or regulatory inspection changes, labor costs will outpace the fixed revenue per unit, breaking the model faster than parts costs will.

**Pre-agreed Kill Trigger:**
Activate the exit clause if **annualized parts and labor costs exceed 45% of that year's collected revenue for two consecutive quarters.** This specific metric captures both the aluminum spike and the labor scalability issue, forcing a pause before the cumulative deficit threatens the core business.
