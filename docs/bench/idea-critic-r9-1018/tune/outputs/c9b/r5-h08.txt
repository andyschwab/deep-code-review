Your current arithmetic contains a critical strategic vulnerability, not just a slip. While $2.5M divided by $300k annually does equal roughly 8.3–8.5 years, this payback period is likely unacceptable for a capital-intensive manufacturing upgrade. Most breweries target 3–5 year paybacks for packaging lines to account for technology obsolescence and maintenance creep. An 8.5-year horizon leaves almost no margin for error before the equipment requires major overhaul or replacement. Furthermore, your model assumes the $300k savings is net; it likely ignores the new operational costs of an in-house line (labor, electricity, maintenance parts, quality control staff), which would extend the payback well beyond a decade.

**Three Most Serious Failure Modes:**

1.  **Utilization Collapse:** You assume 85% utilization by Q4 2026 based on 15% demand growth. If demand grows at only 5% or stalls due to market saturation, the line sits idle. Unlike outsourced canning, where you pay per unit, an idle in-house line incurs fixed costs (depreciation, loan interest, base labor) regardless of volume, turning a cost-saving measure into a massive cash drain.
2.  **The "Hidden" OpEx Trap:** The proposal compares current outsourcing fees against *gross* savings, not *net* savings. In-house canning introduces significant new variable costs: specialized labor (machine operators, mechanics), utilities, packaging waste, and inventory carrying costs for empty cans. If these operational expenses consume 40–50% of the $300k "savings," your ROI halves, and the payback period doubles to 17 years.
3.  **Vendor & Timeline Risk:** Starting build in March 2025 with unconfirmed lead times is a schedule failure waiting to happen. If the vendor delays delivery by 6 months (common in current supply chains), you incur financing costs on the $2.5M for half a year with zero revenue generation, immediately adding ~$100k+ in interest expense and pushing your break-even date further out.
