### Recommendation: Do Not Build the Full Integration Yet

**Decision:** Reject the request for a full three-week development cycle immediately. Instead, propose a "Concierge MVP" or manual workaround for the five C-level executives while validating broader demand.

**Rationale:**
Building a deep, automated integration based solely on five requests from a single client violates core discovery principles. You are currently facing a **Value Risk** (will mid-level managers actually use this?) and a **Viability Risk** (is this a scalable feature or a custom service contract?). The discrepancy between C-level demands and the silence from 2,000 other seats suggests this may be a specific workflow anomaly for that one enterprise, not a universal market need.

If you commit three weeks of engineering time now, you incur a high **opportunity cost**, delaying other roadmap items that might serve the broader 2,000-seat base. Furthermore, building the full automation assumes the problem is purely technical (syncing calendars) when the root cause might be organizational (lack of scheduling protocols). If the mid-level managers do not adopt the tool even after the sync is built, the three weeks are wasted.

**Proposed Action Plan (Next 3 Weeks):**

1.  **Week 1: Validate & Manual Service**
    *   Interview 5–10 mid-level managers from the same enterprise client. Ask: "Walk me through the last time you struggled to schedule a meeting." Do not mention the sync feature yet. Determine if the pain point is truly calendar data or something else (e.g., approval chains, room availability).
    *   Offer the five C-level executives a **manual concierge solution**. Have a support engineer or the PM manually sync their calendars or manage scheduling for one week. This proves value without writing code.

2.  **Week 2: Low-Fidelity Test**
    *   If the manual process saves time, create a simple, non-integrated script or a Zapier prototype for a small pilot group (10 users). Measure actual usage, not just interest.
    *   Survey 50 users from *other* enterprise clients with a specific question about their current scheduling friction to see if the pain exists outside the one vocal client.

3.  **Week 3: Decision Gate**
    *   **Go:** Only if the manual/concierge test shows measurable time savings AND at least 15% of the broader survey respondents indicate similar high-friction pain points.
    *   **No-Go:** If mid-level managers show indifference or if the pain is isolated to the five executives. In this case, negotiate the renewal based on the *promise* of the feature pending further validation, or offer the manual service as a premium "white-glove" onboarding perk.

**Message to Stakeholders:**
*   **To Sales:** "We can commit to solving the scheduling problem for these five executives immediately via a managed service, ensuring their renewal satisfaction. However, committing engineering resources to a full build before validating adoption among their teams risks delivering a feature nobody uses, which hurts our long-term credibility."
*   **To Engineering:** "We are pausing the full build to run a two-week validation sprint. This prevents us from building complex integration logic for a problem that might be solved simpler or doesn't exist broadly. We will revisit the build spec only if the concierge test confirms high adoption."

This approach de-risks the investment, satisfies the immediate client need through service rather than software, and ensures that if you do build, you are building for the market, not just one vocal segment.

*Note: This strategy shifts the conversation from "building a feature" to "solving a problem," aligning with continuous discovery habits.*
