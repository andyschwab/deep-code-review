# Decision Framework: Mobile-Responsive Editor vs. Churn Risk

**Current State Assessment**
The conflict is between **stated intent** (42 tickets, 12 churn threats) and **revealed behavior** (2% mobile open rate). The critical disconnect is the assumption that "mobile editing" solves "mobile reading." If recipients aren't reading on mobile, the urgency for *senders* to edit on mobile may be a proxy for a different problem (e.g., slow desktop workflow, lack of templates, or fear of future obsolescence).

**Risk Analysis**
*   **Churn Risk (High Impact, Low Certainty):** 12 customers explicitly threatening churn represents immediate revenue loss. However, stated intent to churn often exceeds actual churn when alternatives are sticky or switching costs are high.
*   **Usage Risk (Low Impact, High Certainty):** 2% mobile open rate suggests the *output* doesn't need mobile optimization yet. Building a mobile *editor* to solve a mobile *reading* problem is a mismatch.
*   **Opportunity Cost:** Committing 3 weeks of dev time to a feature addressing a behavior (mobile editing) that hasn't been observed risks building a "nice-to-have" while ignoring the root cause of the support tickets.

**Decision Rule**
Do not commit the full 3-week build until we validate **who** is asking for mobile editing and **why**. If the requestors are power users who currently edit late at night or while traveling, the 2% open rate is irrelevant; the value is sender convenience, not recipient experience. If the requestors are general users conflating "mobile-friendly emails" with "mobile editing," the build is premature.

### Evidence Gap & Required Research
Before April 10th, gather the following specific data points:
1.  **Segment the 42 Tickets:** Identify the job titles and usage patterns of the 12 threatening churn. Are they solo founders (likely mobile-heavy) or enterprise marketers (likely desktop-heavy)?
2.  **The "Last Time" Question:** Contact 5 of the 12 churn-risk customers. Ask: *"Tell me about the last time you needed to edit an email but couldn't because you weren't at a desktop."* Do not ask if they *want* mobile editing; ask for the specific story of the failure.
3.  **Workflow Audit:** Determine if the 2% mobile open rate correlates with the customers requesting the feature. If the low mobile-open customers are the ones screaming for mobile edit, the feature won't save them.

### Proposed Low-Effort Experiment: The "Concierge" Test
**Objective:** Validate demand for *mobile editing* specifically, distinct from *mobile-responsive templates*.
**Method:**
1.  **Setup:** Create a simple landing page or in-app banner available only to the 42 requestors: *"Beta: Edit your campaigns on your phone. Join the waitlist."*
2.  **Action:** When a user signs up, do not build the feature. Instead, manually offer them a 15-minute call to "onboard them to the beta."
3.  **Measurement:**
    *   **Conversion Rate:** How many of the 42 click the link?
    *   **No-Show Rate:** How many miss the onboarding call? (High no-show = low pain).
    *   **The "Fake Door" Metric:** If possible, let them click "Start Mobile Edit," then show a message: *"Mobile editor launching soon. Want us to notify you?"* Measure the drop-off.
**Kill Threshold:** If fewer than 20% of the 42 requestors engage with the beta sign-up, or if fewer than 3 show up for the manual concierge session, **do not build**. The vocal demand is not strong enough to justify 3 weeks of engineering.

**Recommendation to VP:** Pause the 3-week build allocation. Run the concierge test and 5 discovery interviews immediately. If the test confirms active mobile editing attempts, scope a 1-week MVP (text-only editing) rather than a full responsive suite.

---
*Note: This framework prioritizes behavioral evidence over vocal requests to prevent building unused capacity. The 2% open rate suggests the market isn't ready for mobile-first consumption, so the editor must solve a specific sender workflow pain to be viable.*
