**Recommendation: Do Not Roll Out; Extend Test with Revised Hypothesis**

Based on the data provided (p=0.14, 2% lift, 3,000 users), there is insufficient evidence to approve engineering resources for a full rollout. Rolling out now carries a high risk of implementing a change that provides no actual value, while the potential upside of a mere 2% improvement may not justify the engineering maintenance cost unless your traffic volume is massive.

However, declaring the test a definitive "failure" is also premature. A p-value of 0.14 indicates a 14% probability of observing this result if the new flow had zero effect. While this fails the standard 5% threshold, it suggests a plausible positive signal that warrants further investigation rather than immediate dismissal.

**Decision Framework for the VP of Product**

**1. The Risk of False Positives vs. Opportunity Cost**
At p=0.14, the likelihood that this result is noise is significant. If you roll out based on this trend, you risk "polluting" the product with complexity that doesn't move the needle. Conversely, if the engineering cost to maintain the new flow is low and the risk of negative side effects is negligible, the threshold for adoption should be lower. However, since you are asking for resource approval for a *full rollout*, the bar for certainty must remain high.

**2. Re-evaluating the Primary Metric**
Your hypothesis states the new flow reduces *cognitive load* to help teachers set up *faster*. Yet, you measured *setup completion*. These are different outcomes.
*   **Completion** measures if they finished.
*   **Speed/Efficiency** measures how hard it was.
It is possible the new flow makes the process significantly faster (validating your hypothesis) but does not increase the number of people who finish (perhaps the drop-off is due to lack of content, not UI friction). You may be measuring the wrong signal for your specific hypothesis.

**3. The "Peeking" and Duration Problem**
Running a test for a fixed two weeks without a pre-calculated sample size often leads to underpowered results. With 3,000 users, if your baseline completion rate is low (e.g., 20%), detecting a 2% relative lift requires a much larger sample than if your baseline is high. The test likely stopped before reaching the necessary statistical power to detect such a small effect size.

**Actionable Next Steps**

1.  **Do Not Roll Out Yet:** The statistical uncertainty is too high to commit significant engineering resources.
2.  **Extend the Test (With Caution):** If traffic allows, continue the test to reach a pre-determined sample size calculated to detect a 2% lift with 80% power. Do not simply "add a month"; calculate the exact number of additional users needed.
3.  **Add Secondary Metrics Immediately:** Instrument tracking for *time-to-complete* and *steps-taken*. If the new flow reduces time-by-30% but completion stays flat, you have validated the "cognitive load" hypothesis even if the completion metric doesn't budge. This might justify a rollout for user experience reasons, even without a conversion lift.
4.  **Segment Analysis:** Check if the 2% lift is concentrated among specific teacher segments (e.g., tech-savvy vs. novice). A global average might hide a significant win for your core persona.

**Conclusion**
The current data is inconclusive, not negative. The prudent business decision is to pause the rollout discussion, refine the success metrics to align with the "cognitive load" hypothesis, and gather more data before committing resources.

*Note: Ensure any extension uses a fixed sample size calculated beforehand to avoid "p-hacking" by stopping as soon as significance is reached.*
*Consider the engineering maintenance cost of the new flow; if near-zero, a lower confidence threshold might be acceptable for UX improvements.*
