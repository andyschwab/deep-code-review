### Analysis Plan: Checkout Redesign & Retention Disconnect

**Objective:** Determine if the March 1st checkout redesign drove low-quality impulse purchases or created expectation mismatches, and identify specific segments driving the 90-day retention drop (22% → 8%).

#### 1. Critical Data Slices to Examine

Do not analyze aggregate data alone. Segment the post-March 1st cohort by these dimensions to isolate the root cause:

*   **Product Category & Price Point:** Compare retention for high-consideration items (sofas, dining sets) vs. low-consideration decor (pillows, lamps). If retention dropped only in high-ticket categories, the issue is likely buyer's remorse on impulse decisions.
*   **Customer Type (New vs. Returning):** Did the new flow alienate loyal customers who valued the previous process, or is the drop entirely driven by new, one-time buyers?
*   **Traffic Source:** Segment by `utm_medium`. Paid social traffic often drives higher impulse rates than organic search. If retention plummeted specifically for paid social but held steady for organic, the checkout is amplifying a traffic quality issue, not creating one.
*   **Time-on-Page & Scroll Depth (Pre-Checkout):** Correlate product page engagement with checkout completion. Users who spent <15 seconds on the product page before purchasing are high-risk for regret.
*   **Return Rate by SKU:** Cross-reference the new purchasers with return data. A spike in returns for specific items indicates a product/expectation mismatch rather than a general checkout flaw.

#### 2. Primary Hypotheses to Test

**Hypothesis A: The "Impulse Regret" Theory**
*   *Premise:* The frictionless flow reduced cognitive load too much, allowing users to buy before validating need or fit.
*   *Validation:* Check if the average order value (AOV) for non-returning customers is significantly lower than returning customers. Analyze if "time to return" has shortened (e.g., returns happening within 48 hours vs. 2 weeks).
*   *Segment Focus:* New customers, mobile users, low-price items.

**Hypothesis B: The "Expectation Mismatch" Theory**
*   *Premise:* The redesign removed critical information (shipping timelines, material details, return policy links) previously visible during checkout, leading to surprise and dissatisfaction.
*   *Validation:* Review customer support tickets post-March 1st for keywords like "shipping time," "material," or "return policy." Compare cancellation rates (orders cancelled before shipping) between pre- and post-redesign cohorts.
*   *Segment Focus:* High-ticket items, specific product categories with complex shipping.

**Hypothesis C: The "Loyalist Alienation" Theory**
*   *Premise:* The new flow optimized for new acquisition but broke a workflow or removed a feature (e.g., saved addresses, gift options) valued by repeat buyers.
*   *Validation:* Compare the repeat purchase rate of the *pre-March* customer base specifically. If their personal retention dropped, the UI change is the culprit. If their retention is stable but diluted by a flood of one-time buyers, the issue is acquisition quality.

#### 3. Immediate Actionable Recommendations

1.  **Pause Broad Reversion:** Do not revert the entire checkout yet. The 5.2% completion rate is a significant revenue driver.
2.  **Implement "Friction for Value":** If Hypothesis A is confirmed, introduce targeted friction only for high-risk segments (e.g., a "Are you sure?" confirmation for first-time buyers spending over $500, or re-inserting shipping estimates prominently).
3.  **Post-Purchase Intervention:** Launch an immediate email/SMS sequence for new purchasers reinforcing product value, care instructions, and easy return policies to reduce anxiety and buyer's remorse.
4.  **Qualitative Flash Study:** Conduct 5-10 rapid user interviews with customers who purchased in March but haven't returned. Ask specifically about their decision process and post-purchase feelings.

**Note:** Ensure your analytics window matches the 90-day cohort exactly; do not mix partial months which will skew the retention rate. Prioritize the "Loyalist Alienation" check first, as losing existing LTV is more dangerous than acquiring low-quality new users.
