### Re-Analysis Plan: Separating Novelty from Value

To isolate the underlying value signal from the initial curiosity spike, you must restructure your analysis from a simple "before vs. after" comparison to a **cohort-based retention curve analysis** with a defined **stabilization window**. Do not average the full two weeks; this blends the novelty spike with the friction-induced drop, obscuring the true signal.

**1. Define the Stabilization Window**
Exclude Days 1–3 entirely from your primary "value" metric calculation. These days represent "novelty usage" driven by discovery, not utility. Based on your data, the signal stabilized (or degraded) by Day 10. Set your **Evaluation Window** as **Days 4–14**. This filters out the initial curiosity spike and measures only the behavior of users who had time to encounter the friction you suspect.

**2. Construct Cohort Retention Curves**
Instead of aggregating all users, group them by **Signup Date** (Daily Cohorts). Plot two lines on a single chart:
*   **Control Group:** Retention rate by Day X (relative to signup).
*   **Test Group:** Retention rate by Day X (relative to signup).

Focus specifically on the **Day 7** and **Day 14** retention deltas between these two lines within the Evaluation Window. If the Test Group curve diverges negatively from the Control Group starting around Day 4–5 and stays flat or declines, this confirms your hypothesis: the feature attracts attention but fails to deliver recurring value, actively harming retention.

**3. Calculate the "Net Friction Cost"**
Compute the specific retention loss attributed to the feature using only the stabilized data:
*   *Formula:* `(Control Retention Days 4–14) - (Test Retention Days 4–14)`
*   If this number is negative (as your 5% drop suggests), this is your **Net Friction Cost**. This is the metric to present to the VP. It answers: "Once the novelty wore off, how much did this feature hurt us?"

**4. Segment by "Sharers" vs. "Non-Sharers"**
Within the Test Group, create a micro-segment of users who actually completed a share vs. those who only saw the button.
*   If "Sharers" have *higher* retention than "Non-Sharers," the friction is in the **flow** (your assumption is correct); the value exists if they can complete the action.
*   If "Sharers" have *equal or lower* retention than "Non-Sharers," the feature itself may be irrelevant or annoying, regardless of flow friction.

**Recommendation Framework for the VP**
Present the decision based on the **Net Friction Cost** and the **Sharer Delta**:
*   **Kill Feature:** If Net Friction Cost is significant (>2%) AND Sharers show no retention benefit. The feature creates noise without value.
*   **Iterate on Friction:** If Net Friction Cost is significant BUT Sharers show high retention. The value is proven; the barrier to entry is the only problem. Prioritize fixing the sharing flow immediately.
*   **Monitor:** If Net Friction Cost is negligible (<1%). The impact is too small to justify major surgery; deprioritize.

Do not weigh early days against later days. Treat them as distinct phases: Phase 1 (Discovery) and Phase 2 (Utility). Your business case rests entirely on Phase 2.

*Ensure your observation windows match exactly when calculating rates; do not divide full-period spend by partial-period results.*
*Label any users still within their 14-day window as "Pending" rather than counting them against retention.*
