### Experiment Analysis: Newsletter Subject Line A/B Test

**Status:** Inconclusive / Preliminary Negative Signal
**Recommendation:** Do not kill the emoji strategy entirely yet; do not implement Variant B. Prioritize deliverability diagnostics and segment analysis before final conclusion.

#### 1. Statistical Significance Assessment
With a sample size of 5,000 per variant, we must determine if the 1.2% vs. 1.5% difference is real or noise.
*   **Variant A (Control):** 75 opens (1.5% of 5,000)
*   **Variant B (Emoji):** 60 opens (1.2% of 5,000)
*   **Result:** Using a standard two-proportion z-test, the p-value is approximately **0.19**.
*   **Interpretation:** This result is **not statistically significant** at the standard 95% confidence level (p < 0.05). There is a 19% probability this difference occurred by random chance. We cannot confidently declare Variant B the "loser" based solely on this math, though the trend is negative.

#### 2. The Anomaly: Systemic Deliverability Issue
The critical finding is not the difference between A and B, but that **both** performed drastically below your 4% historical average.
*   **Historical Baseline:** ~200 opens expected per 5,000 recipients.
*   **Actual Performance:** ~60–75 opens.
*   **Implication:** Something suppressed opens for *everyone*. Since bounce rates were steady (0.3%), emails likely reached the inbox but were ignored, hidden, or filtered to secondary tabs (Promotions/Spam) without triggering a hard bounce. The emoji hypothesis is currently confounded by this systemic drop.

#### 3. Required Data Cuts Before Friday
To answer the Marketing Director, perform these four specific analyses:

**A. Client & Device Segmentation**
Break open rates by email client (Gmail, Outlook, Apple Mail) and device (Mobile vs. Desktop).
*   *Why:* Emojis render differently across clients. If Outlook (which often blocks images/emojis by default) makes up a large chunk of your list, Variant B might appear as blank text or broken characters, suppressing opens only in that segment while Gmail users performed normally.

**B. Domain-Level Performance**
Segment by email domain (e.g., @gmail.com, @corporate.com).
*   *Why:* Corporate firewalls or specific ISP filters may have flagged the emoji subject lines specifically for business domains, while consumer domains remained unaffected. If @gmail.com shows normal open rates but @corporate.com shows near-zero, the issue is enterprise filtering, not user preference.

**C. Time-to-Open Velocity**
Analyze *when* the opens occurred relative to send time.
*   *Why:* If Variant B has a delayed open curve compared to A, it suggests emails landed in a secondary tab (like Gmail's Promotions) where users check later, rather than the primary inbox.

**D. Historical Control Comparison**
Compare Variant A (1.5%) against your actual historical average for similar sends (4%), not just against Variant B.
*   *Why:* This quantifies the "external factor" impact. If Variant A is also down 62% from normal, the test environment was compromised (e.g., send time error, list fatigue, seasonal dip, or reputation hit).

#### 4. Strategic Recommendation
Present the following narrative to the team:
1.  **The test was inconclusive** regarding emojis due to lack of statistical significance (p=0.19).
2.  **The primary finding is a deliverability anomaly:** Both variants underperformed historical norms by ~60%, indicating an external factor suppressed visibility for the entire send.
3.  **Action Plan:** Do not ban emojis. Instead, investigate ISP filtering rules for special characters and re-run the test once baseline open rates return to the 4% historical average. If the baseline remains low, audit sender reputation and authentication records (DKIM/SPF) before testing creative changes again.

*Note: Ensure your email platform's "open tracking" pixel isn't being blocked by a recent privacy update (like Apple MPP), which can artificially deflate open rates across the board.*
*If possible, cross-reference click-through rates (CTR); if CTR remained stable while opens dropped, the tracking pixel may be the culprit rather than actual user behavior.*
