**Recommendation: Do Not Roll Forward Yet; Iterate or Revert**

The result is **statistically valid but practically negligible**. While the p-value of 0.03 confirms the lift is real and not random noise, the absolute impact (12 extra bookings/day) fails to justify the engineering cost of maintaining a divergent code path. Presenting this as a "success" based solely on significance misleads stakeholders about business value.

**Framing for Stakeholders**
1.  **Distinguish Signal from Value:** Explicitly state that "statistically significant" means the measurement is precise, not that the outcome is profitable. The signal is real, but the magnitude is below the threshold of operational relevance.
2.  **The Cost of Complexity:** Highlight that maintaining a new code path incurs ongoing technical debt, testing overhead, and potential friction for future features. If the gain does not cover these hidden costs, the net business value is negative.
3.  **The Compounding Reality:** Address the "compounding" hypothesis directly. Even if this 0.4% lift persists for a year, the cumulative revenue likely remains marginal compared to the opportunity cost of engineering hours spent maintaining this specific optimization versus building new revenue drivers. This suggests a ceiling on what *this specific* checkout tweak can achieve, not necessarily a ceiling on all optimization.

**Decision Logic**
*   **Roll Forward?** No. The return on investment (ROI) is negative when engineering maintenance is factored in.
*   **Revert?** Yes, if the code path introduces complexity. Reverting simplifies the system and frees resources.
*   **Iterate?** Only if you have a specific, high-confidence hypothesis that a *different* change to this flow could yield a 10x larger lift (e.g., 4%+). Do not iterate just to squeeze more out of a 0.4% gain; the law of diminishing returns applies heavily here.

**Actionable Next Steps**
1.  **Define a "Minimum Detectable Effect" (MDE) for Business Viability:** Before running the next test, calculate the minimum lift required to cover engineering costs. If the next test cannot realistically detect this larger effect with the available sample size, do not run it.
2.  **Shift Focus Upstream:** Since checkout friction appears optimized to a point of diminishing returns, redirect experimentation resources to **Activation** or **Retention** levers (e.g., search relevance, personalization, post-booking engagement) where the potential delta in user behavior is larger.
3.  **Guardrail Check:** Verify that the 0.4% gain did not come at the expense of a guardrail metric (e.g., customer support tickets, refund rates, or long-term retention). If the gain is purely short-term conversion with no downstream value, the case for reverting is even stronger.

**Conclusion for the Briefing**
"We observed a real but trivial improvement. The data proves the new flow works slightly better, but not enough to matter financially. We recommend reverting to the simpler codebase to reduce technical debt and reallocating our experimentation capacity to higher-leverage areas of the funnel where double-digit improvements are still possible."
