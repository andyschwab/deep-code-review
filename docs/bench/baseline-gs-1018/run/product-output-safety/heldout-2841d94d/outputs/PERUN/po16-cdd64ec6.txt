**Risk Assessment: Facial-Recognition Access Control with Known Bias**

**1. Harm Inventory**
*   **Bias/Unfair Outcomes:** The system systematically denies entry to employees wearing religious head coverings. This constitutes discriminatory exclusion based on protected characteristics (religion), creating a hostile work environment and potential legal liability.
*   **Over-reliance/Automation Bias:** The current design assumes "high confidence" denial is sufficient for security, ignoring the known failure mode. Relying solely on the automated "deny" decision without a robust override invites false rejections of legitimate employees.
*   **Unsafe High-Stakes Automation:** Denying physical access to a workplace is a high-stakes action with immediate operational and safety consequences. Fully automating this decision, given the known error rate for specific groups, is unsafe.

**2. Measurement Status**
*   **Accuracy:** Aggregate accuracy metrics are **UNVERIFIED** for the subgroup of employees wearing religious head coverings. The vendor's locked weights prevent retraining to fix this.
*   **Bias Rate:** The specific false-rejection rate for this group is **UNVERIFIED** but acknowledged as non-zero based on pilot observations. No fabricated "fairness" score can be assigned.

**3. Management Controls & Operational Workarounds**
Given the inability to retrain the model and the imminent deadline, the following controls are mandatory to manage residual risk:

*   **Human-in-the-Loop Gate (Mandatory):** The system must **not** automatically and irreversibly deny entry. Any "no-match" result, particularly for individuals with head coverings, must trigger an immediate alert to a security officer for manual verification. The automated system should function as a *flagging* mechanism, not a final *denial* mechanism for this rollout phase.
*   **Explicit Override Protocol:** Establish a clear, rapid manual override process. Security personnel must have the authority and tools (e.g., visual ID check against badge photo) to grant access immediately upon verifying identity, bypassing the biometric failure.
*   **Transparency & Disclosure:** Affected employees must be informed *before* deployment that the system has known limitations regarding head coverings and instructed on the specific procedure to follow if denied entry (e.g., "Proceed to the manned booth"). This manages expectations and reduces frustration.
*   **Confidence Tiering (Operational):** Do not display raw confidence percentages (e.g., "87% match") to guards or users, as this implies false precision. Instead, categorize outputs as "Verified Match," "Potential Match (Review Required)," or "No Match (Review Required)." Treat any non-verified output as a prompt for human review.

**4. Governance & Next Steps**
*   **Owner:** The Facilities Project Lead owns this residual risk.
*   **Legal Routing:** The discriminatory impact of denying entry based on religious attire requires immediate review by Legal Counsel to assess compliance with employment and civil rights laws. This assessment does not certify compliance; it flags the duty for legal determination.
*   **Vendor Engagement:** Document the limitation formally and demand a timeline for a model update or patch that addresses this bias. The current locked state is a critical vendor risk.
*   **Monitoring:** Log all manual overrides. If the volume of overrides for head-covering employees exceeds a trivial threshold, the system's utility is compromised, and a non-biometric fallback (e.g., badge-only entry with heightened visual scrutiny) should be considered for those specific users until the model is updated.

**Conclusion:** Shipping this feature with automatic denial enabled for unmatched faces is unsafe due to known bias. The only viable path for the one-week deadline is to degrade the automation to a "decision support" role, ensuring a human always makes the final access decision for any flagged individual.

*Note: This assessment identifies risks and controls; it does not certify the system as safe or compliant. Legal counsel must validate the regulatory implications of the identified bias.*
