**The Call**
Reject the assumption that digitization alone creates a new copyright barrier to your current model; your primary defense is that faithful reproductions of public domain records remain public domain under US law. However, you face a material risk if archives assert rights over *compilations* (selection/arrangement) or if your sourcing agreements contractually restrict commercial use. Do not expand to international records until a licensed attorney reviews your specific data sourcing agreements, as foreign jurisdictions (e.g., EU) recognize "database rights" that do not exist in the US.

**The Numbers**
*Litigation Risk Exposure vs. Cash Reserves*
*   **Formula:** Potential Legal Cost = (Hourly Rate × Hours) + Settlement/Judgment.
*   **User Figures:** Cash Reserves = $150,000; Monthly Revenue = $30,000.
*   **Assumptions (ASSUMED):**
    *   *Low:* Specialized IP litigation settles early (20 hours @ $600/hr) = $12,000.
    *   *Base:* Defense through summary judgment (100 hours @ $600/hr) = $60,000.
    *   *High:* Full trial or injunctive relief stopping operations (300+ hours @ $600/hr) = $180,000+.
*   **Result:** A "High" scenario exceeds your $150,000 savings, threatening business continuity even if you ultimately win on merit.

**What Flips It, and the Trigger**
The single input that changes the call from "defensible" to "existential threat" is the presence of a **signed licensing agreement** with an archive that includes a "no commercial redistribution" clause.
*   **Trigger Value:** If any current data source requires a click-through or signed contract restricting commercial use, your "public domain" defense is waived by contract law.
*   **Stop/Pivot Trigger:** Immediately halt ingestion of any new archive collection until the specific terms of access are reviewed. If an archive demands a fee for "digital access" tied to a restrictive license, consider pivoting to a model where users pay for *search/tools* rather than *access to the raw image*, or source only from archives with explicit open-data policies.
*   **Rule of Thumb:** In the US, sweat of the brow (effort of scanning) does not grant copyright (*Bridgeman Art Library v. Corel Corp.*), but contractual terms override default copyright status.
*   **This Week's Step:** If you have signed agreements or click-through logs for your current 3,000 subscribers' data sources, gather them now; if none exist, document the URL and "Terms of Use" snapshot for every archive currently scraped.

Specifically, ask: "Do our current sourcing agreements contain contractual restrictions that waive our *Bridgeman* defense, and how do EU Database Rights (Directive 96/9/EC) impact our plan to ingest European records?"
