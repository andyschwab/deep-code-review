*   **Federal Level:** Holding customer funds for transmission or as a store of value generally avoids a full "banking charter" if you do not take deposits insured by the FDIC or make loans. However, profiting from the "float" (interest) often pierces the veil of a neutral escrow agent.
*   **State Level (The Binding Constraint):** Money transmission laws are state-specific. Most states define "money transmission" to include selling payment instruments or exchanging currency, but many explicitly include "receiving money for transmission" or holding funds on behalf of others.
    *   **New York:** The Department of Financial Services (NYDFS) requires a **BitLicense** or a **Money Transmitter License** for virtual currency or money transmission activities. New York is exceptionally strict; holding funds in a pooled account for third parties almost certainly requires a license. The "escrow agent" exemption is narrow and typically requires the funds to be held in a non-interest-bearing account or by a licensed attorney/bank. Earning interest suggests you are acting as a principal, not an agent.
    *   **California:** The Department of Financial Protection and Innovation (DFPI) requires a **Money Transmitter License** if you receive money for transmission. California law often exempts agents acting under the direct supervision of a licensed transmitter or banks, but if DomainSafe holds the funds directly in its own name (even in a pooled account), you are likely the transmitter. Earning interest complicates the "agency" defense.
