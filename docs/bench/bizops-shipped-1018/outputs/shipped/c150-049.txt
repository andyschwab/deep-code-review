**The Call**
Do not assert that the user owns the copyright, and do not assume the output is public domain by default; instead, explicitly claim ownership of the AI-generated reports in your Terms of Service (ToS) as "works made for hire" or via explicit assignment, because current US precedent suggests generic prompts yield uncopyrightable outputs that may otherwise remain in a legal limbo unusable for your aggregated data business.

**The Numbers & Legal Landscape**
There is no arithmetic formula here, but the "value" of your aggregated dataset depends entirely on contractual clarity.
*   **Current Precedent (US):** The US Copyright Office and federal courts (e.g., *Thaler v. Perlmutter*, 2023) have consistently held that works generated solely by AI without human authorship are not copyrightable. Crucially, guidance issued in 2023 clarifies that if a user's prompt is generic (e.g., "find patents for a better mouse trap"), the user has not contributed sufficient "creative control" to claim authorship of the AI's output.
*   **The Gap:** If the user cannot claim copyright due to lack of creative input, and you (the platform) are not explicitly named as the author/owner in a contract, the work risks being treated as unprotectable material (effectively public domain) or having unclear ownership status. This creates a fatal flaw for your plan to sell aggregated insights; you cannot license or sell exclusive rights to data you do not clearly own.
*   **Assumption (Labelled ASSUMED):** We assume your AI generates the bulk of the selection, arrangement, and analysis without significant human intervention from your team per query.

**What Flips It, and The Trigger**
*   **The Flip Input:** The specific wording of your Terms of Service. Without a clause stating that "All outputs generated by PatentFind are the sole property of PatentFind [Company Name]," you lack the chain of title required to monetize aggregated data.

*   **Rule of Thumb:** In AI services, "ownership follows the contract, not the code" when statutory copyright fails.
