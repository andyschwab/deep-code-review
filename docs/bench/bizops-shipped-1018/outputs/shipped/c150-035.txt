**What Flips It, and The Trigger:**
The determining factor is not the automation, but **who legally owns the funds** while they sit in the wallet and whether you are acting as an intermediary between two parties or merely providing a software tool where users transact directly.
*   **Trigger:** The moment a regulator or attorney determines you have "control" over the funds rather than just "custody via code," the money transmitter definition likely binds.
*   **Rule of Thumb:** If you hold private keys or can unilaterally reverse/redirect transactions, you likely have control. If the smart contract is immutable and users interact peer-to-peer through your interface, the argument for being a "software provider" strengthens.
*   **Step this week:** If your smart contract allows *any* admin key to pause or redirect funds, consider freezing new deposits until counsel reviews the code structure.

**Routing Note (Critical):**
Money transmission is a state-by-state determination in the US; there is no federal "automation exemption." You must ask a licensed fintech attorney these exact questions:
1.  Does our specific smart contract architecture qualify for the "agent of the payee" exemption in key states (e.g., NY, CA, TX), or does the temporary holding of funds constitute transmission?
2.  Does the decentralized nature of the contract allow us to argue we are selling software services rather than transmitting money, and does this hold up under the "control" test used by FinCEN and state regulators?
3.  Given our $200k reserve, is there a "no-action" letter pathway or a limited pilot exemption we can apply for to remain operational while seeking full licensure?
