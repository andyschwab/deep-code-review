**The Call:** Do not roll out the 10% success-based fee model to your existing user base yet; while paying vendors from grant funds is standard, charging a **contingency fee** (a percentage of the award) is explicitly prohibited by most major foundations and violates ethical standards for grant professionals, creating a high risk of disqualifying your clients from future funding.

**The Numbers & Constraints:**
Your proposed model yields $5,000 on a $50,000 grant. The foundation's 15% administrative cap allows up to $7,500 in total admin costs.
*   **Math:** $5,000 (your fee) < $7,500 (15% cap).
*   **Result:** Mathematically, the fee fits within the *percentage* limit.
*   **The Block:** The barrier is not the *amount* but the *structure*. Most institutional funders (community foundations, government agencies, large family trusts) distinguish between "allowable administrative costs" and "prohibited contingency fees." Even if the fee is under 15%, the act of tying vendor compensation directly to the award amount is often a disqualifier regardless of the total admin ratio.

**What Flips It, and The Trigger:**
The deciding factor is the specific language in a funder's "Prohibited Costs" or "Consultant Compensation" section.
*   **Trigger:** If a foundation's guidelines state "no contingency fees," "no percentage-based compensation," or require "fixed-fee contracts," your model breaches the rule immediately.
*   **Rule of Thumb:** Institutional funders generally view contingency fees as a conflict of interest, implying the grant writer might prioritize fundable projects over the non-profit's actual needs or inflate budget requests.
*   **Actionable Step:** This week, audit the guidelines of the top 10 foundations your 40 clients apply to. If even one explicitly bans contingency fees, you cannot scale this model nationwide without segmenting your pricing by funder type.

**Routing Note:**
You must consult a non-profit law specialist or a CPA with grant compliance expertise before drafting new contracts. The specific question to bring is: "Does a percentage-based success fee constitute a prohibited 'contingency fee' under the Uniform Guidance (2 CFR 200) and common private foundation standards, and does it jeopardize the non-profit's tax-exempt status or future eligibility?" This is a binding legal determination, not a math problem.

*Common Industry Practice:* The standard alternative is a "hybrid" model: a reduced monthly subscription (e.g., $250) plus a fixed "success bonus" (e.g., $1,000) upon award, rather than a floating percentage. This keeps the cost predictable and avoids the "contingency" label while still aligning incentives.
