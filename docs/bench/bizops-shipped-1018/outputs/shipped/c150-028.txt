**The Call**
Switch from straight-line to an accelerated method (likely Units-of-Production) immediately to match expenses with your actual high-utilization revenue; do not attempt this restructuring internally given the audit risk and tax implications—engage a CPA to formalize the change in accounting method.

**The Numbers**
*Current Model (Straight-Line):*
Formula: `Cost / 60 months`
Assumption: $1,000 asset cost (illustrative).
Result: $16.67 expense/month regardless of use.

*Proposed Model (Units-of-Production):*
Formula: `(Cost - Salvage) / Total Estimated Units × Units Used`
Inputs:
- Useful life: 18 months (vs. projected 60).
- Utilization: 3× original model.
- **ASSUMED**: If an item was expected to rent 10 times/year, it now rents 30 times/year.
Result: Expense recognition accelerates ~3.3× in early years compared to straight-line.

*Impact on Financials:*
- **Taxable Income**: Accelerated depreciation increases expenses now, lowering current taxable income and tax liability.
- **Cash Flow**: While depreciation is non-cash, the reduced tax bill improves actual cash flow.
- **Burn Rate**: Your $10k/month burn is unaffected by depreciation (non-cash), but your *reported* net loss will widen, which may concern investors looking at GAAP profitability despite the cash benefit.

**What Flips It, and the Trigger**
The binding constraint is **IRS Form 3115** requirements. You cannot simply "switch" methods on an internal spreadsheet; changing from straight-line to an accelerated method for tax purposes requires filing Form 3115 with the IRS to avoid penalties.
*Trigger*: If your audit occurs before filing this form, your financial statements will be flagged for inconsistency.
*Rule of Thumb*: If asset utilization varies significantly from the time-based estimate (as yours does, 18 vs. 60 months), usage-based depreciation is the accurate accounting principle.
*Step*: If your audit is within 90 days, consider Y: hire a CPA this week to prepare the Form 3115 and adjust the opening balance sheet.
