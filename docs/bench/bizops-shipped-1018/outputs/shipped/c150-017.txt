**The Numbers**
To calculate the customer's payback period (Years to Recoup), we use:
$$ \text{Payback Years} = \frac{\text{Customer CapEx}}{\text{Annual Energy Savings}} $$

*   **Customer CapEx:** €14,000 (Given).
*   **Annual Energy Savings:** This is the missing variable. It depends on the turbine's rated power (kW), the local electricity price (€/kWh), and the operating hours.

*Scenario A: Ideal 24-hour operation (Baseline)*
Let $E_{24}$ be the annual energy output in kWh.
$$ \text{Savings}_{24} = E_{24} \times \text{Electricity Price} $$

*Scenario B: 10-hour constraint (Current Reality)*
You stated output reduces by 60%. Therefore, output is 40% of the ideal.
$$ \text{Savings}_{10} = 0.40 \times \text{Savings}_{24} $$
$$ \text{Payback}_{10} = \frac{€14,000}{0.40 \times \text{Savings}_{24}} = 2.5 \times \text{Payback}_{24} $$

**Result:** The restriction **increases the payback period by 150% (2.5x)**.
*Illustrative Example (ASSUMED inputs for demonstration only):*
If a typical residential turbine saves €1,200/year in a 24-hour scenario (8.5% yield on €14k):
*   **Ideal Payback:** ~11.7 years.
*   **Restricted Payback:** ~29.2 years.
Most residential hardware warranties last 10–15 years. A 29-year payback renders the investment financially irrational for a homeowner.

**What Flips It, and the Trigger**
The single input that changes this call is the **effective electricity price** or **subsidy availability**.
*   **Trigger Value:** If the German feed-in tariff or avoided cost exceeds ~€0.45/kWh *and* the turbine efficiency is significantly higher than industry averages, the math might approach a 15-year payback.
*   **Rule of Thumb:** Residential energy hardware generally requires a <12 year payback to be viable without heavy subsidies.
*   **Step this week:** If the calculated payback exceeds 15 years using real local rates, consider pivoting sales messaging to "off-grid backup" value rather than "ROI," or halt residential pushes in noise-restricted municipalities until battery storage can shift the 10-hour generation to peak evening pricing.

**Unknown Variables & Seasonal Risks**
You must disclose these in sales materials to avoid misleading customers:
1.  **Winter Daylight in Northern Germany:** In December, daylight in Germany averages only 7–8 hours, not 10. This pushes the constraint further, potentially reducing output to <35% of the 24-hour ideal during peak heating demand months.
2.  **Wind Diurnal Patterns:** Wind speeds often drop during the day (thermal lulls) and rise at night. Restricting operation to daylight hours may coincidentally cut out the highest wind-speed windows, meaning the actual loss could be >60%, not exactly 60%.
3.  **Seasonal Demand Mismatch:** Home energy demand peaks in winter (dark months); your restricted production is lowest exactly when the customer needs power most, lowering the *value* of each kWh generated (if time-of-use pricing applies).
