**Lane R: Regulation-Dependent (Route to Counsel)**

Your question regarding specific HIPAA waivers, the sufficiency of broad versus specific consent, and the exact mechanisms required for transferring medical history to pharmaceutical sponsors is a **Lane R** matter. The correct answer depends entirely on a legal determination of your entity's status under the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) and potentially other regulations like the Common Rule or FTC Health Breach Notification Rule.

**The Regime:**
You are operating at the intersection of **HIPAA Privacy and Security Rules** and potentially **FDA regulations** regarding human subjects research. The core legal ambiguity you face is whether TrialMatch acts as a "Covered Entity" (like a clinic), a "Business Associate" (servicing a Covered Entity), or a direct-to-consumer health app that may fall outside HIPAA but inside FTC jurisdiction. This classification dictates entirely different compliance obligations.

**Why This Must Be Routed:**
1.  **De-identification Standards:** You mentioned stripping "obvious identifiers." Under HIPAA, valid de-identification requires adhering to one of two strict statutory methods: the "Expert Determination" method (a statistician certifies risk is very small) or the "Safe Harbor" method (removing 18 specific data points). Medical history often contains quasi-identifiers that, when combined, can re-identify a patient. A lawyer or privacy expert must audit your specific data fields to confirm if your current stripping process meets the federal "Safe Harbor" list or if you need an expert certification.
2.  **Consent Mechanisms:** There is no generic "HIPAA waiver" you can self-apply.
    *   If you are a Covered Entity or Business Associate, sharing Protected Health Information (PHI) for research generally requires either specific **Authorization** from the patient for each study or an **Institutional Review Board (IRB)** waiver of authorization.
    *   "Broad consent" is a specific legal construct under the Revised Common Rule (for federally funded research) but its applicability to commercial pharmaceutical trials via a digital platform is a complex legal question.
    *   If you are *not* covered by HIPAA, state privacy laws (like CCPA/CPRA in California) and FTC rules regarding "unfair or deceptive practices" still govern how you represent your data usage to patients. Misrepresenting a "broad consent" form as covering all future commercial uses could trigger enforcement actions.

**Action Plan Before Your Professional Call:**
Do not share further data with sponsors until counsel reviews your flow. To prepare for your attorney:
*   **Document Your Data Flow:** Map exactly what data fields leave your platform and what remains.
*   **Gather Current Forms:** Bring your current Terms of Service, Privacy Policy, and any consent forms patients sign upon registration.
*   **Define Sponsor Relationships:** Clarify if sponsors are sending you queries (you search) or if you are pushing profiles (you disclose), as this impacts the "minimum necessary" rule analysis.
*   **Identify State Jurisdictions:** List the states where your 5,000 patients reside, as state laws may be stricter than federal HIPAA.
