**Regime Identification**
The relevant regimes here are:
1.  **Federal Tax Law (IRC):** Specifically rules governing "Leasehold Improvements," "Qualified Improvement Property" (QIP), and the distinction between personal property and real property fixtures.
2.  **GAAP (Financial Accounting):** Rules regarding asset capitalization and amortization over the shorter of the useful life or the lease term.
3.  **Property Law:** Determinations of who owns the asset (the landlord or the tenant) at the end of the lease, which dictates who claims the depreciation.

**Actionable Preparation for Professional Consultation**
To get a definitive answer from your tax counsel or CPA, prepare the following specific facts and documents. The professional will need these to apply the law to your situation:

*   **The Lease Agreement:** Specifically the clauses defining "alterations," "improvements," and "surrender of premises." Does the lease require the glass to remain, or does it require removal? Does title transfer to the landlord immediately upon installation?
*   **Installation Contracts:** Who paid the installer? Was the glass purchased as equipment (personal property) or as part of a construction contract (real property)?
*   **Removability Analysis:** Can the glass be removed without material damage to the building? This is often a key test for whether an asset is a "fixture" (real property) or "tangible personal property."
*   **Current Depreciation Method:** How are your clients currently filing? (e.g., MACRS 39-year straight-line, 15-year QIP, or bonus depreciation).
*   **The "Mismatch" Detail:** Clarify if the 7-year term includes renewal options that are "reasonably certain" to be exercised, as tax law often looks at the expected term, not just the initial lease period.

**Why This Cannot Be Solved with Arithmetic (Lane A)**
While you can calculate the *mathematical difference* in expense recognition between a 7-year and 20-year schedule (e.g., $2.2M / 7 vs. $2.2M / 20), the *permission* to use either number is a legal question.
*   If the glass is deemed a **fixture** owned by the landlord, the tenant may not depreciate it at all, but rather amortize leasehold improvements over the lease term.
*   If deemed **personal property** owned by the tenant, it might be depreciated over its class life (potentially 7 years under MACRS for certain assets, or 20 years if different), regardless of the lease term, unless the lease dictates a shorter recovery period due to abandonment rules.
*   **Ownership Structure:** If the units are removed, the tenant likely retains ownership. If they stay, ownership may transfer to the landlord, triggering a "disposition" event for the tenant and a new basis for the landlord. This is a legal fact pattern, not a calculation.
