**Regulatory Triggers and the "Custody" Ambiguity**
Money transmitter laws generally turn on whether an entity accepts "value" from one person and transmits it to another. In your inheritance model, the critical legal friction point is whether holding the *mechanism* to transfer (the encrypted key share or smart contract logic) constitutes "accepting value" or exercising "custody."

*   **The Trigger:** Most states define transmission broadly. If your service verifies a death certificate and then *executes* the movement of assets, you are likely facilitating a transfer. Even if you never hold the raw private key in a hot wallet, having the contractual right or technical ability to initiate the transfer upon an event (death) often qualifies as "control" or "custody" under regulators' eyes.
*   **The "Non-Custodial" Fallacy:** Many builders assume that if users hold one key share and the platform holds another (MPC or multi-sig), the platform is not a custodian. Regulators increasingly disagree if the platform is a necessary party to *any* transaction. If your system is the only way the beneficiary can access the funds after death, you are functionally a transmitter.
*   **Unknown Legal Interpretations:** There is no uniform federal definition of "custody" for crypto inheritance. Some states may view the automated execution via smart contract as a software tool (exempt), while others view the platform's role in verifying the death certificate and triggering the contract as an active transmission service (regulated). This inconsistency is the primary risk.

**State Variability and Strict Jurisdictions**
Money transmitter licensing is state-by-state. There is no single "strictest" list that remains static, but historically, **New York** (BitLicense regime), **California**, and **Texas** have aggressive interpretations of what constitutes transmission and custody.
*   **New York:** The BitLicense framework is comprehensive. If you have even one customer in NY, you likely need a license or a specific exemption opinion, which is costly and time-consuming to obtain.
*   **Surety Bonds:** If classified as a transmitter, states typically require surety bonds ranging from $50,000 to $1,000,000+ depending on volume and risk. You cannot calculate this obligation without a legal determination of your status in each target state.
