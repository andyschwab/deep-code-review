**Lane R: Regulatory Boundary (UPL & Jurisdiction)**
The issue is not arithmetic; it is the legal definition of "practicing law." Both California and New York strictly prohibit non-lawyers from providing legal advice or services that affect legal rights.
*   **The Rule:** Courts and bar associations generally look at whether the service requires legal judgment, is relied upon for legal decisions, and is marketed as a substitute for an attorney. Charging a fee strengthens the argument that you are selling a legal service.
*   **The Risk:** If your AI identifies "legal risks" or suggests specific contract language changes, regulators may view this as legal advice. The 5% refund rate due to errors significantly increases your liability exposure; a false negative (missing a risk) could cause a user financial harm, leading to negligence claims or bar complaints.
*   **State Specifics:**
    *   **California:** The State Bar actively pursues UPL cases involving technology that mimics attorney work. They focus on whether the tool creates an attorney-client relationship or implies legal expertise.
    *   **New York:** New York Judiciary Law § 478 prohibits practicing law without admission. Precedent suggests that providing specific legal analysis, even via software, can violate this if it replaces the need for a lawyer's judgment.
*   **Unknown Preceds:** There is no settled case law specifically defining the threshold where an AI contract reviewer crosses from "legal information" (permissible) to "legal advice" (prohibited) for a flat fee. This is a binding legal determination you cannot make yourself.
