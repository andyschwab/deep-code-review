**Implications of a 12-Year Depreciation Schedule (Matching Mechanical Life)**

*   **Cash Flow & Runway:** This approach minimizes your annual depreciation expense, maximizing your reported net income in the short term. With an 18-month runway, this preserves the appearance of profitability and extends your cash runway on paper, which may be critical for investor relations or loan covenants.
*   **Tax Liability:** You will pay higher taxes in the early years because your taxable income is artificially inflated by the low depreciation deduction.
*   **The Risk:** This creates a "cliff" risk. If the software architecture becomes obsolete or non-compliant in Year 3, forcing a replacement or major capitalization of new development costs, you may face a massive asset impairment charge. You would still be carrying the old hardware value on your books while the unit is functionally obsolete. This could lead to a sudden, large write-off that devastates your financial statements in a single year.

**Implications of a 3-Year Depreciation Schedule (Matching Software Cycle)**

*   **Cash Flow & Runway:** This approach maximizes your annual depreciation expense, significantly reducing your reported net income. Given your current 18-month runway and $2.5M cash position, reporting deep losses immediately could alarm investors or lenders who focus on GAAP profitability, potentially threatening future fundraising.
*   **Tax Liability:** You generate large tax deductions immediately, reducing or eliminating your tax liability for the first three years. This preserves actual cash in the bank, even if your "book income" looks poor.
*   **The Reality Check:** This method aligns your financial reporting with the economic reality of the product's useful life. Since the unit cannot operate compliantly without the software update, the asset's true life is likely tied to the software cycle. This avoids the future impairment shock but accelerates the pain to your current P&L.

**The Hybrid Reality & Professional Necessity**

The core complexity lies in the fact that the IRS and GAAP often treat hardware and software differently.
1.  **Component Depreciation:** A professional may advise separating the asset into two components: the mechanical unit (12 years) and the software license/embedded system (3 years). This requires a detailed cost allocation study to determine what portion of the $120,000 is hardware vs. software.
2.  **Section 179 & Bonus Depreciation:** Current US tax codes often allow for immediate expensing (100% deduction) of qualified equipment in the year it is placed in service. If RoboRx units qualify, you might be able to deduct the entire cost immediately for tax purposes (saving cash now) while depreciating over 12 years for your internal books (looking profitable to investors). This creates a "deferred tax liability" but offers the best of both worlds: cash preservation and clean financial statements.
3.  **Capitalization vs. Expense:** The nature of the "major architectural update" every 3 years matters. Is it a new asset purchase or a maintenance expense? A CPA must determine if these future updates are capitalized (added to the asset basis) or expensed immediately.

**Conclusion**

Choosing the wrong schedule could lead to IRS penalties for underpayment of tax or a sudden financial collapse due to asset impairment. Given your limited runway, the potential to utilize immediate expensing provisions (like Bonus Depreciation) to save cash while managing book income is a sophisticated strategy that demands professional execution.
