**Current US Copyright Office Stance**
The US Copyright Office currently maintains that works generated entirely by artificial intelligence without human authorship cannot be copyrighted. In recent guidance and decisions (such as the "Zarya of the Dawn" registration and the "Théâtre D'opéra Spatial" case), the Office has clarified that copyright protects only the human-authored elements of a work. If an AI generates the vast majority of a text (e.g., 90%+) and the human contribution is minimal (e.g., less than 10% or limited to simple prompts), the Office is likely to determine that the work lacks the necessary "human authorship" to qualify for copyright protection. Consequently, such works may fall immediately into the public domain upon creation, meaning no one—not the user, nor your company—holds exclusive rights to them.

**Ownership Risks for Your Users**
1.  **No Exclusive Rights:** Users who rely on your tool for the bulk of the drafting may find they own nothing exclusively. Competitors could legally copy, distribute, or commercialize their patent drafts without infringement liability.
2.  **Invalidation of Claims:** If a user attempts to register the copyright and the AI contribution is later discovered during litigation or examination, the registration could be invalidated, potentially weakening their overall intellectual property position.
3.  **False Sense of Security:** Users may operate under the mistaken belief that they possess a protected asset, leading to strategic business errors based on non-existent exclusivity.
4.  **Liability Exposure for InventAssist:** If your marketing implies users gain full ownership or copyrightable material when they legally do not, you face risks of false advertising claims or breach of contract suits when users fail to secure the rights they expected.

**Critical Questions for an Intellectual Property Attorney**
Before updating your Terms of Service, an attorney must address the following specific questions regarding thresholds and liability:

1.  **Quantitative vs. Qualitative Threshold:** Does the Copyright Office apply a strict percentage-based threshold (e.g., "less than 15% human input voids protection"), or is the determination purely qualitative based on the "creative choices" made by the human?
2.  **Definition of "Human Authorship" in Patent Drafting:** Specifically for patent applications, does the act of providing technical facts, reviewing outputs, or selecting specific claims constitute sufficient human authorship to copyright the *textual expression* of the draft, even if the AI wrote the sentences?
3.  **Work-for-Hire and Service Terms:** Can contractual terms between InventAssist and the user effectively transfer rights or define ownership in a way that mitigates the statutory lack of copyright, or are such clauses unenforceable against the public domain status?
4.  **Disclosure Obligations:** What are the legal requirements for disclosing AI usage in the generated content to the USPTO or Copyright Office, and does failure to disclose constitute fraud?
5.  **Indemnification Scope:** Should InventAssist explicitly disclaim any warranty of copyrightability in its ToS, and must we indemnify users against third-party copying of AI-generated drafts?

*Note: This analysis reflects current general guidance as of early 2024; copyright law regarding AI is rapidly evolving through ongoing litigation and new policy statements.*
