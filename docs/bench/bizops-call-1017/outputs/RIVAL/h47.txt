Your current unit economics are indeed unsustainable under the assumption that every client triggers a fresh, full-scope $80,000 audit. At $2.00 per vote, you require 40,000 votes just to cover the audit cost, leaving zero margin for operations, infrastructure, or profit before reaching the break-even point. For a typical 30,000-shareholder client, you incur a $20,000 loss per event before any other expenses. This confirms your fear: the fixed compliance cost erodes margins completely for small to mid-sized companies under your current model.

However, the premise that every election requires a *new* $80,000 audit is likely the flaw, not the business model itself. In software security and blockchain governance, audits generally validate the *codebase* and *smart contract logic*, not each individual instance of deployment or execution. Unless regulations explicitly mandate a unique audit for every single shareholder meeting regardless of code changes, you are likely over-auditing.

Here are three viable pathways to profitability without restricting yourself solely to enterprise clients:

**1. Shift from "Per-Event" to "Per-Version" Auditing**
Smart contract audits typically certify a specific version of the code. If your core voting logic remains unchanged between client elections, you should not be paying for a full re-audit. Instead, pay for a full audit once per major software version. For subsequent clients using the same verified code, you only need lighter, cheaper "differential audits" or "configuration reviews" to ensure the deployment parameters (e.g., shareholder list, date) are correct. This could reduce subsequent verification costs from $80,000 to perhaps $5,000–$10,000, making the 30,000-vote client highly profitable.

**2. Implement a Tiered Compliance Model**
Regulatory requirements often scale with the size or public status of the corporation. Publicly traded companies may require rigorous third-party audits, but private companies, startups, or smaller cooperatives might accept internal cryptographic proofs, multi-sig verification, or lighter-weight security attestations. Segment your market:
*   **Enterprise/Public:** Full third-party audit (cost passed through or absorbed in higher volume).
*   **SMB/Private:** "Lite" verification using automated testing suites and limited scope review, drastically lowering the fixed cost barrier.

**3. Restructure Pricing and Cost Allocation**
If the audit is legally required per event for all clients, your pricing model must change. A pure variable cost ($2/vote) fails when fixed costs are this high.
*   **Base Fee + Variable:** Charge a $100,000 base engagement fee (covering the audit) plus $1.00 per vote. This ensures the audit is covered upfront.
*   **Audit Amortization:** If the audit validity lasts 12 months, aggregate all clients within that window. The first client pays the bulk of the audit; subsequent clients within the same period pay a fraction for "maintenance verification."

**Conclusion on Viability**
Your business model is viable only if you decouple the audit cost from a per-client, per-event basis. If you are forced to pay $80,000 for every single client regardless of code reuse, you must indeed target only massive enterprises (100,000+ voters) or raise prices significantly. However, by treating the audit as a product development cost (per version) rather than a COGS (per transaction), you can serve the mid-market profitably. Immediate action should involve clarifying with your auditors and legal counsel whether "code reuse" allows for reduced-scope verification for subsequent deployments.
