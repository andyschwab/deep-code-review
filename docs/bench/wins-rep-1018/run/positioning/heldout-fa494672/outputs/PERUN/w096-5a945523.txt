Your draft positioning leans too heavily on "safety" as the primary hook, which often feels like a compliance cost to fleet managers focused on monthly P&L. While safety is a valuable byproduct, your strongest wedge is the immediate, quantifiable financial bleed caused by reactive repairs. Fleet managers accustomed to fixing things only when they break respond to cash flow and uptime, not abstract risk mitigation.

Here is the committed, stress-tested positioning statement based on your data:

**For fleet managers in Mexico City who rely on reactive repairs at local shops like Taller Rodriguez when buses stall, BusGuard is a predictive maintenance subscription that eliminates unplanned downtime and slashes repair costs; unlike waiting for breakdowns that cost 8,000 pesos and 4 hours per incident, BusGuard prevents major failures for 1,000 pesos per bus per month with near-zero operational disruption.**

**Headline:** Stop paying 8,000 pesos to fix what you could have prevented for 1,000.

**Why this wins:**
This statement forces a direct comparison between the status quo (Taller Rodriguez, stalling, high cost) and your solution. It specifically names the alternative behavior ("waiting for breakdowns") rather than a vague "competition." It quantifies the pain (8,000 pesos/4 hours) using your actual data, making the alternative expensive and visible. It frames the differentiator not as "better technology" but as a specific economic swap: trading a catastrophic, irregular expense for a predictable, low-cost subscription.

**Answering your alternatives:**
*   **Safety Imperative:** If you lead with safety, a skeptical manager hears "spend money to avoid a maybe-accident." They will defer this purchase until budget allows.
*   **Financial Tool:** If you lead with finance, you hear "spend 1,000 to save 8,000 and recover 4 hours of revenue." This is an immediate "yes" because it solves a current, bleeding wound. Safety becomes the closing argument, not the opening hook.

**Riskiest Assumption & Test:**
The riskiest assumption is that fleet managers trust sensor data over their established relationship with local mechanics like Taller Rodriguez. They may believe their mechanics can "hear" problems before they happen.
*   **Test:** Run a pilot with one manager where you predict a specific failure 48 hours in advance. Have them take the bus to Taller Rodriguez *before* it breaks to verify the fault. If the mechanic confirms the issue, trust shifts from "gut feeling" to "proven prediction."
