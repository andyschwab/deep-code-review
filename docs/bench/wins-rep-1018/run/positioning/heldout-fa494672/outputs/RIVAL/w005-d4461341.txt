Your current value proposition fails because it invites a direct price comparison ($600 vs. $150) rather than a total cost-of-care comparison. Insurers are rational economic actors; if they view your product as a "luxury" or "diagnostic tool" competing with blood panels, they will always choose the cheaper upfront option. To win, you must reframe the category from "Genetic Testing" to "Diagnostic Odyssey Termination."

Here is the stress test of your current argument and the missing framing required to shift the conversation:

**1. The Category Error**
*Current Frame:* You are positioning as a "genetic testing kit."
*The Flaw:* In the insurer's mental model, this competes with other tests. A $600 test will always lose to a $150 test if the outcome is viewed simply as "getting data."
*Required Shift:* You must position GeneRare as a **care pathway optimization tool**. The competitor is not the $150 blood panel; the competitor is the **$30,000 diagnostic odyssey**. Your product is not a test; it is an insurance policy against four years of wasteful spending.

**2. The Missing Economic Bridge**
*Current Frame:* "Our test is definitive in two weeks."
*The Flaw:* "Definitive" is a clinical benefit, not a financial one. Insurers do not pay for certainty; they pay for reduced liability and lower claims costs. You have stated the odyssey costs $30,000, but you haven't explicitly modeled the **Net Present Value (NPV)** of your intervention.
*Required Shift:* You must present a unit economics model:
*   **Status Quo:** $150 (initial test) + $30,000 (4.5 years of specialist visits, incorrect medications, ER visits, additional imaging) = **$30,150 total cost.**
*   **GeneRare Scenario:** $600 (your test) + $0 (odyssey avoided) = **$600 total cost.**
*   **The Argument:** "Covering GeneRare saves the payer $29,550 per patient immediately." You must stop selling a $600 expense and start selling a $29,550 saving.

**3. The Time-Value Gap**
*Current Frame:* "Averages 4.5 years."
*The Flaw:* Insurers care about short-term budget cycles. A saving realized over 4.5 years feels abstract and risky if the member switches plans in year two.
*Required Shift:* You need data on **cost concentration**. What percentage of that $30,000 occurs in the *first 12 months*? If you can prove that $15,000 of waste happens in year one alone, your $600 investment pays for itself in months, not years. This addresses the insurer's fear of paying upfront for long-term gains they might not capture.

**4. The Risk of Inaction**
*Current Frame:* Implicitly suggests the alternative is "waiting for a better test."
*The Flaw:* It ignores the cost of *wrong* treatments. Inconclusive blood panels often lead to trial-and-error prescribing.
*Required Shift:* Highlight the cost of **adverse events** and **ineffective therapies**. If a patient takes the wrong medication for two years because of an inconclusive blood panel, the drug costs and side-effect management are direct line items for the insurer. GeneRare prevents this specific waste.

**Missing Facts to Gather Immediately:**
To make this argument stick, you must supplement your narrative with:
1.  **Cost Concentration Data:** Break down the $30,000 by year. Prove the bulk of the waste happens early.
2.  **Intervention Costs:** Quantify the average cost of unnecessary prescriptions or procedures triggered by inconclusive results.
3.  **Member Retention Logic:** Address the "churn" objection. Show that early diagnosis improves member health outcomes, reducing long-term churn and increasing the lifetime value of the member to the payer.

**Revised Core Message:**
"Do not compare our $600 fee to a $150 blood panel. Compare our $600 fee to the $30,000 your system currently spends on every undiagnosed rare disease patient. We are not selling a test; we are selling the elimination of a four-year, high-cost diagnostic odyssey."

*Note: Ensure your pilot data includes a control group showing actual cost avoidance, not just time-to-diagnosis, to validate the $30,000 figure for Canadian payers specifically.*
*Without localized cost data, US-based statistics may be dismissed as irrelevant by Canadian provincial health plans.*
