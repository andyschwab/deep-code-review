### Recommendation: Prioritize the Simplified Medication Tracker

Based on the conflicting evidence, the team should prioritize the **Simplified Medication Tracker** for the Q4 roadmap.

**Rationale:**
While coordination errors affect more families (12 out of 20), missed medication doses drive the most severe business and user outcome: **40% of all emergency calls**. In product discovery, severity of consequence often outweighs frequency of occurrence when resources are constrained. A medication error leads directly to physical harm and emergency intervention, whereas a coordination error typically results in logistical friction. Furthermore, your identified risk regarding elderly users' ability to operate a complex dashboard suggests high usability risk for the comprehensive solution. A simplified tracker addresses the highest-severity pain point with lower implementation risk and a clearer path to immediate adoption by the primary user (the elderly individual).

### Rapid Experiments to Validate Severity & Usability

Before committing to full development, run these two low-fidelity experiments to confirm that medication errors are the critical bottleneck and that the simplified approach is usable:

**1. The "Wizard of Oz" Coordination Test (Validates Dashboard Necessity)**
*   **Goal:** Determine if real-time dashboard updates actually prevent coordination errors or if simple communication suffices.
*   **Method:** Recruit 5 families struggling with shifts. Instead of building the dashboard, manually act as the "system." When a caregiver needs to swap a shift, they text you. You manually update a shared Google Doc and notify the replacement caregiver via SMS.
*   **Success Metric:** If manual coordination resolves 80% of shift conflicts without escalation, a complex real-time dashboard may be over-engineering the solution. If failures persist despite manual intervention, the problem is behavioral, not technological.

**2. The Paper Prototype Usability Sprint (Validates Elderly Accessibility)**
*   **Goal:** Test if elderly users can operate the proposed interface without caregiver assistance during a simulated crisis.
*   **Method:** Create a clickable paper prototype or a basic Figma mockup of the medication tracker interface. Sit with 5 elderly users (without their caregivers present). Simulate a "missed dose" alert scenario. Ask them to acknowledge the alert and mark the dose as taken.
*   **Success Metric:** 4 out of 5 users must complete the task within 60 seconds with zero prompts. If users fail, the interface is too complex, reinforcing the need for extreme simplification or a non-digital fallback (e.g., automated voice calls).

**3. The "Root Cause" Audit (Validates Problem Severity)**
*   **Goal:** Confirm that missed doses are truly the cause of emergency calls, not just a correlation.
*   **Method:** Review the logs of the last 20 emergency calls attributed to missed medications. Interview the responding family members to ask: "What specifically happened leading up to the call?"
*   **Success Metric:** If >70% of incidents confirm that a lack of reminder (rather than lack of coordination) caused the miss, the tracker is validated. If the data reveals that caregivers *knew* the dose was missed but couldn't coordinate a response in time, the root cause is actually coordination, pivoting the priority back to the dashboard.

Execute these experiments within one week. The data will provide the Product Council with behavioral evidence rather than assumptions, ensuring the Q4 roadmap targets the highest-leverage problem.

*Note: Ensure experiments focus on observing actual behavior rather than asking users what they "would" do.*
