### Assessment: Hardware Upgrade vs. Process Failure

**Current Evidence Analysis**
The available data indicates a correlation between sensor failures and spoilage events, but it does not yet confirm causation. Four formal complaints involving $5,000 losses each represent a significant financial risk ($20,000 total in one month). However, attributing these failures solely to low-frequency logging is an unverified assumption.

The pattern of "failure to predict" suggests two distinct possibilities:
1.  **Hardware Limitation:** The current sensors sample data too infrequently to capture rapid temperature spikes during loading or door openings, creating "blind spots."
2.  **Process Failure:** Drivers are bypassing calibration protocols or improper loading techniques are causing immediate thermal shocks that even high-frequency sensors might flag too late without intervention.

Without distinguishing between these root causes, purchasing new hardware risks solving the wrong problem. If the issue is driver behavior, high-frequency sensors will merely record the errors more granularly without preventing the $5,000 losses.

**Critical Data Gaps**
Before the Tuesday meeting with the Operations Director and Finance team, you must collect the following specific data points to validate the root cause:

*   **Timestamp Correlation:** Cross-reference the exact time of spoilage detection against driver loading logs. Did the temperature deviation occur *during* the loading window (suggesting process error) or *during transit* (suggesting monitoring gaps)?
*   **Calibration Audit Records:** Review the last 30 days of calibration logs for the specific trucks involved in the four complaints. Were these units calibrated according to schedule? If logs are missing or show deviations, the root cause is likely procedural.
*   **Granular Incident Reports:** Interview the drivers involved in the four incidents. Ask specifically about their loading procedures and any anomalies they noticed. Look for patterns in their responses regarding time pressure or equipment handling.
*   **Baseline Variance Analysis:** Analyze historical data from the current sensors for the affected routes. Are there subtle fluctuations prior to the spoilage event that were ignored? If current data shows no warning signs whatsoever, a hardware blind spot is more likely. If data shows warnings that were not acted upon, the failure is operational.

**Recommendation for Tuesday's Meeting**
Do not commit to the full capital expenditure for the hardware upgrade yet. The evidence currently supports a **hybrid investigation approach**:

1.  **Immediate Action:** Implement a mandatory, audited calibration check for all Ontario fleet vehicles effective immediately. This costs negligible capital but addresses the process failure hypothesis.
2.  **Pilot Test:** Deploy a small batch (e.g., 5–10 units) of the proposed high-frequency sensors to the specific routes with complaints. Compare their data against the current sensors and the new calibration adherence rates.
3.  **Decision Threshold:** Commit to the full upgrade only if the pilot sensors detect temperature spikes that the current system misses *and* those spikes correlate directly to spoilage events despite strict calibration adherence.

This approach protects the company from unnecessary CAPEX if the issue is behavioral, while gathering the concrete proof needed to justify the investment if the issue is indeed technological.

*Note: Ensure the Finance team sees the potential $20k/month recurring loss as the baseline cost of inaction while testing.*
*Note: Driver interviews should be framed as fact-finding, not disciplinary, to ensure honest feedback.*
