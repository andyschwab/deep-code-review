### Risk vs. Adoption Analysis: Telehealth Video Feature

**Critical Evidence Gaps**
Your current evidence relies entirely on stated preferences ("we want video") rather than observed behavior. The claim that video reduces travel time by four hours is a hypothetical benefit, not a validated outcome. You lack proof that:
1.  **Infrastructure Reality:** Heavy compression actually yields diagnostic-quality video on the specific networks available in these Rajasthan villages during peak clinic hours.
2.  **Operational Feasibility:** Staff have the physical privacy required for video consultations without violating patient dignity or local cultural norms.
3.  **Willingness to Adapt:** Clinics will change their physical workflow (scheduling, room allocation) to accommodate video, which is harder than simply sending a text.

**Risk Breakdown**

*   **Feasibility Risk (High):** Your assumption that "heavy compression" solves low bandwidth is untested. If video freezes during a critical diagnostic moment, trust in the entire platform may collapse. Unlike text, failed video calls are highly visible and frustrating.
*   **Usability/Context Risk (High):** Without private rooms, staff may avoid using the feature entirely due to embarrassment or noise interference. If the environment isn't conducive to video, the feature becomes shelf-ware regardless of technical performance.
*   **Value Risk (Medium):** While travel time savings are attractive, you haven't verified if "speed of diagnosis" is the primary bottleneck. If the delay is actually caused by lab result turnaround or medication supply chains, faster video consultation won't move the needle on patient outcomes.
*   **Go-to-Market Risk (Medium):** Rolling out a feature that requires physical space changes (creating private rooms) requires a different adoption strategy than a software-only update. You have no plan for guiding clinics through this operational shift.

**Potential Adoption Scenarios**

*   **Best Case (Validated Assumptions):** Networks handle compressed streams; clinics designate private corners. Adoption could reach 60-70% of the 450 users quickly, as the value proposition (4 hours saved) is tangible.
*   **Likely Case (Partial Friction):** Video works intermittently, causing frustration. Privacy issues limit usage to only the most urgent cases. Adoption stalls at 20-30%, with high support costs for troubleshooting connection issues.
*   **Worst Case (Failed Rollout):** Bandwidth is too unstable for any usable video, or cultural/privacy barriers prevent usage entirely. The feature is launched but unused, wasting Q3 engineering budget and delaying critical optimizations to the messaging queue that users already rely on.

**Recommendation for Board Decision**
Do not allocate the full Q3 budget to build this feature yet. The gap between "customers asking for it" and "customers successfully using it in their specific context" is too wide. The risk of building a technically functional product that fails operationally (due to privacy or bandwidth) is significant.

**Immediate Next Steps to Close Gaps**
Before committing engineering resources, conduct two low-effort tests:
1.  **Technical Probe:** Have engineers run bandwidth simulations using actual network data from the three specific clinic locations during operating hours, not just general rural averages.
2.  **Contextual Observation:** Visit one clinic to observe patient flow. Ask staff to simulate a video call using a consumer app (like WhatsApp) to see where they would physically sit and what barriers arise regarding privacy and noise.

If these tests reveal unstable networks or no viable private space, optimizing the existing messaging queue is the safer, higher-value investment for Q3.

*Note: This analysis highlights that stated customer desires often mask hidden operational barriers. Validating the environment is as critical as validating the code.*
