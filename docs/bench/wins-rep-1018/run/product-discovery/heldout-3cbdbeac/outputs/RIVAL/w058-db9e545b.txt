### Assessment: Biometric Login Accessibility vs. Security for Oakwood Medical Group

**Executive Summary**
While adding biometric login addresses the 30% volume of password-reset support tickets and enhances perceived security, proceeding without data on user device capabilities poses a critical accessibility risk. With 40% of the patient base being older adults—who statistically have lower smartphone adoption rates and higher reliance on desktop computers—implementing biometrics as a primary or exclusive login method could inadvertently lock out approximately 6,000 active patients.

**Identified Gaps in Current Understanding**
To make an informed decision before the 20th, the following specific data points are missing:

1.  **Device Segmentation by Age:** We do not know the specific breakdown of device types (mobile vs. desktop) used by the 40% older demographic. It is unknown how many rely exclusively on desktops without biometric sensors.
2.  **Biometric Capability Audit:** There is no data on the operating system versions and hardware capabilities of the devices currently used to access the portal. We cannot assume mobile users have enabled or even possess fingerprint/facial recognition hardware.
3.  **Fallback Behavior:** We lack insight into how users currently manage password resets. Do they call support because they forgot the password, or because the reset process itself is unusable on their specific device?
4.  **Assistive Technology Compatibility:** There is no assessment of how biometric prompts interact with screen readers or other assistive technologies frequently used by older patients with visual or motor impairments.

**Risk Analysis**

*   **Accessibility Risk (High):** If biometric login replaces password entry rather than supplementing it, patients without compatible devices will be unable to access mental health records. This violates equitable access principles and may breach healthcare accessibility regulations. Even if offered as an option, a poorly designed hybrid flow could increase confusion and support tickets rather than reduce them.
*   **Security Benefit (Moderate to High):** Biometrics generally reduce the risk of credential stuffing and phishing compared to static passwords. However, since the audit showed zero breaches last quarter, the *actual* security urgency is lower than the *perceived* security benefit. The primary tangible gain is operational (reduced support costs), not necessarily threat mitigation.

**Recommendations for the Advisory Council**

1.  **Do Not Deprecate Passwords:** Biometric login must be implemented strictly as an *alternative* to username/password, never a replacement, until device capability data proves otherwise.
2.  **Immediate Data Collection:** Before the 20th, deploy a non-intrusive analytics script to capture anonymous device metadata (OS, browser, hardware features) across the user base, specifically segmenting by age groups if possible via login patterns.
3.  **Targeted User Research:** Conduct rapid interviews with 10–15 patients from the older demographic to understand their device usage and comfort level with biometric technology.
4.  **Phased Rollout Plan:** Propose a pilot program limited to mobile users who opt-in, while maintaining full password support for all. Measure the reduction in support tickets specifically within this pilot group before expanding.

**Conclusion**
The assumption that biometrics will reduce support costs is valid only if users have the hardware to use them. Without validating device capabilities among the older adult population, the project risks trading a solvable friction point (password resets) for a hard barrier to entry (incompatible hardware). Immediate data gathering is required to de-risk this roadmap decision.
