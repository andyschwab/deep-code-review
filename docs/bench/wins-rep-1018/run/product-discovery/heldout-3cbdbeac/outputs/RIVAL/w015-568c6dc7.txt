### Regulatory & Safety Decision Factors for DiagnoAI Pilot

**1. Critical Decision Factors: Why "Doctor-in-the-Loop" May Not Be Enough**
Regulatory bodies (FDA, EU MDR) increasingly classify AI that provides diagnostic suggestions as **Software as a Medical Device (SaMD)**. The "doctor makes the final call" argument often fails to lower risk classification if the AI output significantly influences the clinical decision.
*   **Intended Use vs. Actual Use:** If your marketing or workflow suggests the AI "identifies conditions," regulators may view this as a diagnostic claim, triggering Class II or higher requirements regardless of human oversight.
*   **Algorithmic Transparency:** An 88% aggregate accuracy is insufficient. Regulators require performance breakdowns by skin tone, age, and condition rarity. If accuracy drops below a safe threshold for specific demographics, the device is considered biased and unsafe.
*   **Failure Mode Analysis:** You must define what happens when the AI is wrong. Does it miss a melanoma (false negative)? If the doctor relies on the AI's "benign" suggestion without independent verification, liability shifts partially to the tool.

**2. Evidence Gaps Halting the Pilot**
Your current internal testing leaves three dangerous voids that cautious stakeholders and regulators will exploit:
*   **The "Real-World" Gap:** Your 5,000 images are likely curated. There is no evidence of performance on poor-quality photos taken by patients in varying lighting conditions, which is how the tool will actually be used.
*   **The Liability Void:** You lack a defined protocol for "AI-Doctor Disagreement." If the doctor overrides the AI and is wrong, or follows the AI and is wrong, who is liable? Without a legal framework and insurance specifically covering AI-assisted diagnosis, clinics face uninsurable risk.
*   **The Bias Blind Spot:** Internal datasets often lack diversity. Without proof of equitable performance across Fitzpatrick skin types I–VI, you risk causing harm to underrepresented groups, leading to immediate regulatory shutdown and reputational damage.

**3. Necessary Validation Steps Before Proceeding**
To satisfy the medical advisory board and unblock the clinic partner, execute these steps immediately:
*   **Conduct a "Silent" Retrospective Study:** Run the AI on historical patient data from the partner clinics *without* showing results to doctors. Compare AI suggestions against the final confirmed diagnoses. This proves real-world viability without exposing patients to risk.
*   **Define the "Human-in-the-Loop" Protocol:** Draft a strict Standard Operating Procedure (SOP) stating the AI is an *informational reference only*. Mandate that doctors document whether they viewed the AI suggestion and explicitly state their independent reasoning. This creates an audit trail protecting both parties.
*   **Seek a Q-Submission (FDA) or Scientific Advice (EMA):** Before the pilot, formally request feedback from regulators on your specific workflow. Ask explicitly: "Does this specific level of human oversight qualify for an investigational exemption?" Do not guess; get it in writing.
*   **Demographic Stress Testing:** Isolate your dataset to prove performance on the darkest skin tones and rarest conditions in your target list. If accuracy dips below 90% for any subgroup, the pilot is unsafe to launch.

**Note:** Do not proceed with live patient exposure until the "Silent Study" confirms real-world accuracy matches your internal metrics.
**Note:** Engage a regulatory counsel specializing in SaMD immediately to draft the liability waiver; standard software contracts are insufficient.
