### Feasibility Evaluation: Dynamic Scheduler vs. Capacity Constraints

**Executive Summary**
Building a dynamic scheduler addresses **allocation efficiency**, not **physical supply**. If your shortages stem from a regional lack of trucks (capacity constraint), software alone cannot solve the problem and may worsen operational friction by promising deliveries that physically cannot occur. The assumption that "dynamic scheduling will eliminate stockouts" is high-risk and likely false if the root cause is insufficient fleet capacity.

**Critical Risk Analysis**

1.  **The Capacity Ceiling (Viability & Feasibility Risk)**
    *   **Assumption:** There are enough trucks in the region to meet peak demand if scheduled perfectly.
    *   **Reality Check:** If total regional trucking capacity < total water demand during peak season, no algorithm can create water. A dynamic scheduler optimizes the *timing* of existing resources; it does not generate new resources.
    *   **Failure Mode:** The software predicts a shortage and attempts to reschedule, but finds no available trucks. The result is the same 14-hour halt, now accompanied by confused site managers trusting a system that promises what physics cannot deliver.

2.  **The Root Cause Ambiguity (Value Risk)**
    *   **Current State:** Static spreadsheets manage 50 weekly truckloads.
    *   **Unknown Variable:** You do not know if the 14-hour halts are due to:
        *   *Inefficiency:* Trucks arriving too late/early due to poor coordination (Software solves this).
        *   *Scarcity:* Zero trucks available to hire when needed (Software fails this).
    *   **Investor Implication:** Investing in software before distinguishing these causes is gambling. If the issue is scarcity, the capital must go toward securing long-term carrier contracts, purchasing assets, or building local storage tanks, not code.

3.  **Operational Fragility (Usability Risk)**
    *   Real-time consumption data at remote mining pits is often noisy or delayed. If the scheduler reacts to false "spikes" in consumption data, it may unnecessarily divert trucks from other sites, creating self-fulfilling shortages elsewhere in the network.

**Recommended Immediate Actions (Before Development)**

Do not commit to the full build. Execute these low-cost tests to validate the root cause:

*   **Test 1: The "Ghost Truck" Audit (Data Analysis)**
    *   Review the last three months of spreadsheet logs. Map every requested delivery time against the reason for rejection/delay.
    *   *Metric:* If >20% of delays are marked "no trucks available" or "carrier declined," the issue is capacity, not scheduling. Stop software development; focus on logistics procurement.
*   **Test 2: Manual Dynamic Simulation (Concierge Test)**
    *   For one week, have an operations manager manually simulate the dynamic algorithm using current data. When a "spike" is predicted, attempt to secure the truck immediately via phone.
    *   *Success Criterion:* If the manager cannot find the truck manually, the software won't find it either.
*   **Test 3: Buffer Storage Feasibility**
    *   Calculate the cost of increasing on-site water storage by 24 hours versus the cost of the software build. If storage is cheaper and eliminates the 14-hour halt, it is the superior MVP for supply security.

**Conclusion for Investors**
Software is a force multiplier for existing capacity, not a creator of capacity. Until you prove that unused trucking capacity exists in the region but is currently misaligned with your needs, building a dynamic scheduler is a solution looking for a problem. The priority is not coding; it is diagnosing whether the bottleneck is **information** (fixable by software) or **atoms** (fixable by logistics/infrastructure).

***

*Note: This evaluation assumes no hidden fleet assets exist outside current knowledge. Immediate field verification of carrier availability is required before any sprint planning.*
