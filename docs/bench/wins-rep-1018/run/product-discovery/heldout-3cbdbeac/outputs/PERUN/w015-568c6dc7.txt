### Decision Factors: Safety & Regulatory Exposure

**1. The "High-Risk" Classification Trigger**
Regulatory bodies (FDA, EU MDR) generally classify AI that provides diagnostic suggestions as **SaMD (Software as a Medical Device)**. The critical factor is not just the 88% accuracy, but the **intended use** and **risk of harm**.
*   **Factor:** If the output influences treatment decisions (even as a suggestion), it is likely Class II or higher.
*   **Risk:** "Doctor in the loop" does not automatically exempt you from pre-market clearance if the AI is marketed as a diagnostic aid. The paused clinic is correctly identifying that using an uncleared device on patients may violate their institutional compliance, regardless of your internal accuracy stats.

**2. The Validity Gap: Internal vs. Clinical Evidence**
Your current evidence (5,000 images, internal testing) is insufficient for regulatory clearance or cautious clinical adoption.
*   **Gap A (Data Provenance):** Regulators require proof that the 5,000 images represent the *diversity* of real-world patients (skin tones, lighting conditions, camera types, comorbidities) found in the pilot clinics. Internal datasets often suffer from selection bias.
*   **Gap B (Study Design):** Internal testing against labels is not a clinical trial. You lack evidence of **clinical utility**: Does the AI actually improve doctor diagnosis speed or accuracy compared to standard care? Or does it introduce new errors (automation bias)?
*   **Gap C (Failure Mode Analysis):** You have not defined the cost of the 12% error rate. Are these false negatives (missing melanoma) or false positives (unnecessary biopsies)? Regulatory scrutiny focuses entirely on the severity of the false negatives.

**3. Liability Exposure**
*   **The Clinic's Fear:** If a doctor relies on your suggestion and misses a diagnosis, who is liable? Without regulatory clearance, the clinic assumes 100% of the liability and reputational risk. They cannot indemnify themselves against an unvalidated tool.
*   **The Founder's Risk:** Deploying without clearance could be construed as practicing medicine without a license or distributing an adulterated device, leading to cease-and-desist orders or fines.

### Required Validation Steps to Unblock Stakeholders

To satisfy the medical advisory board and the paused clinic, you must shift from "software testing" to "clinical validation."

1.  **Conduct a Retrospective Clinical Validation Study:**
    *   Do not use new patients yet. Take a held-out set of images with known outcomes.
    *   Have board-certified dermatologists diagnose them *without* AI, then *with* AI.
    *   **Metric:** Measure the delta in diagnostic accuracy and confidence. You must prove the AI *adds* value, not just that it works in isolation.

2.  **Define the "Human-in-the-Loop" Protocol Explicitly:**
    *   Draft a Standard Operating Procedure (SOP) stating the AI is strictly an **informational triage tool**, not a diagnostic determinant.
    *   Mandate that the doctor must document an independent assessment before viewing the AI suggestion to prevent automation bias.

3.  **Engage a Regulatory Consultant for a "Q-Submission" (FDA) or Equivalent:**
    *   Before the pilot, submit a formal query to the relevant regulatory body describing your specific workflow. Ask explicitly: "Does this specific limited pilot require an Investigational Device Exemption (IDE)?"
    *   Present this written regulatory feedback to the clinic. Their pause is due to uncertainty; a formal regulatory pathway (even if it says "yes, you need a trial") is better than ambiguity.

4.  **Perform a Harm Analysis on the 12% Error Rate:**
    *   Categorize the errors in your 5,000-image set. If *any* false negatives involve life-threatening conditions, you likely cannot proceed with *any* patient exposure until that specific failure mode is mitigated or the risk is acceptably low per regulatory standards.

**Immediate Recommendation:** Do not proceed with the June pilot on live patients until you have the retrospective clinical validation data and a written regulatory strategy. Propose a **simulation pilot** instead: run the AI on historical clinic data where outcomes are already known, comparing AI suggestions against what the doctors actually did. This generates safety evidence without patient risk.
