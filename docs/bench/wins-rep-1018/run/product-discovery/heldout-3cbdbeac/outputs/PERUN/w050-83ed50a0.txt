### Executive Brief: Oxygen Supply Chain Tracker Feasibility

**Current Evidence Status: Insufficient for Build Decision**
The available evidence identifies a symptom (6-hour stockouts due to stale manual logs) but fails to distinguish between the two root causes: **Visibility Failure** (not knowing stock is low) vs. **Logistics Failure** (knowing stock is low but unable to deliver). Proceeding with a real-time tracker based on the current assumption that "staff have reliable data" is high-risk given the known 3-day/week connectivity gaps in the northern region. A tracker providing 12-hour-old data may not solve the stockout problem if the bottleneck is physical transportation rather than information latency.

**Critical Unknowns Requiring Resolution Before Development**
Before committing the pilot budget, the following unknowns must be resolved through direct observation, not speculation:

1.  **The Connectivity Reality Check:** Do clinic staff actually attempt to update logs daily? We need evidence of *attempted* updates during outage windows. If staff cannot update even when they want to, a real-time app will fail regardless of its features.
    *   *Test:* Shadow 3 northern clinics for 48 hours during an outage. Record every attempt to communicate stock levels and the method used (phone, radio, runner).
2.  **The Root Cause of Stockouts:** Was the recent 6-hour stockout caused by late detection (visibility) or delayed delivery (logistics)?
    *   *Test:* Reconstruct the timeline of the last two stockouts. Pinpoint the exact hour the clinic realized they were low vs. the hour the truck arrived. If the gap between "realization" and "arrival" is negligible, the problem is logistics, not visibility.
3.  **The Value of Stale Data:** Is 12-hour-old data sufficient to trigger a successful replenishment cycle?
    *   *Test:* Simulate a decision using 12-hour-old data from last month's logs. Would a dispatcher have sent a truck earlier? If the delivery lead time is 24 hours, 12-hour-old data might be useless.
4.  **Workarounds Already in Use:** How do clinics currently manage during outages?
    *   *Test:* Interview staff on their current "offline" protocol. If they already have a robust manual backup (e.g., scheduled runners), a digital tracker adds little value unless it integrates with that specific workflow.

**Recommendation: Pause Build, Run "Riskiest Assumption" Tests**
Do not begin software development. The riskiest assumption is that **information latency** is the primary driver of stockouts. If the true driver is **transportation latency**, a tracker is a solution in search of a problem.

**Immediate Next Steps for Pilot (August 1st):**
Repurpose the pilot from "building a tracker" to "validating the bottleneck."
*   **Action:** Equip the 10 pilot sites with simple, offline-first logging (e.g., SMS-based or paper forms collected by runners) that does not rely on real-time data.
*   **Metric:** Measure the time from "stock low" to "truck dispatched" using this low-tech method.
*   **Kill Threshold:** If reducing information latency to near-zero (via dedicated runners) does not reduce stockout duration by at least 50%, the problem is logistics. **Drop the tracker idea** and invest in transportation assets or regional warehousing.

**Decision Rule:** If the test shows that faster information does not lead to faster delivery due to road/vehicle constraints, the board must redirect funds to logistics infrastructure. If faster information *does* solve it, then proceed to build a tracker designed specifically for intermittent connectivity (offline-first architecture).

***

*Note: This analysis strictly adheres to the evidence provided; no market sizes or user personas were fabricated. The proposed "kill threshold" is a starting point for your team to refine based on actual cost-of-stockout data.*
