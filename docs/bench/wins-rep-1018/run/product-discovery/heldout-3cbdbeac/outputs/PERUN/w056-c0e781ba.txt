### Decision Analysis: MetroCare Bed Availability Model Release

**Current Status:** The proposal to release the model on June 1 based on an 89% accuracy rate from stable periods is **high-risk and currently unsupported by sufficient evidence** for general public use. The lack of data regarding surge scenarios represents a critical "unknown unknown" that disclaimers cannot mitigate.

#### 1. The Core Uncertainty: Performance Under Stress
Your internal trials validate the model only for **baseline conditions**. Hospital bed availability is non-linear; during a surge (e.g., pandemic wave, mass casualty event), the variables driving bed turnover change fundamentally.
*   **The Risk:** Models trained on stable data often fail catastrophically when input distributions shift (distributional shift). An 89% accuracy rate in normal times does not guarantee even 50% accuracy during a surge.
*   **The Consequence:** If the model predicts availability that does not exist during a crisis, hospitals may divert ambulances incorrectly or delay critical admissions based on false confidence. This is not a "potential error"; it is a systemic failure mode.

#### 2. The Liability Trap: Disclaimers Are Insufficient
Your assumption that "clear disclaimers" protect against liability is flawed in a medical context.
*   **Legal Reality:** In healthcare, the duty of care often supersedes contractual disclaimers. If a clinician relies on your tool to make a triage decision and a patient is harmed due to a prediction error during a known blind spot (surge data), the organization is liable for **negligent deployment**.
*   **The Argument:** You cannot claim ignorance of the surge risk; you have explicitly identified it. Releasing a tool while knowingly lacking data for its most critical failure mode creates a strong case for recklessness, not just error.

#### 3. Comparison: Release Now vs. Wait

| Feature | **Release Now (June 1)** | **Wait for Surge Data/Simulation** |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Primary Benefit** | Early market presence; feedback on UI/UX in normal times. | Validation of safety in high-stakes scenarios; defensible liability posture. |
| **Primary Risk** | Catastrophic failure during the first surge; loss of trust; lawsuits. | Delayed revenue; competitor movement (if any). |
| **Liability Exposure** | **Extreme.** Known gap in testing + high harm potential = negligence. | **Managed.** Demonstrates due diligence and commitment to patient safety. |
| **Stakeholder Trust** | Erodes immediately if errors occur during a crisis. | Builds long-term credibility with medical leadership. |

#### 4. Required Next Steps (The "Cheap Test")
Before any public release, you must address the surge uncertainty without waiting for a real disaster.
*   **Synthetic Stress Testing:** Generate or source historical surge data from public datasets (e.g., CDC, WHO, or open hospital data) to test the model against non-MetroCare surge patterns.
*   **Wizard-of-Oz / Concierge Pilot:** Do not automate the decision. Run the model in "shadow mode" alongside human planners during a simulated surge exercise. Compare the model's recommendations against expert human decisions.
*   **Define a Kill Threshold:** Establish a metric such as: *"If model error rate exceeds 15% during simulated surge conditions, public release is cancelled until retrained."*

**Recommendation:** **Do not release publicly on June 1.** Proceed only with a restricted, shadow-mode pilot that specifically targets surge simulation. The cost of being wrong in this domain is human life, which no disclaimer can offset.
