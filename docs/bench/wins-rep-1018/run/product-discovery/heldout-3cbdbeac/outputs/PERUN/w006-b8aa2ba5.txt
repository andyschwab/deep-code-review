### Recommendation: Prioritize the Simplified Medication Tracker

Based on the evidence provided, the **Simplified Medication Tracker** offers the higher immediate impact and lower execution risk for Q4.

**Reasoning:**
1.  **Severity of Outcome:** The usage logs indicate that missed medication doses directly drive **40% of emergency calls**. This is a verified, high-severity outcome affecting user safety. While shift coordination is a frequent struggle (12/20 families), the data does not yet link it to emergency-level events. In product discovery, preventing active harm (emergency calls) takes precedence over reducing friction (coordination), especially when the harm is quantified.
2.  **Riskiest Assumption Failure:** Your core assumption is that a "dashboard allowing real-time shift updates" will reduce errors. However, you explicitly identified a critical unknown: **whether elderly users can operate the dashboard interface** without primary caregiver support.
    *   If the elderly user (the person in crisis) cannot operate the dashboard, the solution fails exactly when it is needed most.
    *   Building a comprehensive dashboard before validating elderly usability violates the **Riskiest-Assumption Gate**. You would be building complex infrastructure on an untested behavioral premise.
3.  **Speed to Value:** A simplified medication tracker addresses the known cause of emergencies directly. It likely has a lower usability barrier for the elderly user than a multi-caregiver coordination dashboard, reducing the risk of adoption failure during a crisis.

**Decision Rule:** Do not commit to the dashboard until the usability assumption is disconfirmed or validated. Commit to the medication tracker to address the verified emergency driver immediately.

### Proposed Rapid Experiments

Run these two tests in parallel over the next 5–7 days to validate severity and usability before full development.

#### Experiment 1: The "Crisis Usability" Test (Wizard-of-Oz)
*   **Goal:** Disconfirm the assumption that elderly users can operate a dashboard interface during a crisis without help.
*   **Method:** Recruit 5 elderly users from your base. Simulate a "crisis" scenario (e.g., "Your primary caregiver is unreachable, and you need to check the shift schedule"). Present them with a low-fidelity prototype (paper or clickable wireframe) of the proposed dashboard's critical path. Do not guide them.
*   **Metric:** Task completion rate without assistance.
*   **Kill Threshold:** If **fewer than 3 of 5** users can successfully navigate the critical path within 2 minutes without prompting, the dashboard concept is **rejected** for the elderly demographic in its current form.
*   **Decision:** If killed, pivot the dashboard idea to a "Caregiver-Only" tool (excluding elderly interaction) or deprioritize it until a voice/UI breakthrough occurs.

#### Experiment 2: The "Severity & Willingness" Fake-Door
*   **Goal:** Validate if families perceive coordination errors as severe enough to pay for or actively adopt a solution, compared to medication safety.
*   **Method:** Send a survey to the 20 interviewed families. Present two distinct value propositions:
    1.  "Never miss a dose again: Automated alerts that reduce emergency calls."
    2.  "Stop shift conflicts: Real-time coordination for multiple caregivers."
    Ask them to pre-register for a beta of *one* solution (limit slots to force choice).
*   **Metric:** Conversion rate to pre-registration.
*   **Kill Threshold:** If the coordination solution receives **<50% of the sign-ups** compared to the medication solution, it indicates lower perceived urgency despite the interview frequency.
*   **Decision:** If sign-ups are low, the "struggle" mentioned in interviews is likely a complaint, not a job-to-be-done worth building for in Q4.

**Next Step for Product Council:** Approve the Medication Tracker for Q4 development contingent on Experiment 1's results. If Experiment 1 fails, scope the tracker to ensure it requires zero dashboard interaction from the elderly user (e.g., automated calls/SMS).
