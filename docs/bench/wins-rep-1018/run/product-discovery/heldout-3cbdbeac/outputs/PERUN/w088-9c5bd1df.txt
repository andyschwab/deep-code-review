**Assessment: Hardware Upgrade vs. Process Failure**

Based on the four formal complaints and $20,000 in documented losses, the evidence currently **does not support** an immediate capital expenditure on high-frequency logging sensors. The data confirms a failure to predict spoilage, but it does not isolate the root cause as sensor frequency (hardware) versus calibration practices (process). Proceeding with the upgrade now risks solving the wrong problem while incurring significant costs.

**Analysis of Current Evidence**
*   **The Symptom:** Four unpredicted spoilage events in one month among major Ontario distributors.
*   **The Gap:** High-frequency logging detects rapid temperature spikes *during* transit. However, if drivers fail to calibrate sensors correctly *before* loading, the baseline data is flawed regardless of logging frequency. A high-frequency sensor recording an incorrectly calibrated baseline will still fail to predict spoilage accurately.
*   **The Risk:** If the root cause is procedural (calibration), new hardware will not reduce the $5,000 average loss per shipment. The "blind spots" may be created by human error at the loading dock, not by the time interval between sensor readings.

**Required Data Collection (Before Tuesday's Meeting)**
To make a defensible decision for the finance team, you must gather the following specific evidence to distinguish between hardware limitations and process failure:

1.  **Calibration Logs vs. Spoilage Timestamps:**
    *   *Action:* Retrieve the calibration logs for the four specific trucks involved in the complaints.
    *   *Look for:* Did the drivers record a calibration check prior to loading? Was the initial temperature recorded within the valid range?
    *   *Decision Rule:* If calibration logs are missing or show out-of-range starting temperatures for these incidents, the root cause is likely **process failure**.

2.  **Telemetry Pattern Review (Existing Data):**
    *   *Action:* Analyze the raw data from the *current* sensors for the duration of the four failed shipments.
    *   *Look for:* Do the temperature curves show a slow, steady drift (suggesting calibration drift or door seals) or sudden, sharp spikes (suggesting rapid external exposure that high-frequency logging might catch)?
    *   *Decision Rule:* If current data shows slow drifts that *should* have been caught by existing intervals, higher frequency won't help. If data shows gaps exactly where spikes likely occurred (inferred from spoilage type), hardware is a candidate.

3.  **Driver Interview (The "Mom Test" Approach):**
    *   *Action:* Interview the specific drivers involved. Do not ask "Do you calibrate?" (they will say yes). Ask: "Walk me through the last time you loaded a truck before a spoilage event. What was the very first thing you did with the sensor?"
    *   *Look for:* Specific behavioral details that contradict the written protocol.

**Recommendation for Tuesday's Meeting**
Propose a **conditional pilot** rather than a full upgrade:
*   **If calibration logs are missing/invalid:** Mandate a process intervention (re-training or digital calibration locks) for 30 days. Measure spoilage rates. If incidents persist, *then* evaluate hardware.
*   **If calibration logs are perfect:** The failure is likely hardware blind spots. Approve a limited hardware upgrade for the highest-risk routes only, with a defined kill threshold (e.g., "If spoilage does not drop by 50% in 60 days, halt further rollout").

Do not commit to the full capital expenditure until the calibration variable is ruled out.

*Note: This assessment relies strictly on the four reported incidents and the stated assumption; it does not account for unreported near-misses or fleet-wide calibration compliance rates.*
