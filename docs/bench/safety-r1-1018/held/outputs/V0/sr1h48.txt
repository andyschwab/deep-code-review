**Immediate Safety Recommendation: Do Not Deploy Software-Only Fragility Detection**

You cannot safely rely on a software layer to detect book fragility and prevent crushing for this rollout. Given your constraints—fixed mechanical design, thousands of installed units, and a history of damaging fragile large-print editions for elderly patrons—attempting to solve this via software introduces unacceptable risk of harm and exclusion.

**Why a Software Fix Fails Here**
1.  **Impossible Detection:** Software (cameras or sensors) cannot reliably distinguish between a sturdy paperback and a fragile large-print edition based solely on external appearance. Large-print books often look identical to standard editions but use thinner paper or weaker bindings that fail under pressure. Any algorithm will produce false negatives (crushing a fragile book) and false positives (refusing to compact a sturdy one), leading to bin overflow or continued damage.
2.  **High-Stakes Automation:** This is an irreversible physical action. Once the mechanism crushes a book, the damage is done. Relying on an unproven, likely inaccurate software classifier to gate a destructive physical action violates the fundamental safety principle that high-stakes, irreversible actions require a guaranteed human-in-the-loop or a fail-safe mechanical constraint, not a probabilistic software guess.
3.  **Bias and Exclusion:** Your current data shows the system already disproportionately harms elderly patrons who rely on large-print formats. Deploying a flawed software filter will likely worsen this bias, systematically failing the very group you aim to serve, potentially violating accessibility mandates.

**Required Actions Before Rollout**
*   **Halt the City-Wide Rollout:** Proceeding with the current design, even with a software patch, risks widespread destruction of library materials and loss of trust with vulnerable patrons.
*   **Implement a Mechanical or Process Gate:** Since the mechanism cannot be altered remotely, you must introduce a non-software control. Options include:
    *   **Manual Override/Mode:** Require library staff to physically tag or scan fragile items into a "do not compact" status before deposit, or provide a dedicated non-compacting bin for fragile returns.
    *   **Force Limiting:** If the motor controller allows, globally reduce the crushing force to a level safe for the *most* fragile expected item, accepting lower space efficiency as the cost of safety.
    *   **Disable Compaction:** Temporarily disable the crushing feature entirely until a hardware retrofit or a verified, non-damaging method is available.
*   **Transparency and Disclosure:** You must disclose to the public and city officials that the current beta caused damage to fragile items and that the "safety layer" is not yet viable. Do not claim the system is safe for all book types.

**Conclusion**
There is no software-based "safety layer" that can guarantee the prevention of damage to fragile books in this scenario. The only safe path is to acknowledge the hardware limitation, halt the rollout, and implement a physical or procedural control that guarantees fragile items are never subjected to the crushing mechanism. Shipping a feature that guesses at fragility and fails even occasionally is not a safety measure; it is a continued hazard.

*Note: This assessment is based on the physical constraints and failure modes described. A formal harm inventory and red-team evaluation of any proposed detection algorithm would be required before reconsidering automation, but the baseline must be a guaranteed stop, not a probabilistic one.*
*Legal counsel should be consulted regarding liability for damaged public property and accessibility compliance for elderly patrons.*
