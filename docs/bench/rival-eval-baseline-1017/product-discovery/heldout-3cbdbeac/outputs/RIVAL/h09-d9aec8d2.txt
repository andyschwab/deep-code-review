**Recommendation: Proceed with Prototype B, Conditional on Micro-Optimization**

Based on the usability study data, **Prototype B** is the superior choice for immediate development, provided the team treats the additional 45 seconds as a critical optimization target rather than an acceptable trade-off.

**Reasoning**

1.  **Completion Rate is the Primary Gatekeeper**
    In e-commerce, a user who fails to complete checkout generates zero revenue. Prototype A's 80% completion rate implies a 20% abandonment rate due to usability friction. Prototype B reduces this friction significantly, achieving a 90% completion rate. Mathematically, even if Prototype B is slower, capturing 10% more potential customers is a foundational win. You cannot optimize the speed of a transaction that never happens. With only 10 users per variant, the difference between 8/10 and 9/10 successes is small in absolute numbers, but the directional signal is clear: Prototype A has a blocking usability issue that Prototype B resolves.

2.  **The "45-Second" Risk Context**
    Your concern regarding the 45-second delay during high-traffic holiday seasons is valid but likely misattributed. In checkout flows, time-on-task only negatively impacts conversion if the user perceives the delay as a stall, error, or lack of progress. If the extra 45 seconds in Prototype B results from additional, necessary validation steps (e.g., address verification, clearer error prevention) that *cause* the higher success rate, then the time is well-spent. Users generally tolerate slightly longer flows if they feel confident the transaction will succeed. Conversely, if the delay is due to confusion or hunting for buttons, the 90% completion rate would likely drop in a larger sample. Since the completion rate *increased*, the extra time is likely productive friction, not confusing friction.

3.  **Sample Size Limitations**
    With only 10 users per group, statistical significance is low. However, in qualitative usability testing, observing *why* users fail is more important than the raw percentage. The fact that two users failed completely in Prototype A suggests a hard stop or confusing element that the other eight navigated around. Prototype B likely removed this hard stop. Moving to development with Prototype A risks engineering a flow that 20% of users cannot navigate, which is a far greater business risk than a slight increase in transaction time.

**Action Plan for the Design Director**

*   **Freeze UI Specs for Prototype B:** Authorize engineering to begin sprint planning using Prototype B as the baseline to ensure the 90% completion capability is built.
*   **Mandate a "Speed Sprint":** Task the design and engineering leads with a specific goal to reduce the Prototype B time-on-task by 20–30 seconds before the holiday push. Focus on perceived performance (loading states, progress indicators) and removing non-essential fields.
*   **Monitor Real-World Data:** Once launched, track the correlation between time-on-task and abandonment specifically during peak traffic hours. If abandonment spikes as latency increases, you have the data to justify further UI simplification.

**Conclusion**
Do not sacrifice the 10% gain in completed orders for speed. Fix the flow that works (B), then make it faster. Building the faster but broken flow (A) will cost more to fix post-launch.
