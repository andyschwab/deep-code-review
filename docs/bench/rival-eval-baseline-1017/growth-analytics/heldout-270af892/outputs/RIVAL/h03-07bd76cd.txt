**Recommendation: Do Not Implement Globally; Archive as a Learning**

Based on the data provided, you should **kill the test** and retain the original blue button. While the result is statistically significant, it fails the test of business significance and strategic alignment.

**Interpretation for the CMO**

1.  **Statistical vs. Practical Significance:**
    The test achieved statistical significance (95% confidence), meaning the 0.4% lift is likely real and not random noise. However, with 120,000 visitors, your sample size was massive enough to detect microscopic differences that are mathematically real but operationally irrelevant. A 0.4% lift on a high-traffic site is a "micro-optimization," not a growth lever.

2.  **Cost-Benefit Mismatch:**
    You noted significant engineering effort is required for rollout. If the engineering cost to implement exceeds the $3,000/month projected gain (annualized $36,000) within a reasonable payback period (e.g., 6–12 months), the ROI is negative. Furthermore, maintaining a new design version incurs ongoing QA and design debt. If the gain is this fragile, it does not justify the technical overhead.

3.  **The "Color Expectation" Fallacy:**
    Your confusion stems from expecting a color change to drive double-digit lifts. In mature e-commerce environments, isolated UI tweaks rarely move needles by more than 1–2%. A 0.4% lift is actually a *typical* result for cosmetic changes. The fact that it didn't fail completely is a minor win, but it confirms that button color is not a primary friction point for your users.

4.  **Unmeasured Brand Risk:**
    You correctly identified a potential negative impact on brand perception. Green may signal "go" or "eco-friendly," but if it clashes with ShopSwift's established brand identity, it could erode long-term trust or recognition. Since your test only measured immediate conversion, it captured zero data on brand sentiment. Implementing a change that risks brand equity for a $3,000/month gain is a poor strategic trade-off.

**Decision Factors Beyond P-Values**

*   **Effect Size vs. Minimum Detectable Effect (MDE):** Before running the test, you should have defined the smallest lift worth the engineering cost. If your MDE was 1% (to justify the work), this test technically "failed" because 0.4% < 1%, regardless of the p-value.
*   **Implementation Cost:** Always weigh the "lift value" against the "rollout cost." High-effort changes require high-confidence, high-magnitude wins. Low-effort changes (like a CSS update) might justify small lifts. This appears to be high effort for low return.
*   **Opportunity Cost:** Engineering hours spent rolling out a green button are hours not spent on high-impact features (e.g., checkout flow simplification, payment options) that could drive 5–10% lifts.
*   **Brand Consistency:** If the green button deviates from your design system, it creates fragmentation. Consistency often drives more long-term conversion than marginal color tweaks.

**Final Verdict**
Do not run the test longer; extending it will only narrow the confidence interval around a trivial effect. Do not implement globally; the ROI and brand risk do not support it. Document this in your experimentation playbook: "Button color is not a key driver for ShopSwift checkout conversion; future efforts should focus on friction reduction rather than cosmetic variants."

*Note: Statistical significance confirms the difference exists; it does not confirm the difference matters.*
