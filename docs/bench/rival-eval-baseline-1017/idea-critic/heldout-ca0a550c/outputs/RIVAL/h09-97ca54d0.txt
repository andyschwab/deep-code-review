**Recommendation: Do not promote Alex to Team Lead at this time.**

Promoting a high-performing individual contributor (IC) with a documented history of interpersonal conflict to a leadership role is a high-risk strategy that threatens the sustainability of your new team. While Alex's technical velocity is a significant asset, the core function of a Team Lead is not code output, but team enablement, conflict resolution, and culture setting.

**Stress Test: The "Responsibility Will Fix Behavior" Assumption**
Your assumption that added responsibility will force Alex to improve their soft skills is fundamentally flawed and dangerous for three reasons:

1.  **Incentive Misalignment:** Leadership roles often reduce direct coding time. For an engineer who derives status and satisfaction from being the "fastest coder," removing that primary outlet without having first established strong interpersonal rewards can lead to frustration, not growth. Alex may view management tasks as distractions rather than responsibilities.
2.  **Power Dynamics Amplify Friction:** Soft skills deficits do not vanish with a title; they become institutionalized. An abrasive comment from a peer is annoying; the same comment from a Lead who controls performance reviews and task assignment is coercive and creates a hostile work environment. The existing complaints suggest a pattern, not a situational lapse. Patterns rarely self-correct under pressure; they typically calcify.
3.  **The "Peter Principle" Risk:** You are risking promoting Alex to their level of incompetence. The skills that made Alex a top IC (speed, individual focus) are distinct from, and sometimes opposite to, the skills required for leadership (empathy, patience, delegation). Without prior evidence of behavioral change or coaching success, betting on a personality overhaul mid-promotion is speculative at best.

**Attrition and Cultural Impact**
The risk of immediate attrition is severe. You noted three formal complaints in the past year. In a new team of eight, even one or two departures represent a 12–25% turnover rate, destroying team cohesion and velocity before the product even ships. High performers often leave bad managers faster than they leave bad code. If Alex alienates the team, you lose the collective output of eight people to save the individual output of one.

**Strategic Alternative**
Do not force a binary choice between "promote Alex" and "hire external." Instead, decouple technical leadership from people management:

*   **Appoint an External People Lead:** Hire or assign a leader with strong emotional intelligence to handle mentoring, conflict resolution, and culture. This protects the team environment.
*   **Create a "Principal Engineer" Track for Alex:** Formalize Alex's role as a technical authority. Give them ownership of architecture, code quality standards, and complex problem-solving. This leverages their 40% speed advantage without granting them direct authority over people they have clashed with.
*   **Conditional Growth Plan:** Make future people-leadership consideration contingent on Alex successfully completing executive coaching and demonstrating sustained behavioral change over 6–12 months in a non-management senior role.

**Conclusion**
Building a sustainable team culture requires prioritizing psychological safety over individual coding velocity. Promoting Alex now gambles the success of the entire automation suite on an unproven hope that their personality will change. Secure the team's foundation first; leverage Alex's technical genius second.
