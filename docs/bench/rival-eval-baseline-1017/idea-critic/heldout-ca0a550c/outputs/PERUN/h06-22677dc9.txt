**Recommendation: Proceed with a "Go-If" Open-Source Strategy**

Release the library under a strong copyleft license (e.g., AGPLv3) or a source-available license (e.g., BSL 1.1) rather than a permissive one (MIT/Apache). This approach captures the employer branding and community contribution benefits while legally preventing larger competitors from fork-and-optimizing the code to undercut your service performance.

**Decision Framework:**
*   **Verdict:** Go-If (Conditional Go).
*   **Condition:** The release must utilize a license that mandates sharing improvements back to the core project or prohibits competitive commercial redistribution without a separate agreement.
*   **Kill Criteria:** If legal counsel confirms that the chosen license creates friction too high for genuine community adoption (stifling the 20% acceleration goal) by July 25th, revert to "No-Go" (keep proprietary) or delay until a dual-licensing model is viable.
*   **Leading Metric:** Ratio of external pull requests merged vs. internal engineering hours saved (Target: >0.2 ratio by Q4).
*   **Review Date:** August 1st (pre-strategic session).

**Stress Test of Assumption (Community Contributions vs. Competitor Risk):**
Your assumption that community contributions will yield a 20% acceleration is plausible but fragile without structural guards.
1.  **The Contribution Trap:** Open-source projects often attract "drive-by" contributions (minor bug fixes, documentation) rather than deep architectural optimizations. The 20% gain relies on attracting *expert* contributors who care more about the tool's evolution than your specific business use case. Without a clear roadmap and active maintainer bandwidth to triage complex PRs, the net velocity may remain neutral or even decrease due to review overhead.
2.  **The Competitor Vector:** Your fear of a competitor undercutting you is valid *only* under a permissive license. If you release under MIT/Apache, a competitor can indeed legally ingest your 30% speed advantage, optimize it internally, and never share those improvements. However, under AGPL/BSL, they cannot use your optimized code in their proprietary competing service without open-sourcing their own stack or paying for a commercial license. This neutralizes the "free rider" risk while keeping the code public for brand building.
3.  **The Net Outcome:** The risk is not "competitor adoption" but "license mismatch." If you choose the wrong license, you lose the edge and gain little community help. If you choose the right license, you retain the edge and unlock the community. The variable is not the community's willingness to help, but your legal framework's ability to enforce reciprocity.

**Immediate Next Step:** Engage legal counsel immediately to draft a licensing strategy that balances "community friendliness" with "competitive defense" before the August 5th session.

*Note: This recommendation assumes the library's architecture is modular enough to be understood by outsiders without exposing core proprietary algorithms unrelated to data processing speed.*
*Ensure the Developer Relations team has a dedicated budget for community management, as unmanaged open-source projects rarely yield the assumed 20% acceleration.*
