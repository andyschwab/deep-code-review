**Recommendation: Accept the work under a "Generalization Gate" contract.**

Declining sacrifices 25% of your capacity for $500k, implying a $2M annualized revenue opportunity cost that likely exceeds the risk of a single custom module. However, accepting without strict constraints validates the "custom trap" where one client dictates your roadmap. The decision is not binary (accept/decline) but conditional: proceed only if the contract explicitly funds the refactoring phase required to generalize the code.

**Stress Test: Market Applicability Assumption**
Your assumption that this feature will sell to five other clients within a year is currently **unverified and high-risk**. The failure mode is not technical inability to generalize, but market misalignment. GlobalCorp's requirements may stem from legacy internal processes, unique compliance burdens, or idiosyncratic workflows that no other enterprise shares. If the code is built to satisfy GlobalCorp's specific exceptions rather than the common denominator of enterprise needs, generalization will require a near-total rewrite, negating the "build once, sell many" economics. The "unknown" you cited is actually the primary determinant of ROI; treating it as a secondary variable is a strategic error.

**Execution Framework**
To mitigate this, structure the engagement with three specific kill criteria:
1.  **The 30-Day Validation:** Before full build-out, require a joint workshop to map GlobalCorp's requirements against the needs of your next two largest prospects. If >40% of the logic is identified as unique to GlobalCorp during this mapping, trigger a contract renegotiation or pause.
2.  **The Refactoring Escrow:** Stipulate that 20% of the $500k payment is withheld until the "generalized" version is successfully deployed in a second, non-affiliated environment. This aligns incentives: you only get full paid if the code is actually reusable.
3.  **The Sunset Clause:** If the feature is not sold to a second client within 12 months, GlobalCorp agrees to an additional annual maintenance fee (e.g., 30% of the build cost) to cover the permanent divergence from the core roadmap.

This approach captures the immediate revenue and keeps the team utilized while transferring the risk of "one-off" status back to the customer who requested the deviation. It forces the validation of your market assumption *during* the build rather than hoping for it afterward.

*Note: Ensure the board sees the "Refactoring Escrow" as a protection of long-term vision, not a lack of confidence in the product. The October 15th deadline requires a signed term sheet, not just a verbal agreement.*
