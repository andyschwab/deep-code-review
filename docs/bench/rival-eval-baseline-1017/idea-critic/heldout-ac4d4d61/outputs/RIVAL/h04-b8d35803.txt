**Recommendation: Build In-House**

Despite the allure of immediate market entry, the financial and technical risk profile heavily favors building the feature internally. The acquisition asks for a 4x premium ($600k vs. $150k) based on two unverified assets: code quality and customer conversion.

**1. Stress Test: The Integration Assumption**
Your assumption that integration will take "less than three weeks" is your highest-risk variable. In software M&A, "straightforward" integration is the exception, not the rule.
*   **Architecture Mismatch:** DataPeek likely built their module on a different stack, database schema, or authentication model. Rewriting their logic to fit your architecture often takes longer than building from scratch.
*   **The "Black Box" Risk:** Without a deep-dive audit (which takes time you may not have), you cannot know if their code is brittle, undocumented, or reliant on deprecated libraries.
*   **Reality Check:** If the integration slips from 3 weeks to 3 months (a common scenario), you incur the $600k cost *plus* three months of engineering distraction, missing your board deadline anyway.

**2. Customer Conversion Risk**
You are paying $3,000 per user ($600k / 200 users) for DataPeek's list.
*   **Price Sensitivity:** Moving these users to your "higher pricing tier" introduces friction. If even 40% churn during migration due to price or feature gaps, your effective cost per retained user skyrockets.
*   **Distraction:** Migrating users requires support, billing changes, and onboarding—diverting focus from your core product roadmap.

**3. Strategic Control & Cost**
*   **Build Cost:** $150k is a known, contained expense. It buys you clean, native code that fits your existing infrastructure perfectly, ensuring long-term maintainability.
*   **Time to Market:** While building takes three months, a botched acquisition integration can easily drag into 6+ months of remediation. Building offers a predictable timeline; acquiring offers a gamble.
*   **Capital Efficiency:** Saving $450k upfront preserves cash for marketing the new feature or further R&D.

**Decision Framework for the Board**
Present this as a risk-mitigation strategy. The acquisition only makes sense if DataPeek's technology is uniquely proprietary (a "moat") rather than just a standard reporting module. If it is standard reporting, you are overpaying for speed you might not actually get.

**Action Plan:**
1.  **Reject the acquisition** unless a rigorous technical due diligence (code audit) proves their architecture is identical to yours and their code is flawless—a high bar for a small competitor.
2.  **Authorize the build** immediately to meet a Q1 launch.
3.  **Monitor DataPeek:** If they struggle post-offer rejection, you may be able to acquire their customer list later for a fraction of the cost, without the technical debt.

**Bottom Line:** Do not pay a 300% premium for a "speed" advantage that relies on an unverified technical assumption. Build the asset you control.

*Note: This analysis assumes the reporting module is a commodity feature rather than a patented, unique algorithm. If DataPeek holds exclusive IP impossible to replicate, the calculation shifts.*
