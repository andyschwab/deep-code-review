### Lane A: Unit Economics & Pricing Structure Application

You have provided the inputs to model two distinct strategies. Below is the arithmetic applied to your numbers to show the trade-offs. The decision rests on your risk tolerance regarding brand reputation versus immediate margin capture.

**Current Baseline (Standard Orders)**
*   **Price:** $200 | **Cost:** $80 | **Margin:** $120 per part.
*   **Defect Rate:** 1% (Implicit cost of rework/waste not quantified in your prompt, but noted).

**Scenario 1: Rush Pricing Strategy ($500 price point)**
*   **Inputs:** Price $500; Base Cost $80 + Rush Labor $30 = $110 total cost.
*   **Unit Margin:** $500 - $110 = **$390 per part**.
*   **Comparison:** This is **3.25x** the margin of a standard order ($390 vs $120).
*   **Sensitivity Analysis:**
    *   If you lose 50% of rush customers due to the price hike, you still generate $195 margin per accepted order versus the current $120 (assuming current rush pricing matches standard).
    *   *Break-even on Volume:* To match the total margin of 100 rush orders at current economics (assuming current rush price is $200), you only need to sell ~31 orders at the new $500 price point.
*   **Risk Factor:** You noted a defect rate increase from 1% to 5% under rush conditions. At $500/part, a 5% defect rate implies higher liability exposure in aerospace. If a failed part costs you a client permanently, the Customer Lifetime Value (LTV) loss may outweigh the $390 unit margin.

**Scenario 2: Capacity Cap Strategy (Max 10 rush parts/week)**
*   **Inputs:** Cap = 40 parts/month (10 × 4 weeks). Total Capacity = 1,000 parts/month.
*   **Volume Allocation:** 40 Rush (4%) + 960 Standard (96%).
*   **Financial Impact:**
    *   This strategy explicitly limits the revenue upside of the 40% market demand you identified.
    *   If 400 orders/month are rush requests (40% of 1,000), you are turning away 360 potential high-margin orders.
    *   *Opportunity Cost:* If those 360 orders were captured at even a moderate rush premium (e.g., $300), the forgone margin is significant.
*   **Quality Control:** Limiting volume aims to revert the defect rate from 5% toward 1%. In aerospace, "zero defects" is often a contractual requirement, not just a goal. A 5% failure rate could trigger disqualification from supplier lists, regardless of price.

**Decision Aid & Thresholds**
1.  **The Flip Input:** The deciding variable is the **cost of a defect**. If one defective part results in the loss of a major aerospace contract (high LTV), the 5% defect rate makes the uncapped rush model mathematically dangerous regardless of the $390 margin.
2.  **Rule of Thumb:** In high-liability industries, if the defect rate exceeds the client's tolerance threshold (often <0.1% in aerospace), volume caps or process changes are mandatory before price increases can be effective.
3.  **Next Step:** Before choosing, calculate the **expected value of a lost client**. If losing one client costs more than the annual profit from 360 rush orders, implement the **Capacity Cap** immediately. If the defect risk is manageable via inspection, test the **Tiered Pricing** on a small segment (e.g., offer the $500 price to only 20% of rush requesters) to measure price elasticity before a full rollout.

**Note:** This analysis applies your provided figures; it does not account for unquantified legal liabilities associated with aerospace part failures.
