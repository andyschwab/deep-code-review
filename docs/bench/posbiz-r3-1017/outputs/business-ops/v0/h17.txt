### Lane A: Pricing Model Evaluation (Arithmetic & Incentives)

To evaluate your options, we must apply your specific constraints: high fixed costs ($2M hardware), variable job times (10 mins to 12 hours), and the need to align with academic incentives.

**1. The Math of Your Options**
*   **Option A (Qubit-Hour @ $50/hr):**
    *   *Short Job (10 mins):* Revenue = $50 × (10/60) = **$8.33**.
    *   *Long Job (12 hours):* Revenue = $50 × 12 = **$600**.
    *   *Incentive:* Researchers are incentivized to optimize code for speed to save money. However, for very short jobs, revenue may not cover the marginal cost of queue management and system overhead.
*   **Option B (Flat Fee @ $2,000/run):**
    *   *Short Job (10 mins):* Revenue = **$2,000**. (High margin, but risks customer churn if perceived as unfair).
    *   *Long Job (12 hours):* Revenue = **$2,000**. (Protects margin on long runs, but if a run fails due to hardware error, do they pay again? This creates friction).
    *   *Incentive:* Researchers are incentivized to ensure the algorithm is perfect before submitting, but may be deterred from experimentation due to high per-run risk.

**2. Decision Aid & Sensitivity**
*   **Break-Even Analysis:** Your primary constraint is covering the $2M hardware cost over 18 months.
    *   Monthly Burn Target (Hardware only) ≈ $2,000,000 / 18 ≈ **$111,111/month**.
    *   *Option A Volume Needed:* At an average job time of 2 hours ($100/job), you need ~1,111 jobs/month.
    *   *Option B Volume Needed:* At $2,000/job, you need ~56 jobs/month.
*   **The Flip Input:** The deciding factor is **average successful completion rate** and **job duration distribution**. If most jobs are short experiments (<1 hour), Option B ($2,000) will likely suppress demand entirely. If jobs are consistently long and complex, Option A leaves money on the table.
*   **Rule of Thumb:** If LTV (Lifetime Value) of a university < 3x CAC (Customer Acquisition Cost), or if payback period > 12 months, reconsider the model.
*   **Recommended Next Step:** Do not choose globally yet. Run a split test for 30 days: offer Option A to 7 universities and a "Good-Better-Best" tiered version of Option B (e.g., $500 for small, $2,000 for large) to the other 8. Measure conversion and total revenue.

### Lane R: Regulatory Compliance (Route to Professional)

**Regime Identified:** Canadian Export Controls and Technology Transfer Laws.

Your question regarding restrictions on billing or licensing quantum algorithms to international universities falls under **Canada's Export and Import Permits Act (EIPA)** and the **Automated Export Reporting System (AERS)**, specifically concerning controlled goods and technology. Quantum computing technologies often appear on the **Export Control List (ECL)**, particularly items related to national security and dual-use technologies.

*   **Routing Requirement:** Determining whether your specific quantum algorithms or access methods constitute "controlled technology" requiring an export permit when accessed by non-Canadian entities is a legal determination. This depends on the specific technical parameters of your hardware and the destination countries of the universities.
*   **Action Required:** You must engage **Canadian legal counsel specializing in trade compliance and export controls**. They must review your Terms of Service and technical specifications to determine if an export permit is required for international access.
*   **Compliance Officer:** Given the high-risk nature of quantum technology and potential penalties for violations, retaining a dedicated compliance officer or external consultant to manage ongoing export license applications is likely necessary once counsel confirms the regime applies.

**Summary:** Apply the pricing split-test to validate revenue assumptions immediately. Simultaneously, pause any international expansion marketing until legal counsel confirms your export control status under the EIPA.
