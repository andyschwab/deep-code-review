**Lane R: Regulation and Compliance (Route Immediately)**
Your core question—whether you can bill under existing Durable Medical Equipment (DME) codes versus waiting for a new code, and what clinical evidence an insurer requires for coverage—is a regulatory determination.
*   **Regime:** This falls under CMS (Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services) regulations, private payer policies, and potentially the Anti-Kickback Statute if pricing involves inducements.
*   **Action:** You cannot resolve the "temporary workaround" viability or the specific clinical evidence threshold without a licensed reimbursement attorney or specialist. They must analyze your device's FDA classification (likely Investigational Device Exemption status) against specific payer Local Coverage Determinations (LCDs).
*   **Preparation for Counsel:** Before engaging counsel, gather: (1) Your current FDA correspondence regarding investigational status; (2) The specific CPT/HCPCS codes you suspect might apply; (3) Any preliminary clinical data on safety/utility. The time-sensitive item is the nine-month cash runway; counsel needs to know this deadline to prioritize a "coverage with evidence development" pathway if available.

**Lane A: Arithmetic and Unit Economics (Apply Your Numbers)**
While the *permission* to bill is Lane R, the *impact* of pricing decisions on your runway is arithmetic you can model now.

**1. Runway Sensitivity Analysis**
*   **Formula:** `New Runway (months) = (Current Cash + Pilot Revenue) ÷ (Monthly Burn − Pilot Contribution)`
*   **Application:** You have $2.1M. If your monthly burn is $X, your current runway is $2.1M / $X.
*   **The Pilot Variable:** If you secure a trial for $N$ patients at $15,000 each, total revenue is $15,000 \times N$. However, if your cost to serve (device COGS + support) is $C$ per patient, your contribution margin is $(15,000 - C) \times N$.
*   **Decision Aid:** If $C > 15,000$, every pilot patient *increases* your burn rate, shortening runway despite the revenue top-line. You must calculate $C$ immediately. If selling at a loss, the "cash inflow" is illusory unless the contract includes an upfront non-refundable deployment fee that covers variable costs.

**2. Price Precedent Risk Modeling**
*   **Formula:** `Effective Discount Rate = (Target Commercial Price − Pilot Price) ÷ Target Commercial Price`
*   **Application:** If your target commercial price is $50,000 and you pilot at $15,000, the discount is 70%.
*   **Sensitivity:** Payers often anchor future reimbursement rates to the lowest historical price paid. If you set the pilot at $15,000, model a scenario where your final allowable rate is capped at $25,000 instead of $50,000.
*   **Break-Even Volume Shift:** `Required Volume at Lower Price = Original Revenue Target ÷ New Lower Price`. If the price drops 50%, you need double the volume to hit the same revenue, which may be impossible given your manufacturing capacity or patient pool size.

Instead, draft a "Coverage with Evidence Development" proposal outline that asks payers specifically: "What minimum clinical endpoints are required to trigger a temporary code assignment?" Use this question to open doors with payer medical directors, rather than leading with a price that might anchor your value too low.
