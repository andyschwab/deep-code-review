### Net Profit Analysis: First-Come-First-Served vs. Lottery

**Assumptions & Inputs**
*   **Inventory:** 2,500 pairs (Hard Cap).
*   **Price:** $220/pair.
*   **Variable Cost:** $65/pair.
*   **Contribution Margin:** $155/pair ($220 - $65).
*   **Potential Revenue (Max):** $550,000 (2,500 × $220).
*   **Potential Gross Profit (Max):** $387,500 (2,500 × $155).
*   **Site Crash Cost (FCFS Risk):** $10,000 (lost sales + fixes).
*   **Platform Upgrade Cost:** $5,000 (applied to FCFS to mitigate crash risk).
*   **Demand:** 15,000 interested buyers (6× oversubscription).

**Scenario A: First-Come-First-Served (FCFS) with Upgrade**
*   **Strategy:** Spend $5,000 to upgrade infrastructure, aiming to capture 100% of inventory sales without crashing.
*   **Revenue:** $550,000 (assuming upgrade prevents crash and all 2,500 sell).
*   **COGS:** $162,500 (2,500 × $65).
*   **Launch Costs:** $5,000 (upgrade).
*   **Net Profit Calculation:** $550,000 - $162,500 - $5,000 = **$382,500**.
*   *Risk Note:* If the upgrade fails and the site crashes partially (e.g., only 2,000 sales before failure), profit drops to $305,000 (2,000 × $155 - $5k cost). If it crashes completely early, losses compound.

**Scenario B: Lottery System**
*   **Strategy:** No upgrade cost ($0), guaranteed fair distribution, zero crash risk from traffic spikes.
*   **Revenue:** $550,000 (Inventory sells out regardless of order; lottery ensures all 2,500 are allocated).
*   **COGS:** $162,500.
*   **Launch Costs:** $0.
*   **Net Profit Calculation:** $550,000 - $162,500 = **$387,500**.
*   *Risk Note:* This assumes the lottery mechanism does not reduce the *final* conversion of the 2,500 units. If "urgency dampening" causes 10% of winners to drop out (only 2,250 sold), profit becomes $348,750.

**Comparative Analysis**
Mathematically, the **Lottery System yields higher baseline net profit ($387,500 vs. $382,500)** because it avoids the $5,000 upgrade cost while still selling the same hard-capped inventory. The FCFS model only becomes superior if the Lottery causes a sales volume drop of more than 33 pairs (approx. 1.3% of inventory). Since you have 15,000 interested buyers for 2,500 spots, demand is sufficiently high that a lottery is unlikely to leave inventory unsold unless the user experience is fundamentally broken.

The FCFS model introduces a binary risk: pay $5,000 to potentially secure the same sales volume, or risk a crash that costs $10,000+. Given the 6× oversubscription, the "impulse buyer" advantage of FCFS is negligible because supply cannot meet demand regardless of speed.

**Decision Thresholds**
*   Choose **Lottery** if you believe >98.7% of lottery winners will complete checkout.
*   Choose **FCFS** only if historical data shows lottery abandonment rates exceed 1.3% *and* you are certain the $5,000 upgrade guarantees 100% uptime.

**Required Metrics from Last Three Drops**
To finalize this decision, calculate these specific figures from your history:
1.  **Checkout Abandonment Rate by Entry Method:** Compare completion rates of users who entered via a waiting queue (simulated lottery) vs. immediate access.
2.  **Peak Concurrent User Conversion:** What % of users purchased during the highest traffic minute of previous drops? If this was <16.7% (2,500/15,000), FCFS technically leaves money on the table due to technical limits anyway.
3.  **Customer Support Ticket Volume per 1,000 Visitors:** High volumes during drops indicate friction that a lottery would eliminate, preserving brand equity.
