### Payment Structure Strategy for ModularShelter

You cannot fulfill this contract under the current terms without risking insolvency. With $6M in upfront material costs ($30k × 200 units) and only $1.5M in capital, you face a $4.5M immediate deficit, not accounting for your $200k monthly burn. You must restructure the payment schedule before signing.

**1. Negotiate Progress Payments (Primary Solution)**
Do not accept a single lump sum upon delivery. Request a **Progress Payment Clause** based on milestones. A viable structure for federal contracts often looks like this:
*   **30% Mobilization:** Paid upon contract award to secure materials ($2.7M).
*   **40% Manufacturing Milestone:** Paid when 50% of units are completed ($3.6M).
*   **25% Delivery:** Paid upon shipment/delivery of units ($2.25M).
*   **5% Retainage:** Paid 30 days after final acceptance ($450k).

This aligns cash inflow with your cash outflow, allowing you to purchase materials and cover burn rates as production advances.

**2. Leverage Federal Acquisition Regulations (FAR)**
You do not need to ask for special favors; you are requesting standard mechanisms defined in the **Federal Acquisition Regulation (FAR)**:
*   **FAR Part 32.4 (Progress Payments):** This explicitly authorizes progress payments for commercial items if customary in the industry or necessary for performance. Prefabricated construction often qualifies.
*   **FAR Part 32.1 (Contract Financing):** Agencies are encouraged to use contract financing to facilitate small business participation.
*   **The Prompt Payment Act:** While this mandates timely payment, it does not override the specific "net-90" term if written in the solicitation. However, you can argue that net-90 on a custom manufacturing contract violates the spirit of FAR 32.4 if it prevents performance.

**3. Invoice Factoring (Secondary Solution)**
If the agency refuses to alter the payment schedule, you may use **invoice factoring** or **accounts receivable financing**.
*   **How it works:** You assign the rights to the government invoice to a lender. They advance you ~80-90% of the value immediately.
*   **Constraint:** Lenders view government contracts as low-risk, but they will still require proof of contract award.
*   **Cost:** Expect fees of 1-3% of the invoice value. On $9M, this could cost $90k–$270k, which is preferable to bankruptcy but reduces your margin.
*   **Note:** You generally cannot factor an invoice until it is generated (i.e., upon delivery). This does not solve your *upfront* material cost problem unless you secure a "purchase order financing" loan specifically against the contract award.

**4. Legal Expertise Requirement**
**Yes, you absolutely need a legal expert specializing in government contracts.**
*   **Why:** Federal contracts are rigid. Attempting to negotiate terms without understanding the specific solicitation clauses (e.g., whether it is a Fixed-Price vs. Cost-Reimbursement contract) can lead to your proposal being rejected as "non-compliant."
*   **Risk:** An amateur attempt to rewrite payment terms might signal financial instability to the contracting officer, jeopardizing the award. A specialist can frame the request as a standard "Contract Financing" modification under FAR 32, ensuring you remain compliant while securing liquidity.

**Immediate Action Plan:**
1.  Halt signing until terms are revised.
2.  Retain a government contracts attorney immediately.
3.  Submit a formal request for **Progress Payments** citing FAR 32.4, detailing your production schedule and cash flow needs.
4.  If progress payments are denied, explore **Purchase Order Financing** specifically for government contracts to cover the initial $4.5M gap.

*Do not proceed with production using only your current working capital; you will run out of cash before completing 50 units.*
