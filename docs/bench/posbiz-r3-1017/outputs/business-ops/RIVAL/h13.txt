### Revenue Model Analysis: SkyPath

**Recommendation:** Pursue the **Airport Licensing Model (B2B)** as your primary revenue stream, with a optional "Freemium" consumer layer for premium upsells (e.g., lounge access discounts), rather than a hard paywall.

**Unit Economics & Scalability Comparison**

1.  **Direct-to-Consumer (DTC) Subscription ($2.99/trip)**
    *   **Projected Revenue:** 45M passengers × 3% conversion × $2.99 = **~$4.04M annually**.
    *   **Reality Check:** This model assumes a frictionless payment flow in a high-stress environment (travelers rushing to gates). Conversion for single-use navigation apps is typically <0.5% unless integrated into the airline's existing app. Acquiring these users requires massive marketing spend, destroying your unit economics.
    *   **Scalability:** Low. You must negotiate with every traveler individually. Churn is 100% after the trip.
    *   **Risk:** High. With 6 months of runway, you cannot afford the customer acquisition cost (CAC) required to hit that 3% conversion.

2.  **Airport Licensing (B2B)**
    *   **Projected Revenue:** $150,000 per airport × 5 major SEA hubs = **$750,000 annually** (conservative base).
    *   **Upside Potential:** This is not a cap. Contracts should include performance bonuses based on passenger engagement metrics or expansion fees for new terminals. As you prove value (reduced missed flights, increased retail dwell time), you can raise licensing fees to $500k+ per hub.
    *   **Scalability:** High. One sale covers 45M users. Sales cycles are long, but retention is sticky once integrated into airport infrastructure.
    *   **Unit Economics:** Superior. CAC is high per deal but amortized over millions of users. Margins are near 100% after development.

**Strategic Verdict:**
The DTC model is a "lottery ticket" requiring viral adoption you likely cannot fund. The B2B model provides the **guaranteed cash flow** necessary to extend your runway. Use the $150k Changi deal as a case study to sell to Kuala Lumpur, Bangkok, and Jakarta. Do not charge travelers for basic wayfinding; it creates friction that airports will reject. Instead, offer the base app for free (sponsored by the airport) and monetize via the license, or upsell non-essential features (e.g., "Reserve a lounge chair") where you take a transaction fee.

### Singapore Legal & Compliance Overview

**Data Privacy (PDPA):**
Singapore's Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA) strictly governs how you collect, use, and disclose passenger data.
*   **Consent:** You cannot sell or share passenger movement data with the airport authority without explicit, informed consent from the user. Buried terms of service are insufficient for sensitive location data.
*   **Purpose Limitation:** Data collected for navigation cannot be repurposed for commercial analytics (selling to retailers/airport) unless the user specifically opts in for that secondary purpose.
*   **Restriction:** Charging users specifically for "navigation data" is not illegal, but restricting safety-critical wayfinding behind a paywall may attract scrutiny from the Competition and Consumer Commission of Singapore (CCCS) if deemed an unfair practice, though this is a gray area.

**Do You Need a Lawyer?**
**Yes.** Do not draft commercial licensing agreements or data processing addendums yourself.
*   **Licensing Agreement:** Needs specific clauses on SLA (uptime guarantees), liability (if a user misses a flight due to app error), and IP ownership.
*   **Data Sharing:** If the airport wants aggregated heatmaps, you need a lawyer to structure this so it complies with PDPA anonymization standards to avoid heavy fines.
*   **Cost vs. Risk:** A single compliance breach or poorly written liability clause could bankrupt your startup. Allocate budget for a Singapore-based tech counsel immediately.
