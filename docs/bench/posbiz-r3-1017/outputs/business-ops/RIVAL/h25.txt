DeepSea Data should adopt a **component-based straight-line depreciation method** with a conservative useful life estimate of **5 to 6 years**, rather than the standard 3-year IT cycle or the optimistic 8-year hardware potential.

**Recommended Depreciation Schedule:**
*   **Method:** Straight-Line (SL). Do not use an accelerated method (like Double Declining Balance) unless you anticipate a specific, imminent technological obsolescence event. Accelerated methods imply the asset loses most value early on; however, your primary risk is not rapid functional decay but rather sudden, total loss or stable long-term operation. SL better matches the steady revenue generation of a data center.
*   **Useful Life:** Set at **5.5 years (66 months)**. This creates a middle ground between the standard 3-year IT write-off and the theoretical 8-year lifespan. It acknowledges the stability of the Norwegian deep-sea environment (low temperature, no oxygen corrosion) while pricing in the "inaccessibility risk premium."
*   **Salvage Value:** Set to **$0**. Given the $15,000/day retrieval cost and the physical difficulty of recovering failed pods from 300 meters, the net realizable value of a decommissioned unit is negligible. Assuming any salvage value would be aggressive and likely challenged by auditors.

**Justification for Auditors:**
To defend this approach to lenders and auditors unfamiliar with subsea infrastructure, frame the argument around **"Economic Utility vs. Physical Existence."**

1.  **The Inaccessibility Constraint:** Argue that while the *physical* hardware might last 8 years, the *economic* useful life is shorter because the inability to perform routine repairs (component swaps, fan replacements) increases the probability of catastrophic, non-recoverable failure as the units age. The "maintenance gap" creates a reliability cliff that standard land-based servers do not face.
2.  **Environmental Stress Factors:** Highlight that while the cold water aids cooling, the pressure (approx. 30 bar) and saline environment create unique stress points on seals and connectors. Unlike land-based servers where a single component failure can be fixed, a single seal failure in your context could mean total asset loss. This justifies a life estimate shorter than the theoretical maximum.
3.  **Retrieval Costs as a Sunk Cost:** Explicitly state that the high cost of Remotely Operated Vehicles (ROVs) renders end-of-life resale or parts harvesting economically unviable. This supports the $0 salvage value assumption.

**Specific Factors Influencing Asset Life Estimates:**
*   **Seal Degradation Curves:** Use manufacturer data on O-ring and pressure housing lifespan under constant high pressure. This is likely your limiting factor, not the silicon chips.
*   **Thermal Cycling:** Even in stable deep water, internal heat generation creates micro-cycling. If your design cannot mitigate this internally, it accelerates solder joint fatigue.
*   **Technological Obsolescence vs. Repairability:** Since you cannot upgrade these pods, their useful life ends when they can no longer efficiently run modern workloads, not necessarily when they break. However, given the high capex ($4.2M/pod), extending life too far risks carrying assets on the books that are effectively stranded if a minor internal fault occurs.

By choosing a 5.5-year straight-line schedule, you present a prudent, defensible position that satisfies lender conservatism without unnecessarily punishing your P&L with an overly aggressive 3-year write-off that doesn't reflect the actual stability of your operating environment.
