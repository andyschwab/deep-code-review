### Unit Economics & Seasonal Variance Model: DryAir Systems

**Current Baseline (Non-Summer)**
*   **Hardware Margin:** $2,700 per unit ($4,500 Price - $1,800 COGS).
*   **Cash Position:** $550,000 (covers ~203 units of COGS).
*   **Installed Base:** 200 units.

**The Variable: Energy Cost Impact**
To model the impact, we must estimate consumption. Assuming a standard atmospheric water generator in an arid climate produces ~5 gallons/day and requires ~0.5 kWh per gallon:
*   **Daily Usage:** ~2.5 kWh.
*   **Summer Monthly Usage (90 days):** ~225 kWh.
*   **Winter Monthly Usage (275 days):** ~687 kWh (annualized context, but we focus on the *delta*).
*   **Cost Delta:** The price doubles from $0.12 to $0.24.
    *   Winter Cost (per kWh): $0.12.
    *   Summer Cost (per kWh): $0.24.
    *   **Extra Cost to Customer:** $0.12 × 225 kWh (summer months) = **$27.00 extra per summer month per unit.**

While $27/month seems low, in high-heat regions where AC usage spikes, this adds to "sticker shock" on utility bills. However, the real risk is not the absolute dollar amount, but the *perception* of variable costs on a fixed-income asset.

**Scenario A: Absorb 50% of Energy Cost (Subsidy Plan)**
*   **Subsidy Amount:** $13.50 per unit/month during summer (3 months).
*   **Total Annual Subsidy Cost (200 units):** $13.50 × 200 × 3 = **$8,100.**
*   **Impact on Hardware Margin:** Reduces effective margin by $40.50 per unit annually.
*   **New Effective Margin:** $2,659.50.
*   **Cash Runway Impact:** Negligible ($8,100 is 1.4% of cash reserves).
*   **Churn Mitigation:** High. Customers feel protected from volatility. This acts as an insurance policy against "bill shock" churn.

**Scenario B: Pass Full Cost to Customer (Status Quo)**
*   **Subsidy Cost:** $0.
*   **Hardware Margin:** $2,700.
*   **Risk:** If elasticity causes a >20% drop in sales volume due to fear of high operating costs:
    *   Current Sales Velocity (assumed for model): If you sell 10 units/month at $2,700 margin = $27,000/mo.
    *   20% Drop: Lose 2 units/month = **$5,400/mo lost margin.**
    *   **Annual Lost Opportunity:** $64,800.
*   **Comparison:** The potential lost margin from a volume drop ($64,800) vastly exceeds the cost of the subsidy ($8,100).

**Scenario C: Increase Hardware Price to Buffer**
*   To cover the average annual energy variance (~$81/year per unit), you would need to raise prices by ~$100.
*   **New Price:** $4,600.
*   **Risk:** Upfront friction. In a price-sensitive market, a $100 increase on a $4,500 item is a 2.2% hike. If demand is elastic, this kills conversion before the customer even calculates energy savings.

**Recommendation: The "Summer Shield" Strategy**
Do not raise the hardware price. Instead, offer an optional **"Summer Shield" add-on** or include it in a service contract.
1.  **Absorb 50% of energy costs** for the first 2 summers only. Cost: ~$8,100 (Year 1) + scaling with sales.
2.  **Marketing Angle:** "Guaranteed operating costs even when electricity spikes." This removes the unknown variable for the buyer.
3.  **LTV Impact:** Retaining a customer is worth far more than the $40/year subsidy. If churn drops by even 5%, you save ~10 units ($27,000 margin), paying for the subsidy 3x over.

**Conclusion:** The math favors absorbing the cost. The $8,100 subsidy is an inexpensive hedge against the $64,800+ risk of volume contraction due to price sensitivity.

*Note: This model assumes ~2.5 kWh/day usage; adjust if your specific unit efficiency differs. Implement the subsidy as a rebate check or account credit to maintain visibility of the benefit.*
