**Recommendation: Adopt Net Retention Rate (NRR) as your North Star, not MRR or NPS.**

While your binary choice is between MRR and NPS, selecting either alone creates a fatal flaw for your Series B narrative. MRR is a vanity metric in this context; it can rise via aggressive sales while your churn隐患 (indicated by the NPS drop from 60 to 45) silently destroys long-term value. Investors seeing 20% growth today will discount your valuation tomorrow if they detect the underlying retention leak. Conversely, NPS is a lagging sentiment indicator, not a business outcome; you cannot pay servers with "likelihood to recommend," and improving it does not guarantee immediate revenue stability without a defined translation mechanism.

**The Argument for Net Retention Rate (NRR)**
NRR (starting MRR + expansion - churn - contraction) / starting MRR) is the only metric that mathematically forces the behavior you need: it rises *only* when you keep customers happy enough to renew (addressing the NPS slip) AND grow their accounts (addressing the 20% growth target).

1.  **Operational Focus:** Your data shows a stark reality: customers with NPS >50 have a 95% renewal rate, while those below drop to 70%. By making NRR the North Star, you force the sales team to stop selling into accounts that will churn (protecting the denominator) and force the success team to fix the experience drivers causing the NPS slip (protecting the numerator). If you choose MRR, sales will ignore the NPS slip to hit quarterly targets, worsening the churn cliff you face in 12 months. If you choose NPS, product may over-optimize for satisfaction features that don't drive renewal or expansion. NRR aligns both: you cannot improve NRR without fixing the satisfaction issues causing the 70% renewal rate.
2.  **Investor Narrative:** For Series B, investors prioritize efficiency and durability over raw top-line growth. A story of "20% growth driven by NRR expansion" is significantly more valuable than "20% growth driven by new sales with leaking buckets." It demonstrates that your product has found product-market fit and that the unit economics are sustainable. It directly answers your fear about revenue stability: NRR *is* the measure of that stability. If NRR is >100%, your revenue grows even if you stop selling to new customers, proving the business model works.

**Implications of the Choice**
*   **Choosing MRR:** You risk securing Series B based on inflated top-line numbers, only to face a down-round or failure in Series C when the churn from your dissatisfied (NPS 45) base catches up. You incentivize selling to the wrong customers.
*   **Choosing NPS:** You risk missing your 20% growth target entirely. While satisfaction may recover, the link to revenue is too indirect to guarantee the specific financial milestones required for your round. You incentivize building features that make users happy but don't necessarily make them pay more or stay longer.

**Strategic Path Forward**
Make NRR the single North Star. Set the input metrics to drive it: (1) Renewal Rate of the NPS <50 cohort (to fix the leak), and (2) Expansion Revenue from the NPS >50 cohort (to fuel growth). This tells your founding team: "We will not count new sales as success if we are losing existing customers, and we will not count satisfaction as success if it doesn't translate to retention and expansion." This single metric bridges the gap between your immediate growth need and your long-term health fear, providing a coherent, defensible narrative for investors that proves you understand the mechanics of a sustainable SaaS business.

*Note: NRR requires accurate tracking of churn and expansion MRR; ensure your billing data supports this before presenting. Do not revert to MRR even if NRR dips temporarily; the dip signals the exact retention issue you must fix.*
