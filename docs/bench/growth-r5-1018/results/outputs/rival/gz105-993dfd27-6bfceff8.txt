### Experiment Interpretation: Verified Identity Badge Test

**Executive Summary**
The experiment presents a classic "efficiency vs. exploration" trade-off rather than a failure. The 2% lift in checkout completion indicates the badge successfully reduces friction for users ready to buy, validating the trust hypothesis for conversion. However, the 5% drop in product page views suggests the badge may be altering user behavior earlier in the funnel, potentially by reducing the need for extensive browsing or by creating visual friction that discourages initial engagement.

**Interpretation of Conflicting Signals**
Your assumption that trust signals uniformly increase browsing time is likely incorrect. In high-risk cross-border markets, users often browse extensively *because* they lack trust, searching for signals of legitimacy across multiple products. The badge may be providing immediate reassurance, allowing users to skip the "investigative browsing" phase and move directly to checkout on the first viable option. This would naturally decrease page views per session while increasing conversion rates—a positive outcome for revenue efficiency, even if it looks like reduced engagement.

Alternatively, the drop could indicate UI clutter deterring initial clicks, but this is less likely given the checkout improvement. If the badge were purely distracting, you would expect drops across both metrics.

**Recommended Data Slices for Product Council**
To distinguish between "efficient conversion" and "deterred browsing," request these specific cuts before Friday:

1.  **Session Depth by Variant:** Compare average pages per session. If the treatment group has fewer pages but higher conversion, the badge is creating efficiency. If both pages and time-on-site drop without conversion gains in specific segments, it suggests deterrence.
2.  **New vs. Returning Users:** New users likely benefit most from trust signals. If returning users show the page view drop without conversion lift, the badge may be redundant or clutter for familiar shoppers.
3.  **High-Value vs. Low-Value Items:** Trust signals typically matter more for expensive purchases. Slice by product price tier to see if the checkout lift is concentrated in high-risk categories.
4.  **Mobile vs. Desktop:** Badges can cause significant UI clutter on mobile screens. If the page view drop is isolated to mobile, you have a design issue, not a conceptual one.
5.  **Seller Tier Performance:** Analyze if the badge helps new/unknown sellers more than established ones. If established sellers see a drop in views, the badge might be creating an unintended "two-tier" perception that discourages browsing their inventory.

**Strategic Recommendation**
Do not reject the rollout based on the page view metric alone. If the data confirms that users are converting faster (fewer pages, higher checkout), this is a win for user experience and revenue. Frame the narrative around "reducing friction" rather than "increasing browsing." However, if mobile users or returning customers show pure degradation (lower views, flat conversion), recommend a targeted rollout: deploy globally for new users and high-value categories, but refine the mobile UI or suppress the badge for returning customers.
