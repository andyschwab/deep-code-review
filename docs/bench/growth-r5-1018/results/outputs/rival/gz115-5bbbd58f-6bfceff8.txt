**Recommended Goal Metric: 30-Day Driver Return Rate (Active Retention)**

For a two-sided marketplace like TruckGig, **liquidity** (available loads) and **satisfaction** (driver earnings) are interdependent, but **retention** is the ultimate lagging indicator that validates both. If drivers return within 30 days, it proves they found sufficient load density *and* achieved acceptable earnings per hour. If they do not return, the platform has failed regardless of how many total loads were moved in January.

Prioritizing "Total Loads Completed" risks burning out drivers by pushing low-margin freight to hit volume targets, worsening the "low pay" complaint. Prioritizing "Earnings Per Hour" is operationally difficult to track in real-time across independent contractors and may encourage drivers to cherry-pick only high-value lanes, leaving shipping companies with unfilled capacity.

The **30-Day Return Rate** directly addresses your conflicting data points:
1.  **Inconsistent Availability:** If a driver cannot find enough loads to stay busy in their first 30 days, they will not return. A drop in this metric signals a liquidity gap in specific regions or lanes.
2.  **Pay Per Mile:** If loads are available but pay poorly, drivers will try the platform once and leave. A low return rate captures this economic dissatisfaction without needing complex hourly wage calculations.

**Tracking Strategy to Avoid Perverse Incentives**

To track this without creating negative behaviors, you must define the metric strictly and pair it with guardrails:

*   **Definition:** The percentage of unique drivers who complete at least one load in a given cohort month (e.g., January) and complete at least one additional load within the subsequent 30 days.
*   **Segmentation:** Track this metric by **region** and **lane**. Aggregate data hides local liquidity crises. If retention drops 15% overall but 40% in the Midwest, you know exactly where load density is failing.
*   **Guardrail Metric:** **Average Revenue Per Load (ARPL).**
    *   *Why:* If you optimize purely for retention, operations might flood the network with ultra-low-paying "filler" loads just to keep drivers active. Monitoring ARPL ensures that while you are getting drivers to return, you aren't doing so by degrading the economic quality of the network.

**Implementation Logic**
Do not measure this as a simple monthly snapshot. Use **cohort analysis**. Compare the return rate of drivers who joined/completed their first load in December vs. January. This isolates the recent drop from seasonal noise. If the January cohort shows a lower projected 30-day return rate than December by day 15, you have an early warning signal to adjust load distribution or pricing incentives before the month closes.

This metric aligns your executive strategy: improving it requires solving both the "empty miles" (availability) and "rate per mile" (earnings) problems simultaneously, ensuring sustainable growth rather than short-term volume spikes.

*Note: Ensure your data team excludes drivers who voluntarily pause for personal reasons (if identifiable) to prevent skewing the data, though most freelance churn will appear as simple attrition.*
*Present this to leadership as the "Health Score" for the network, distinct from "Volume Score" (total loads).*
