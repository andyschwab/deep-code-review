**Executive Summary: Price Experiment Results (Faroe Islands Pilot)**

**Recommendation: Do not roll out the price globally based on this data.**

The current dataset from the Faroe Islands pilot is insufficient to determine if the price change had any real effect. The extreme daily volatility you observed (200–900 units) is characteristic of small-sample environments where external factors overwhelm the signal of a price change. Making a global decision on this basis carries a high risk of financial loss.

**Why the Data Is Inconclusive**

In a market of only 3,000 people, daily sales figures are too "noisy" to reveal trends. When the total number of potential buyers is this low, a single local event—a community gathering, a tour bus arrival, or indeed, a sunny versus rainy day—can double or triple your sales regardless of price. This creates the illusion of a pattern where none exists.

Because we lack the granular weather data to mathematically separate the impact of rain from the impact of your price test, we cannot isolate the price variable. The "swing" you see is likely the environment, not the customer's reaction to the cost.

**What to Look For Instead (How to Smooth the Noise)**

To find a reliable signal in future tests, stop looking at daily numbers. They will almost always be misleading in small markets. Instead, apply these three aggregation rules:

1.  **Aggregate by Full Weeks, Not Days**
    Daily fluctuations are random; weekly patterns are structural. Sum your revenue and units sold into 7-day blocks. This smooths out the "weather noise" (e.g., a rainy Tuesday is balanced by a sunny Saturday). If the price had a real effect, you would see a consistent shift in the *weekly* total compared to the period before the test, not just day-to-day chaos.

2.  **Compare Against a "Lookalike" Control**
    You cannot judge these numbers in a vacuum. You must compare the test villages against similar villages where the price *did not change*. If sales in the test villages moved in lockstep with the control villages (both went up on sunny weeks, both dropped on rainy weeks), then the price change had no effect. The movement was purely environmental. Without a control group, every spike and dip is uninterpretable.

3.  **Focus on Conversion Rate, Not Raw Volume**
    Raw unit sales (200 vs. 900) depend entirely on how many people walked past the store or visited the site. If a sunny day brings 10x the traffic, sales will skyrocket even if the price is too high. Instead, look at the percentage of visitors who bought. If foot traffic doubled but the *percentage* of buyers stayed the same, the price did not deter them. If traffic doubled but the *percentage* of buyers dropped, the price is likely the culprit.

**Next Steps for the VP of Sales**

*   **Pause the Global Rollout:** The risk of setting the wrong price globally is too high given the ambiguity of this pilot.
*   **Extend the Test or Change Locations:** We need either a much longer testing window (4+ weeks) to average out the weather, or we must move the test to a larger market with higher traffic volume where daily noise is naturally lower.
*   **Secure a Control Group:** Any re-test must include identical locations where prices remain unchanged to serve as a baseline for comparison.

**Note:** The variance observed is consistent with small-population statistical noise rather than price sensitivity.
**Note:** Proceeding without a control group or larger sample size will likely result in a failed global launch.
