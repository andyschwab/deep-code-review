**Recommendation: Prioritize Monthly Retention as Your Q3 North Star**

Based on the metrics provided, optimizing for retention offers a higher probability of sustainable growth for FreshBox Meals than focusing on referrals. Your current retention rate of 62% suggests a significant leakage problem; improving this by even 5 percentage points compounds value across your entire existing base, whereas referral optimization only impacts the 8% of new acquisitions currently using that channel.

In the meal kit industry, Lifetime Value (LTV) is almost entirely dependent on how long a subscriber stays. A user who stays two months longer generates significantly more margin than the cost savings acquired from a referred user. Unless your referral program can be scaled to drive >25% of new sign-ups with high-quality users who retain better than paid channels, the mathematical ceiling for referral growth is too low to serve as your primary lever right now. Fixing the "bucket" (retention) must happen before you turn up the "hose" (referrals), or you simply churn through referred customers faster.

**Critical Data Gaps & Immediate Actions**

To present a definitive financial model to your VP, you must address these three missing data points immediately:

1.  **Cohort-Based LTV by Acquisition Source:** You cannot compare retention vs. referrals without knowing if referred users actually stay longer than paid users. Your current 7-day attribution window is critically flawed for meal kits, where the decision cycle often spans 2–3 weeks. You are likely misattributing organic or delayed referral sign-ups to "direct" or "brand," artificially depressing the perceived value of your referral program.
    *   *Action:* Extend your attribution lookback window to at least 30 days and run a cohort analysis comparing Day-30 and Day-90 retention of referred users vs. paid users.

2.  **Marginal Cost of Retention vs. Acquisition:** You need the specific cost to lift retention by 1% (e.g., cost of win-back emails, recipe improvements, support interventions) versus the Cost Per Acquisition (CPA) of the referral program (fixed $20 credit + operational costs).
    *   *Action:* Calculate the break-even point: How many additional months of retention equal the margin of one new referred customer?

3.  **Referral Funnel Drop-off Rates:** You know the conversion rate (8% of sign-ups), but not the viral coefficient (how many invites are sent per user). Low conversion could be due to low participation (nobody sends invites) or low conversion (invites are sent but ignored).
    *   *Action:* Segment the referral metric into "Invites Sent per Active User" and "Invite Acceptance Rate" to diagnose if the issue is motivation or offer appeal.

**Strategic Note:** Do not abandon the referral program, but deprioritize it as the *primary* Q3 north star. Run a targeted experiment to test a higher incentive ($25 vs. $20) on a small segment to see if acceptance rates lift, but allocate 70-80% of your growth team's bandwidth to retention initiatives (e.g., onboarding flow optimization, pause-vs-cancel features) until monthly retention exceeds 70%.

*Note: Ensure your analytics team recalibrates the attribution window before finalizing the Q3 budget, as current data likely underreports referral ROI.*
*Note: This recommendation assumes your unit economics (food cost + logistics) remain stable; if margins are thin, retention becomes even more critical due to the high fixed costs of acquiring any new box.*
