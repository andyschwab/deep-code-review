**Recommendation: Adopt Net Revenue Retention (NRR) as your North Star, not MRR or NPS.**

While your choice is framed as MRR vs. NPS, both are flawed standalone North Stars for your current stage. MRR is a vanity metric in this context—it can rise via aggressive sales while churn accelerates underneath, masking the very risk threatening your Series B. NPS is a lagging sentiment indicator; it predicts value but is not value itself. You need a metric that mathematically forces the behavior you require: sustainable growth that satisfies investors *and* reflects customer health.

**The Argument for Net Revenue Retention (NRR)**
NRR (Starting MRR + Expansion – Churn – Contraction) / Starting MRR) is the only metric that reconciles your conflicting pressures.
1.  **Investor Narrative:** Investors funding Series B care about efficient growth. A high MRR growth rate with low retention is a "leaky bucket" that burns cash. NRR > 100% proves your product creates enough value to grow even without new sales, which commands higher valuations than raw MRR growth alone.
2.  **Operational Focus:** Unlike MRR, which incentivizes selling to anyone, NRR penalizes churn. If your NPS slip (60 → 45) causes churn to rise, NRR drops immediately, signaling the team to fix retention before pouring fuel on acquisition. Unlike NPS, which is abstract, NRR ties satisfaction directly to the P&L.

**Implications of the Choice**
*   **If you choose MRR:** Your sales team will optimize for closed deals, likely ignoring the onboarding friction causing your NPS drop. You may hit your 20% QoQ target temporarily, but if the underlying churn rate (currently implied by the NPS slip) exceeds your expansion, you will miss future targets catastrophically. Investors will discover this during due diligence when they see your cohort curves flattening.
*   **If you choose NPS:** Your product team may over-optimize for satisfaction features that do not drive renewal or expansion. While NPS > 50 correlates with 95% renewal, NPS itself does not guarantee revenue. You risk building a beloved product that fails to scale financially, missing the 20% growth mandate entirely.
*   **If you choose NRR:** Every department aligns. Sales must sell to customers who will stay (improving NPS quality). Customer Success must prevent churn and drive expansion (directly lifting NRR). Product must fix the "aha" moment to stop the NPS bleed. This metric forces you to address the NPS slip *because* it hurts revenue, not just because it feels bad.

**Strategic Execution for the Next Year**
Make NRR your primary narrative. Frame the 20% growth goal as a function of NRR + New Logo Acquisition.
*   **Immediate Action:** Calculate your current NRR. If it is below 100%, your 20% growth goal is mathematically impossible to sustain without infinite acquisition spend.
*   **The Link:** Use your internal data (NPS > 50 = 95% renewal) to model the revenue impact of restoring NPS. If moving NPS from 45 back to 55 improves renewal by 10%, calculate the exact MRR saved. This turns "fixing NPS" from a soft goal into a hard revenue target.
*   **Investor Deck:** Present NRR as your leading indicator of product-market fit. Show that while MRR is the outcome, NRR is the engine. Acknowledge the NPS slip as a diagnosed risk to NRR that is now the primary operational focus.

Choosing NRR stops the false trade-off between growth and satisfaction. It dictates that you cannot have growth without satisfaction, satisfying both your founding team's split and your investors' need for durable scale.

*Note: Ensure your data pipeline accurately tracks contraction and churn by cohort to calculate NRR correctly; do not rely on aggregate MRR figures. If NRR data is currently unavailable, instrument it immediately as your highest priority event.*
