## Toughest objections

**Aisha Bello (CTO)**

1. **"20% faster" is a feeling, not a measurement.** Self-reported speed is vulnerable to novelty effects, enthusiasm among volunteers, and the tendency to credit the tool. Faster PRs also don't mean faster delivery if review, rework, or defect rates rise.
2. **Your pilot group isn't your company.** 25 of 220 engineers (about 11%) is a small sample, and likely volunteers or early adopters. Assuming it holds everywhere is the weakest link, and the embedded firmware group, with constrained toolchains, hardware-specific code, and tight memory and timing, was never tested.
3. **Quality and security costs are unmeasured.** Where are the data on bug rates, review burden, and AI-generated vulnerabilities? Speed gained at authoring can be lost at review.
4. **Cost versus a measured benefit.** $39 × 220 × 12 = **$102,960 per year**. The benefit case rests on an unverified 20%. Why all 220 at once rather than a phased rollout with a measured gate?
5. **Lock-in and vendor risk.** What happens if Lanternworks changes pricing, degrades, or disappears? What is the exit plan?
6. **Timing.** January 5 is under three months away, and your own legal dependency has no end date.

**Henrik Sand (General Counsel)**

1. **Data retention and training use are unknown.** This is likely a blocker. Until we know whether proprietary source code is retained or used to train models, rollout to everyone is unacceptable. "Not yet reviewed" means the proposal has a gating dependency with no timeline.
2. **Scope of exposure.** Source code may include secrets, customer data, third-party or open-source code under license constraints, and export-controlled material. Firmware may carry additional sensitivity.
3. **Contract terms.** What do the DPA, confidentiality, IP ownership of outputs, indemnification for infringing suggestions, breach notification, audit rights, subprocessors, and data residency say?
4. **Customer obligations.** Do any customer contracts restrict sending their code or data to third parties?
5. **The pilot may already be a problem.** What code did the 25 engineers send, under what terms, and is that exposure itself reviewed?
6. **Commitment before approval.** A fixed start date creates pressure to approve legal review under a deadline.

## Missing evidence

- **Measured outcomes**: PR cycle time, review time, rework, and defect rates for pilot engineers against a comparable control group or their own pre-pilot baseline.
- **Pilot representativeness**: how participants were chosen, their seniority and teams, and whether results vary by group.
- **Firmware data**: a small pilot or a technical assessment of Scribe on firmware code, or an explicit exclusion.
- **Legal findings**: written answers on retention, training use, ownership, indemnity, and customer-contract constraints, plus a review completion date.
- **Security review**: secret handling, access controls, logging, and exclusion of sensitive repos.
- **Cost and exit**: price protection beyond year one, volume discount options, cancellation terms, and data deletion on exit.
- **Adoption realism**: actual usage rates in the pilot, since licenses for 220 people only pay off if most use them.

## Recommendation

As written, I would not present this for approval. A stronger proposal: a phased rollout (expanded measured pilot of ~50–75 engineers, firmware excluded), with go/no-go criteria agreed up front, starting only **after** legal sign-off. Tie the January 5 date to that sign-off rather than the reverse.
