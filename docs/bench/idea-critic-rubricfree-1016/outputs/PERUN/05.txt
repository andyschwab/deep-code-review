## The three questions Elena will most likely ask

1. **"What is the 1.5x worth in dollars, and how do you know it applies to us?"**
   She will go straight to your central assumption. Be ready to say which retention metric it is (D30, D90 or paid retention), what that lift does to subscriber lifetime value, and what evidence you have beyond a competitor's remark. Expect the objection that people who install an app are already your most engaged users, so the lift may be selection rather than cause.

2. **"What does this cost net, once store fees and the team's time are counted?"**
   She will want 24 person-months at loaded cost, plus ongoing maintenance for two platforms. She will also want the fee hit on the 18% of new subscriptions that start on mobile, and what you give up by pulling 4 people off other work.

3. **"Why not improve the mobile web or ship a PWA first?"**
   72% of your sessions are already on mobile browsers. She will ask what an app does that a better mobile web experience can't (push notifications, offline lists, home-screen presence), and why you need two native builds rather than one cheaper test. Your parity-versus-subset question is tied to this, because a subset launch is the natural answer to "what's the smallest version that tests the claim?"

## Where your numbers are too thin

- **The 1.5x retention figure.** It is a single secondhand source with an unknown metric, cohort and definition. It is also the number the whole case rests on. Replace it with something you can defend:
  - Compare retention for your own users who have push enabled or a home-screen shortcut against those who don't.
  - Work out the break-even lift: how much retention improvement covers the cost? Then 1.5x becomes a hypothesis you test, not a premise.
- **The 18% figure.** Its denominator is unclear. The gap between 72% of sessions on mobile and only 18% of new subscriptions starting there suggests mobile web converts poorly. Elena may ask whether fixing that is cheaper than building apps. You also need to translate 18% into revenue flowing through the stores, since fee exposure depends on revenue, not subscription share. Check current Apple and Google terms and whether any web-checkout route is allowed; I can't confirm the rates.
- **Missing economics.** You give no ARPU, churn, conversion rate or loaded cost, so there is no payback period. Without one, "6 months, 4 people" has nothing to be compared against.
- **Sessions versus users.** 72% of sessions is not 72% of users who would install an app. Use the share of monthly active users on mobile and how often they return.
- **The timeline.** If approval comes on December 9, a 6-month build lands in mid-June with no buffer for store review, bugs or a parity decision. "Launch June 2027" is risky unless scope is fixed.
- **No stop condition.** Name a measurable checkpoint, for example a beta retention lift by a set date, below which you pause.

Decide parity versus subset before the review. Walking in undecided on scope will undermine the cost estimate.
