**Recommendation: Do not launch as currently modeled.** The plan relies on a dangerous assumption: that behavior under a recurring, unlimited-satisfaction subscription will mirror historical one-off guarantee usage. This is a classic "moral hazard" blind spot. You are incentivizing customers to use the service until they are bored, then claim dissatisfaction to reset the clock for free. The 2.8% refund rate will almost certainly explode, turning this into a loss leader rather than a profit center.

**Three Biggest Failure Modes:**

1.  **Refund Rate Explosion:** Under a subscription, "100% satisfaction" is subjective and recurring. Customers may abuse the guarantee to get free cleanings during high-need periods (holidays, parties) or simply rotate cleaners until they find one they like, then cancel. If the refund rate hits just 12% instead of 2.8%, your margin vanishes.
2.  **Labor Cost Underestimation:** You assumed a 4% operating cost rise. However, deep cleans are labor-intensive. If subscribers maximize their "three visits" allowance (which is likely given the fixed cost), your labor hours could surge by 20–30%, not 4%. At $13.50/hr, this destroys the unit economics before refunds even apply.
3.  **Competitor Price War:** If a competitor launches a similar guarantee at $39 or offers "four visits" for $49 within 60 days, your differentiation disappears. Since your model depends on high volume (15% conversion) to cover fixed costs, losing 5%+ of sign-ups to a copycat makes the program unsustainable.

**Specific Halt/Pivot Trigger:**
Halt or pivot the program immediately if **refund costs exceed 6% of subscription revenue for any single month** OR if **average labor hours per subscriber exceeds 2.5 visits/month for two consecutive months**. These metrics directly test the moral hazard and labor assumptions before they bleed cash for a full quarter.

*Note: Run a 4-week pilot in the North zone only, capping the guarantee at one refund per customer lifetime, before committing to a full rollout.*
