**The Call**
Adopt the outcome-based pricing model but reject a purely self-reported savings mechanism; instead, contractually bind the fee calculation to a third-party verified baseline or the client's own audited cloud billing data to preserve audit integrity.

**The Numbers**
*Formula:* Revenue = (Verified Baseline Spend − Actual Spend) × Fee %.
*User Figures:* Baseline $100k, Actual $80k, Savings $20k, Fee 20%.
*Result:* $4,000 monthly revenue per client.
*Assumptions (ASSUMED):*
1.  **Verification Method:** The "Verified Baseline" is defined as the average of the prior 3 months' actual bills, not a theoretical projection (Low risk). If based on a theoretical "ideal state" calculated by your tool alone, audit risk spikes (High risk).
2.  **Volume:** Closing 5 of 15 pilots.
3.  **Unit Economics:** At $4k/month/client, 5 clients yield $20k MRR. If your burn rate exceeds $20k/month, you remain unprofitable even with these deals.

**What Flips It, and the Trigger**
The single input that changes the call is the **definition of "Verified Savings."** If the contract allows CloudSaver to unilaterally calculate the baseline savings without external validation, auditors will likely flag this as a conflict of interest where revenue recognition depends on management estimates prone to bias.
*Trigger:* If a pilot customer's finance team refuses to sign off on the baseline calculation method within the SOW (Statement of Work) phase, do not proceed to a full enterprise contract.
*Rule of Thumb:* Revenue recognition standards (like ASC 606) generally require variable consideration to be constrained until it is probable that a significant reversal will not occur. Self-calculated savings often fail this "constraint" test.
*Step this week:* Draft a "Baseline Definition" clause for your next pilot that ties savings strictly to line-item reductions in the official AWS/Azure/GCP invoice compared to a fixed historical average, then ask the pilot's CFO to sign off on that specific math before work begins.
