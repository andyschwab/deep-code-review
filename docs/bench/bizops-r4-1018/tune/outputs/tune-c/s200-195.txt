**The Analysis (Tier 2: Legal Framework & Risk Factors)**
While "educational purpose" is a factor in fair use analysis, it is rarely a shield for commercial entities selling subscriptions. Courts weigh four specific factors, and your current model fails the two most critical ones:

1.  **Purpose and Character of Use:** You are operating a for-profit business ($8,000/month revenue). Using copyrighted characters to drive sign-ups is a commercial exploitation, not a nonprofit educational activity. The "transformative" argument is weak when the characters are used to demonstrate techniques rather than to critique or parody the characters themselves.
2.  **Effect on the Market:** This is often the deciding factor. By using these characters to attract users, you are potentially usurping the market for official tutorials or licensed merchandise that the rights holders (likely major studios) could develop. The 20% spike in sign-ups proves these characters are the draw, directly impacting the owner's potential licensing revenue.

**Potential Damages (Illustrative Figures)**
If a rights holder pursues action, damages are not limited to your profits. In the US, statutory damages for willful copyright infringement can reach **$150,000 per work**.
*   **Scenario:** If they claim infringement on 3 tutorials containing 2 distinct characters each (6 works total).
*   **Low-end estimate (non-willful):** $750 per work × 6 = $4,500.
*   **High-end estimate (willful):** $150,000 per work × 6 = **$900,000**.
*   **Your Exposure:** Even a conservative settlement demand would likely exceed your entire annual revenue (~$96,000), potentially bankrupting the operation. There are numerous precedents where small educational or fan sites were sued successfully despite "educational" claims because the underlying business model was commercial.

**What Flips the Risk & The Trigger**
The single input that changes this from "high risk" to "managed risk" is **licensing**. Without a written license, you are exposed.
*   **Trigger:** If you receive a Cease and Desist letter, your liability clock starts ticking immediately.
*   **Rule of Thumb:** If the character is recognizable and owned by a major studio (Disney, Viacom, etc.), assume they enforce aggressively. Do not wait for a letter.

**Immediate Action Plan**
1.  **Stop:** Remove the tutorials featuring the duck and sponge characters immediately.
2.  **Replace:** Re-record those specific lessons using original characters or generic archetypes.
3.  **Audit:** Review all other content for similar unlicensed assets.
