**The Call:** Your current TAM calculation is significantly inflated because it counts potential *seats* as unique *customers*; institutional investors expect a bottom-up TAM based on "paying entities" (teams or companies), not individual developer seats, especially given your 30% multi-stack overlap.

**The Numbers:**
Your current top-down logic: $2.5\text{M developers} \times \$40/\text{mo} \times 12 = \$1.2\text{B}$.
This assumes 1:1 mapping of developer to subscription. However, your data shows 30% of users span multiple stacks. In B2B developer tools, the buying unit is rarely the individual developer; it is the engineering team or organization.

*Formula for Bottom-Up TAM:*
$\text{TAM} = (\text{Target Companies}) \times (\text{Avg. Seats per Company}) \times (\text{Annual Price per Seat})$

Since you lack the "Target Companies" count, we must adjust your current figure using your overlap data as a proxy for seat consolidation.
*   **Assumption (Base):** If 30% of developers are double-counted across stacks, the effective unique *seat* count drops. However, the bigger distortion is treating every developer as a separate buyer.
*   **Illustrative Adjustment:** If your 2.5M figure represents "developer instances" and 30% are redundant due to multi-stack work, a naive unique seat adjustment yields $2.5\text{M} \times 0.70 = 1.75\text{M}$ seats.
*   **Realistic B2B Adjustment:** Investors will argue that one company buys one subscription covering multiple seats. If the average customer has 5 developers (a common small-team metric), your 2.5M developers represent only 500k *potential customers*.
*   **Revised TAM Estimate:** $500,000 \text{ companies} \times \$480/\text{year} = \$240\text{M}$.

*Unit Economics Check:*
With 5,000 customers and CAC of $120$, your early traction is valid. The issue is not your product fit, but the market definition. A $1.2B TAM based on individual seats suggests a B2C model, which conflicts with a $120 CAC (typical for B2B sales cycles).

**What Flips It, and The Trigger:**
The input that changes the call is the **Average Seats Per Paying Organization**.
*   **Trigger:** If your average customer has >10 seats, your TAM approaches the $500M+ range; if <3 seats, you are in a fragmented SMB market closer to $150M.
*   **Action:** Calculate your current "Seats per Customer" metric from your 5,000 payers immediately.
*   **Rule of Thumb:** If Seats/Customer > 4, pitch the "Team/Enterprise" TAM. If < 2, pitch the "Prosumer" TAM but justify the $120 CAC.
*   **This Week:** Segment your 5,000 customers by number of connected repositories/users. If the median is >1 stack/user, your pricing page likely needs a "Team" tier to capture the value of that overlap, shifting your TAM narrative from "per dev" to "per org."
