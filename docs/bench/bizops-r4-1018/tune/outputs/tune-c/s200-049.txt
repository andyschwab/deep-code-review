**The call:** Do not include the opportunity cost of aging inventory in your cash burn or runway calculation for your Series A deck; it artificially shortens your runway and misrepresents your actual liquidity risk. Your co-founder's approach conflates economic profit with cash solvency. Investors care about when you run out of cash, not when you theoretically lose value on idle assets. Present the standard cash runway (40 months) as your primary metric, and discuss the inventory cycle separately as a working capital characteristic of your business model.

**The numbers:**
*   **Standard Cash Runway Formula:** Cash Balance / Monthly Operating Burn
*   **Your Figures:** $1,200,000 / $30,000 = **40 months**.
*   **Co-founder's Proposed Adjustment (Opportunity Cost):**
    *   Inventory Value: $500,000
    *   Annual Opportunity Cost Rate: 8%
    *   Annual Implicit Cost: $500,000 × 0.08 = $40,000
    *   Monthly Implicit Cost: $40,000 / 12 ≈ $3,333
    *   *Hypothetical* Adjusted Burn: $30,000 + $3,333 = $33,333
    *   *Hypothetical* Adjusted Runway: $1,200,000 / $33,333 ≈ **36 months**.

**Assumptions:**
*   **Operating Burn:** Assumes the $30k includes all cash outflows (rent, labor, utilities) but excludes non-cash items like depreciation.
*   **Inventory Status:** Assumes the $500k is fully paid for and sits on the balance sheet, requiring no further cash outflow until bottling/packaging costs (which should be included in the $30k if recurring).
*   **Opportunity Rate:** The 8% is an assumed cost of capital, not a cash expense.

**What flips it, and the trigger:**
The only input that changes the call is if the inventory requires significant *additional* cash outflows to complete (e.g., expensive bottling, labeling, or distribution costs not yet incurred). If the cash required to convert that $500k inventory into sellable goods exceeds your current monthly burn projections, you must model those specific future cash outflows.
*   **Trigger:** If total estimated completion costs > $100,000, revise the burn rate to include the monthly average of these future costs.
*   **Rule of Thumb:** Runway measures liquidity (cash left / cash out), not economic efficiency.
*   **Step this week:** If your inventory completion costs are high, create a separate "Working Capital Needs" slide showing the cash required to bottle the current stock, rather than distorting the core burn rate.

**Routing note:**
While this is a strategic finance question, ensure your financial statements correctly classify the 18-month aging stock as "Long-term Inventory" or "Work in Progress" under US GAAP. A CPA should verify that your capitalization of costs into inventory (vs. expensing) aligns with tax regulations for manufacturing/agriculture to avoid audit flags during due diligence.

Including implicit costs makes you look inefficient because it suggests you don't understand the difference between cash flow and accounting profit. Sophisticated planning is shown by accurately forecasting the cash needed to bottle and sell the inventory, not by inventing expenses.
