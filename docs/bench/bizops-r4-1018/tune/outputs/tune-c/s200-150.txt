**The Call:** Do not pivot to an insurance-heavy model yet; the math shows a 60% revenue collapse if you simply swap rates while losing half your base. Instead, adopt a hybrid "good-better-best" packaging strategy immediately: retain a premium cash tier for high-touch/long-duration care (tier 1) while introducing a limited, lower-margin insurance track strictly for volume stabilization, provided your unit economics on the insurance side remain positive after administrative overhead.

**The Numbers:**
*Current Monthly Revenue:* 400 patients × $150 = **$60,000**.
*Scenario A (50% Churn to Insurance):*
Assumption: 200 patients stay cash; 200 switch to insurance at $60/session.
Formula: $(200 \times \$150) + (200 \times \$60) = \$30,000 + \$12,000 = \mathbf{\$42,000}$.
*Result:* A **30% total revenue drop** despite retaining the patient count.

*Scenario B (Volume Compensation Required):*
To recover the lost $18,000 solely via insurance volume at $60/session:
Formula: $\$18,000 / \$60 = \mathbf{300}$ *additional* insurance sessions needed.
*Result:* You must grow total monthly sessions from 400 to 700 (75% growth) just to break even with current revenue, ignoring increased administrative costs of billing insurance.

*Unknown Input (Elasticity):* The willingness of cancer patients to pay a $90/session premium ($150 vs $60) is the critical variable. Without survey data, assume a "thin" market where only patients valuing time/concierge service > cost will stay cash.

**What Flips It, and the Trigger:**
The flip input is the **administrative burden cost per insurance claim**. If your staff time to bill/collect $60 exceeds $25 per session, the insurance channel destroys value compared to cash.
*Trigger Rule:* If net collection per insurance session (after labor) falls below $45, cap insurance intake at 20% of capacity.
*This Week's Step:* Run a Van Westendorp price-sensitivity survey with your top 50 cash patients asking: "At what price would our premium sessions be too expensive?" and "At what price would they be a bargain?" to quantify the cash premium elasticity before signing payer contracts.

However, if you accept insurance for *any* patient, federal anti-kickback statutes and state consumer protection laws may restrict how you steer patients toward cash upgrades.
