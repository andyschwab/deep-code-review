**What Flips It, and the Trigger:**
The single input that changes the regulatory classification is the **specific wording of the output recommendations**.
*   **Trigger:** If your algorithm uses prescriptive language (e.g., "You should take," "This prevents," "Treatment for") rather than descriptive language (e.g., "Studies suggest," "Associated with," "Informational only"), regulators will likely classify it as medical advice.
*   **Rule of Thumb:** Wellness tools provide data for user consideration; medical devices provide diagnoses or treatment plans. The moment your output tells a user *what to do* regarding a health condition based on genetic data, you cross into medical advice.
*   **Step this week:** If your current reports contain any imperative verbs regarding health actions, consider pausing sales and engaging counsel to rewrite the output engine before processing report #2,001.

**Regulatory Criteria and Penalties (Tier 2 Explanation):**
Regulators (such as the FDA in the US or equivalent bodies in the EU/UK) generally distinguish between wellness and medical advice using a "intended use" and "risk of harm" test.
*   **Criteria:** They examine whether the product claims to diagnose, cure, mitigate, treat, or prevent a disease. Genetic data linked to specific disease risks (e.g., BRCA mutations for cancer) is heavily scrutinized. Even with a disclaimer, if the *function* of the tool is to guide medical decisions, the disclaimer may be insufficient.
*   **Usual Treatment:** Companies offering "wellness" reports typically restrict outputs to non-clinical traits (e.g., ancestry, taste preference) or provide raw data without interpretation. Once interpretation links genes to disease risk or lifestyle changes for disease prevention, it often triggers Medical Device regulations or laws against practicing medicine without a license.
*   **Potential Penalties:** If deemed to be practicing medicine without a license or selling an unapproved medical device, penalties can include:
    *   Cease and desist orders shutting down operations.
    *   Significant fines (often per violation/user).
    *   Civil liability for any harm caused by incorrect advice (malpractice suits).
    *   Criminal charges in extreme cases of negligence.
