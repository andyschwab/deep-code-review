**The Call**
It is not impossible to use FIFO for unique, sporadic inventory; you can structure your records to satisfy an audit by treating each piece as a distinct lot with a specific acquisition date and cost, rather than trying to average them into bulk categories. The reliability of your Cost of Goods Sold (COGS) depends entirely on maintaining a granular ledger that links every sold item directly to its specific purchase event, which is standard practice for high-mix, low-volume retailers like estate sale resellers.

**The Numbers**
Your current unit economics show a healthy margin, but the accounting precision relies on tracking individual costs, not averages.
*   **Formula:** `Gross Profit = (Units Sold × Average Sale Price) - Sum of Specific Costs for Those Units`
*   **Your Figures:**
    *   Units Acquired: 450
    *   Units Sold: 320
    *   Average Sale Price: $450
    *   Total Revenue: $144,000 (320 × $450)
    *   Cost Range: $20 – $800 per item
*   **Assumptions (Labelled ASSUMED for illustration):**
    *   *ASSUMED Average Cost of Goods Sold:* Since you sold 320 of 450 items, and costs vary wildly, we cannot know the exact COGS without your ledger. However, if we *assume* a weighted average cost of $200 per item for the sold mix:
    *   *Illustrative COGS:* $64,000 (320 × $200)
    *   *Illustrative Gross Profit:* $80,000 ($144,000 - $64,000)
    *   *Illustrative Gross Margin:* ~55.6%
*   **Result Sensitivity:** If your actual average cost of the sold items was higher (e.g., you sold the expensive pieces first under FIFO), say $300, your profit drops to $48,000. If you sold cheaper pieces first, profit rises. The specific identification method (tracking each item's actual cost) eliminates this guessing game and is often preferred for unique goods over strict FIFO.

**What Flips It, and the Trigger**
The one input that changes the audit outcome is **traceability**. An auditor will flag your accounts not because you used FIFO, but if you cannot produce a document linking a specific sold chair to its specific $150 estate sale receipt.
*   **Trigger:** If you cannot match >95% of sold items to a specific purchase date and cost within 48 hours of an auditor's request, your current method is unreliable.
*   **Rule of Thumb:** For unique items, "Specific Identification" is often more accurate and acceptable than FIFO, as it matches the actual flow of goods.
*   **Step This Week:** If you are currently averaging costs or grouping items, consider implementing a simple SKU system where every item acquired gets a unique ID tagged with its exact purchase price and date before it hits the sales floor.

**Routing Note**
While Specific Identification is generally the standard for unique goods like art or custom furniture, confirming whether Oregon state tax authorities or your specific investor requires strict FIFO versus Specific Identification is a binding accounting determination; bring your proposed ledger format to a CPA to validate it meets GAAP standards for your specific volume.
