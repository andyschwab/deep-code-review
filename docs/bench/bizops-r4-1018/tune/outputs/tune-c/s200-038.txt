**The Call**
Use your specific historical data (2%) as the base case for your financial model, but explicitly model a "conservative stress case" using the industry average (5%) to demonstrate risk awareness. Investors view company-specific traction as more credible than generic benchmarks for a hardware startup with 10,000 units sold; however, relying solely on the lower number without sensitivity analysis signals naivety about scaling risks.

**The Numbers**
*Formula:* Warranty Reserve = (Total Units Sold) × (Estimated Failure Rate) × (Cost Per Repair)

*Scenario A: Historical Data (Base Case)*
- Inputs: 10,000 units × 2% failure × $100 repair
- **Reserve Liability:** $20,000
- *Assumption:* Your quality control processes remain constant as you scale.

*Scenario B: Industry Average (Conservative Case)*
- Inputs: 10,000 units × 5% failure × $100 repair
- **Reserve Liability:** $50,000
- *Assumption:* Your product performance degrades to the sector mean under mass production or extreme conditions.

*Impact on Valuation:*
The difference is $30,000 in immediate liability. While this reduces current equity value slightly, the $50,000 reserve provides a $30,000 cash-flow buffer if failures spike.

**What Flips It, and The Trigger**
The deciding factor is **statistical significance relative to scaling**. Your sample of 10,000 units is generally large enough to be statistically significant for early-stage modeling (margin of error approx. ±0.3% at 95% confidence), making your 2% data superior to a generic 5% guess.
*Trigger:* If your next production run exceeds 5,000 units *and* involves a new supplier or material change, revert to the 5% industry assumption for those specific new units until new data arrives.
*Rule of Thumb:* Use historical data for proven SKUs; use industry averages for new designs or unproven supply chains.
*This Week's Step:* If your failure rate creeps above 2.5% in the next quarter, consider tightening quality assurance protocols before the next fundraise.

**Accounting Principles & Investor Credibility**
The guiding principle is **GAAP ASC 450 (Contingencies)**, which requires losses to be accrued when they are "probable" and "reasonably estimable." For pre-profit companies, the standard is not "conservatism" in the sense of picking the worst-case number, but "faithful representation."
- **Credibility:** Investors prefer a model grounded in your actuals (2%) because it proves you have product-market fit and quality control. Using the 5% industry average arbitrarily implies you don't trust your own engineering.
- **Strategy:** Present the 2% reserve on the P&L to show healthy margins, but include a footnote or sensitivity tab showing the impact of a 5% failure rate. This shows you understand the downside without penalizing your current valuation unnecessarily.
- **Sample Size:** 10,000 units is rarely considered "insignificant" in hardware startups unless the failures are clustered in a specific batch. If the 200 failures are random, your data is robust.

**Routing Note**
While ASC 450 provides the framework, the final determination of "probable and estimable" for your specific audit and SEC filings (if applicable later) is a Tier 3 binding decision; confirm your reserve methodology and disclosure language with a CPA specializing in hardware inventory before finalizing the Series A data room.
