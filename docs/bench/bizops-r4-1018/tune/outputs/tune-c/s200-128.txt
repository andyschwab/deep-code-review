**The Call**
Pass the 8% tax to the consumer in New York immediately; absorbing it destroys unit economics and creates an unsustainable subsidy from rural bookings to urban ones. Do not absorb the cost unless urban cancellation rates exceed 15% in a controlled test.

**The Numbers**
*Current Monthly Gross Booking Value (GBV):* 400 bookings × $1,200 = $480,000.
*Current Monthly Revenue (12% commission):* $57,600.

*Scenario A: Absorb the Tax (Margin Hit)*
- Urban GBV: 240 bookings × $1,200 = $288,000.
- Tax Liability (8%): $23,040.
- **New Urban Revenue:** $28,800 (commission) - $23,040 (tax) = **$5,760**.
- Rural Revenue (unchanged): 160 bookings × $1,200 × 12% = $23,040.
- **Total New Revenue:** $28,800.
- **Revenue Loss:** $57,600 - $28,800 = **$28,800 per month (50% drop)**.

*Scenario B: Pass to Consumer (Volume Risk)*
- New Urban Price: $1,200 + ($1,200 × 8%) = $1,296.
- Break-even Volume Drop: To match the $5,760 revenue from Scenario A while keeping full margin, you can only afford a ~80% drop in volume (mathematically impossible to lose money vs absorbing, unless volume hits zero).
- *Assumption (UNVERIFIED):* If price elasticity causes a 10% drop in urban bookings (216 bookings), revenue is $216 × $1,296 × 12% = ~$33,592. This is still **$27,832 higher** than absorbing the tax.

**What Flips It, and the Trigger**
The single input that changes this call is the **price elasticity of demand** (cancellation/conversion drop) in the urban market.
- *Trigger:* If passing the tax causes urban booking volume to drop by more than **83%** (reducing 240 bookings to <41), only then does absorbing the tax become mathematically preferable.
- *Rule of Thumb:* Most travel services see <20% volume drop on single-digit tax increases.
- *This Week's Step:* If your historical data shows price sensitivity >15%, consider a temporary 4% subsidy (splitting the tax) while A/B testing the full pass-through, but do not commit to full absorption.
