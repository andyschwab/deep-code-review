**The Call**
Do not claim your device "helps allergy sufferers" or "removes 99% of pollen/dander" in marketing materials yet; this specific phrasing creates a high risk of your product being classified as a Class II medical device by the FDA, which would legally require you to halt sales until you obtain 510(k) clearance. To stay in the "general wellness" or consumer appliance lane, pivot your messaging to focus on "air quality," "dust reduction," and "particulate removal" without referencing medical conditions (allergies) or specific biological agents (pollen/pet dander) as the primary benefit.

**The Numbers & Regulatory Thresholds**
*   **Current Unit Economics:**
    *   Price: $120
    *   COGS: $45
    *   Gross Margin: $75 per unit (62.5%)
    *   Total Gross Profit (1,000 units): $75,000
*   **The Regulatory Cost Trigger (ASSUMED):**
    *   **Scenario A (Consumer Claim):** Marketing stays general. Cost = $0 additional regulatory spend. Sales continue.
    *   **Scenario B (Medical Claim):** Marketing targets allergies.
        *   Estimated 510(k) Preparation & Testing: $15,000 – $50,000 (Low/High Assumption).
        *   FDA User Fee (Small Business): ~$5,000 (UNVERIFIED, varies annually).
        *   Time to Clearance: 3–9 months (ASSUMED).
        *   **Result:** You must stop sales immediately upon making the claim if challenged, or risk seizure/injunction. The $75,000 profit you have generated is insufficient to fund a robust clinical trial if the FDA demands one for a novel claim.

**What Flips It, and The Trigger**
The single input that changes the call is the **presence of disease/condition keywords** (e.g., "allergy," "asthma," "hay fever") linked to the product's function.
*   **Trigger Rule:** If your marketing copy answers "What health problem does this solve?" with a specific medical condition, you are likely a medical device. If it answers "What does this clean?" with generic particulates (dust, smoke, odors), you are likely a consumer appliance.
*   **Stop/Pivot Date:** Effective immediately for any new ad copy or packaging updates. Do not print new boxes or launch ads with the "allergy" language until counsel reviews the exact phrasing.
*   **Actionable Step:** If you want to target allergy sufferers without triggering the FDA, consider phrasing like "Reduces airborne particulates commonly associated with seasonal discomfort" only after a lawyer confirms this specific "structure/function" claim does not cross into disease treatment under current FDA guidance documents for air purifiers.
