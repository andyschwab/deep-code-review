**What Flips It, and The Trigger:**
The trigger is the **input data** used in your algorithm. Even if you charge the *homes* (B2B), courts look at the *effect* on the *tenants* (B2C). If your algorithm charges higher fees for homes in zip codes with high minority populations, or lowers fees for homes that historically reject protected classes to incentivize those listings, you have created a **disparate impact**.
*   **The Flip Input:** Any correlation between your fee variable (price) and a protected class characteristic (race, color, religion, sex, disability, familial status, national origin).
*   **Stop Trigger:** If your pricing logic includes **Zip Code**, **Demographic Data**, **School District Ratings**, or **Historical Acceptance Rates** broken down by tenant type, stop immediately. These are proxies for protected classes.
*   **Rule of Thumb:** If a variable can be mapped to a demographic group, it is toxic for pricing in housing.
*   **This Week's Step:** If you have a draft list of pricing variables, run a correlation check against census demographic data; if any variable shows >5% correlation with a protected class, consider stripping it entirely before legal review.

**Data Points to Avoid:**
To stay safe, your pricing logic must strictly exclude:
1.  **Geographic proxies:** Zip codes, neighborhood names, or school district boundaries (often correlate with race/income).
2.  **Tenant demographics:** Any data regarding the race, family size, disability status, or source of income (e.g., Section 8) of the tenants the home serves.
3.  **Historical bias data:** Past acceptance/rejection rates if those rates were influenced by human bias.

**Why Algorithms Create Disparate Impact:**
Algorithms optimize for efficiency. If "high demand" in your model is defined by neighborhoods that are historically white or wealthy, the algorithm will automatically assign higher fees to homes in those areas. Conversely, it might lower fees for homes in marginalized areas to stimulate supply. While this looks like smart economics, the law views this as steering or discriminating based on location, which is a proxy for race. The intent (profit) does not matter; the outcome (discriminatory effect) is what violates the law.
