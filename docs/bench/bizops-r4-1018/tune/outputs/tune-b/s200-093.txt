**The Call:** Your current TAM calculation is significantly inflated because it counts potential *seats* as unique *customers*; institutional investors expect a bottom-up TAM based on "paying entities" (teams or companies), not individual developers, which automatically resolves the multi-stack overlap issue.

**The Numbers:**
*   **Current Flawed TAM:** 2.5M developers × $40/mo × 12 = **$1.2 Billion**.
*   **The Overlap Factor:** You noted 30% of users connect multiple stacks. If you count "seats per language," you risk double-counting this segment entirely. However, the larger distortion is assuming 1 developer = 1 customer.
*   **Corrected Approach (Bottom-Up):**
    *   *Formula:* (Total Target Companies) × (Average Seats per Company) × (Annual Price).
    *   *Assumption (Base):* If your 5,000 customers represent an average team size of 10 developers (common for tools selling to engineering orgs), your 2.5M developer pool represents only **250,000 potential companies**.
    *   *Revised TAM:* 250,000 companies × $40/mo × 12 = **$120 Million**.
    *   *Sensitivity:* If average team size is 5 devs, TAM = $240M. If 20 devs, TAM = $60M.
*   **Unit Economics Check:** With a CAC of $120 and Price of $480/year, your payback period is 3 months (assuming 100% gross margin). This is strong, but investors will scrutinize if that $120 CAC acquires a single developer seat or an entire team contract.

**What Flips It, and The Trigger:**
The input that changes the verdict is the **definition of your "Customer."**
*   *Trigger:* If your sales motion targets individual developers (PLG) vs. Engineering VPs (Enterprise).
*   *Rule of Thumb:* If >50% of your revenue comes from companies with >5 seats, your TAM must be calculated by *company count*, not developer count.
*   *Action:* If your average contract value (ACV) is >$500/year, recalculate TAM using the number of software development firms in North America (approx. 100k–150k) multiplied by a penetrable seat count, rather than the total developer population.

**Industry Standard Methodology:**
Do not adjust for "multi-stack developers" by reducing the user count; instead, change the unit of analysis.
1.  **Avoid "Per Language" Counting:** Never sell or count seats per language unless your tool is strictly language-gated. This creates friction and inflates churn risk.
2.  **The "Seat" vs. "Customer" Distinction:** Investors know one developer uses multiple stacks. They do not buy "developer TAM"; they buy "Enterprise TAM."
    *   *Top-Down:* Total software spend in NA × % allocated to DevTools.
    *   *Bottom-Up (Preferred):* Number of target companies × Average Revenue Per Account (ARPA).
3.  **Presentation Strategy:** Present your TAM as: "There are X thousand engineering teams in North America. At an average ARPA of $Y, our serviceable obtainable market is $Z." This bypasses the double-counting error entirely because a company is a unique entity regardless of how many stacks its employees use.
