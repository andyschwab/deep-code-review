**The Call**
Treat the $350 last-mile logistics cost as **Cost of Goods Sold (COGS)**. Classifying it as Sales & Marketing (S&M) inflates your gross margin artificially and obscures the true unit economics of your "white-glove" promise, leading to dangerous scalability assumptions for your next hire plan.

**The Numbers**
*Formula:* Contribution Margin = Revenue − Variable COGS (including fulfillment).
*Your Figures:*
- Revenue per unit: $2,500
- Logistics cost: $350 (Variable, incurred per unit sold)
- Industry benchmark (standard freight): $150

*Scenario A (Correct: Logistics in COGS)*
- Gross Profit = $2,500 − $350 − [Other Product Costs]
- Gross Margin % = (Gross Profit / $2,500)
- *Result:* Your margin reflects the actual cost to deliver the promised value. If other product costs are $1,000, Gross Profit is $1,150 (46% margin).

*Scenario B (Incorrect: Logistics in S&M)*
- Gross Profit = $2,500 − [Other Product Costs]
- Gross Margin % = (Gross Profit / $2,500)
- *Result:* Using the same $1,000 product cost, Gross Profit appears as $1,500 (60% margin).
- *Distortion:* You overstate margin by 14 percentage points. This hides the fact that your fulfillment model is $200/unit more expensive than the industry average, making the business look more scalable than it is.

**What Flips It, and the Trigger**
The only input that changes this classification is if the delivery becomes a fixed overhead (e.g., you buy a fleet and pay drivers salaries regardless of volume). Currently, it is variable.
*Trigger Rule:* As long as the cost scales 1:1 with units sold, it is COGS.
*Decision Threshold:* If your blended gross margin (after including the $350) drops below 40%, your current pricing ($2,500) may not support the white-glove model at scale without raising prices or reducing logistics costs.
*This Week's Step:* If your recalculated gross margin (Revenue minus all variable product and fulfillment costs) is below 45%, consider running a Van Westendorp price sensitivity survey to test if customers will pay $2,700+ to cover the premium delivery gap.

**Routing Note**
While US GAAP (ASC 606) generally requires fulfillment costs necessary to transfer control of the good to be capitalized into COGS, the specific presentation in your investor deck and the tax implications of inventory capitalization in California should be reviewed by a CPA before finalizing your audit or raising a priced round.
