**The Call**
For IRS tax reporting, you must generally capitalize the $12,500 accreditation fee and amortize it over the 12-month benefit period, rather than expensing it immediately. While immediate expensing improves your internal cash-flow view, it creates a mismatch between revenue and expenses that the IRS typically disallows for multi-period benefits, increasing audit risk.

**The Numbers**
The treatment changes your reported quarterly profit but not your cash balance.
*Formula:* Quarterly Expense = (Monthly Amortization × 3) OR (Full Fee if expensed).
*User Figures:* Annual Fee = $12,500; Benefit Period = 12 months.
*Assumption:* The fee provides rights lasting exactly 12 months starting January 1.

**What Flips It, and the Trigger**
The deciding input is the **duration of the benefit**. Under the IRS "12-Month Rule" (Treas. Reg. § 1.263(a)-4(f)), you can only expense immediately if the benefit does not extend beyond the earlier of 12 months or the end of the tax year following the payment year.
*Trigger:* If your accreditation renewal date slips such that the benefit period extends **more than 12 months** from the payment date (or past the end of the next tax year), capitalization is strictly mandatory.
*Rule of Thumb:* If the right lasts >12 months, you must capitalize.
*Action:* If your renewal cycle is exactly 12 months, prepare the amortization schedule now; if you previously expensed it, consider filing an amendment to capitalize to avoid audit flags.

**Routing Note**
While the general rule favors amortization for 12-month benefits, the specific application to "professional accreditation fees" versus "licensing" can vary by entity structure (S-Corp vs. LLC) and state conformity. Bring the question "Does the 12-month rule under Reg. 1.263(a)-4(f) definitively apply to our specific nursing board accreditation certificate, or is it considered a permanent license requiring longer amortization?" to your CPA before filing.
