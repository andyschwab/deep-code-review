**The Call:** Treat foot-scan data as **special category biometric data** under GDPR Article 9, requiring explicit, separate consent (not buried in Terms of Service) and a Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA) before launch; do not assume local EU storage is mandatory, but ensure any cross-border transfer has valid legal mechanisms.

**The Numbers & Compliance Logic:**
Your unit economics (£180 price, £70 cost, £110 margin) are healthy, but a GDPR violation carries fines up to €20M or 4% of global turnover, dwarfing your £50k dev budget.
*   **Data Classification:** Under GDPR Article 4(14), biometric data is "personal data resulting from specific technical processing... allowing unique identification." Foot geometry for bespoke manufacturing creates a unique identifier. Because you are processing this for commercial personalization (not strictly "necessary for performance of a contract" in the eyes of most EU regulators when less intrusive options exist), it triggers **Article 9** prohibitions unless an exception applies. The safest, standard path is **explicit consent**.
*   **Consent Mechanism:** Standard Terms of Service are insufficient. You need a granular, opt-in checkbox specifically for "Biometric Data Processing" that explains the purpose, separate from the general account creation.
*   **Storage Location:** GDPR does not mandate data *storage* within the EU. It mandates that the *protection level* travels with the data. If you store data outside the EU (e.g., US clouds), you must implement **Standard Contractual Clauses (SCCs)** and verify the recipient's security measures. However, given your "bespoke" claim, marketing "EU-hosted data" may be a competitive advantage.
*   **Retention:** There is no fixed statutory number of days. The rule is "storage limitation": keep data only as long as necessary to fulfill the specific purpose (manufacturing the shoe). Once the shoe is delivered and the return window closes, the biometric template should be deleted unless the user explicitly consents to retention for future orders.

**What Flips It, and The Trigger:**
The single input that changes your risk profile is **whether you can argue "contractual necessity."**
*   *Trigger:* If a supervisory authority (like the UK ICO or French CNIL) determines that a foot scan is *strictly necessary* to fulfill the shoe contract (i.e., you cannot make the shoe without it), you might bypass Article 9 explicit consent requirements, relying instead on Article 6(1)(b).
*   *Pivot Rule:* If you offer a manual measurement alternative (e.g., "upload a tracing" or "visit a partner store"), you destroy the "necessity" argument, making explicit Article 9 consent mandatory.
*   *Action this week:* If you cannot remove the manual alternative, **draft a standalone Biometric Consent form** and schedule a DPIA. Do not write a single line of database schema until the DPIA identifies your lawful basis.
