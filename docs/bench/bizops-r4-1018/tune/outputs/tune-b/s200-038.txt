**The Call**
Use your specific historical data (2%) as the base case for your financial model, but explicitly model a sensitivity scenario using the industry average (5%) to demonstrate risk awareness. Investors view company-specific empirical data as more credible than generic industry benchmarks for a hardware startup with 10,000 units sold, provided you disclose the sample size and variance. Over-reserving based on industry averages artificially depresses your current valuation and suggests a lack of confidence in your product quality, while under-reserving without disclosure risks credibility.

**The Numbers**
*Formula:* Warranty Reserve = (Total Units Sold) × (Estimated Failure Rate) × (Cost Per Repair)

*Scenario A: Historical Data (Base Case)*
- Inputs: 10,000 units × 2% failure × $100 repair
- **Reserve Liability:** $20,000
- *Assumption:* Your manufacturing quality control remains consistent with the historical period.

*Scenario B: Industry Average (Conservative Stress Test)*
- Inputs: 10,000 units × 5% failure × $100 repair
- **Reserve Liability:** $50,000
- *Assumption:* Your product performance degrades to match the broader market average.

*Impact Analysis:*
- Difference in Liability: $30,000
- Impact on Gross Margin (at $450 price): The difference represents roughly 0.67% of total revenue ($4.5M). While the absolute dollar amount is manageable, the *methodology* signals your operational maturity.

**What Flips It, and The Trigger**
The deciding factor is **statistical significance and trend direction**. Your sample size of 10,000 units is generally considered statistically significant for early-stage hardware; however, if your failure rate shows an *increasing* trend in the most recent 1,000 units (e.g., rising from 2% to 3.5%), you must pivot to a blended rate or the higher industry average.
- *Trigger Rule:* If the failure rate in the last quarter exceeds 3%, update the base case model to reflect the higher rate immediately.
- *Actionable Step:* This week, segment your failure data by production batch. If the newest batches show no degradation, present the 2% rate with a footnote disclosing the 5% industry stress test.

**Accounting Principles & Investor Credibility**
The guiding principle is **GAAP ASC 450 (Contingencies)**, which requires losses to be accrued when they are "probable" and "reasonably estimable." For pre-profit companies, the standard emphasizes using the best available evidence.
1.  **Credibility:** Investors prefer specific data over averages. Using the 5% industry rate without justification implies your product is no better than the average, ignoring your competitive advantage (extreme weather design).
2.  **Conservatism vs. Accuracy:** Conservatism does not mean arbitrarily choosing the highest number; it means ensuring liabilities are not understated. Presenting the 2% base with a clear "what-if" analysis for 5% demonstrates sophisticated financial modeling better than simply booking the higher reserve.
3.  **Sample Size:** 10,000 units is sufficient to establish a pattern unless the product design recently changed. If the design changed, historical data becomes less relevant, and industry averages (or new testing data) become the primary estimator.

**Routing Note**
While the calculation methodology is a modeling decision, the final determination of the reserve amount for your audited financial statements and the specific language used in your Series A disclosure schedules regarding "probable" losses is a binding accounting and legal determination; consult your CPA and securities counsel before finalizing the investor deck.
