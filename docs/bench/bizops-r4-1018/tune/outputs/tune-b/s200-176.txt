**The Call:** Your donation and grant revenue is generally tax-exempt if you hold 501(c)(3) status, but the $12,000 interest is likely taxable "Unrelated Business Income" (UBI) if it exceeds specific thresholds, and corporate sponsorships require careful structuring to avoid being classified as taxable advertising revenue. You must file an annual information return (Form 990 series) to maintain compliance; failure to file for three consecutive years results in automatic revocation of exempt status.

**The Numbers & Rules:**
*   **Donations ($180k) & Corporate Sponsors ($50k):** Under IRS rules for 501(c)(3) organizations, contributions made with "detached and disinterested generosity" are excluded from gross income.
    *   *Assumption:* The $50k corporate sponsorship is a pure gift with no substantial return benefit (e.g., no advertising, logos, or exclusive marketing rights provided to the sponsor).
    *   *Result:* If structured as a gift, $230,000 is tax-exempt contribution revenue. If the sponsor receives advertising value, that portion is taxable commercial income.
*   **Interest Income ($12,000):** Passive income (interest, dividends, royalties) is generally excluded from UBI tax *unless* it is derived from debt-financed property. However, it must still be reported.
    *   *Rule:* If your total gross receipts are under $200,000 and total assets under $500,000, you likely file **Form 990-EZ**. If you have any UBI (e.g., if the sponsorship is reclassified as advertising), you may also need **Form 990-T** if the net UBI exceeds $1,000.
    *   *Result:* The $12,000 interest is reportable. It is typically not taxable itself, but it counts toward your gross receipts total for determining which Form 990 variant you must file.

**What Flips It, and The Trigger:**
The classification of the $50,000 corporate sponsorship is the single biggest risk. If your organization provides "substantial return benefits" (like logo placement, mentions in press releases, or booth space at events) proportional to the payment, the IRS reclassifies that portion as **advertising revenue**, which is taxable commercial activity.
*   *Trigger:* If the fair market value of benefits provided to sponsors exceeds 2% of the payment (or $119 for 2024, adjusted annually), the excess is taxable.
*   *Action:* If your sponsorship agreements include marketing deliverables, consider separating the "gift" portion from the "sponsorship/advertising" portion in your contracts immediately.

**Routing Note:**
Determining whether your specific sponsorship agreements constitute "qualified sponsorship payments" (tax-free) or "advertising" (taxable) is a binding legal determination based on the exact wording of your contracts and the benefits provided. You must engage a CPA or tax attorney specializing in non-profit law to review your sponsorship agreements and prepare your Form 990 to ensure the $50,000 is not inadvertently taxed or flagged as excessive commercial activity.
