**The Call**
Your made-to-order model likely exempts you from Texas finished-goods inventory tax, but your raw material stock ($8,000 now, projected $25,000) generally remains taxable business personal property; scaling materials increases your liability but does not change your fundamental tax status. **The Numbers**
Texas Business Personal Property (BPP) tax applies to tangible property used to produce income, including raw materials and supplies, regardless of whether finished goods are held.
*   **Current Taxable Base:** $8,000 (Raw Filament).
*   **Projected Taxable Base:** $25,000 (Raw Filament + Safety Stock).
*   **Estimated Tax Impact:** Texas BPP rates vary by jurisdiction (county/city/school district), typically ranging from 1.5% to 2.5% of the assessed value.

**What Flips It, and the Trigger**
The single input that changes the call is the **classification of partially printed items**. If a sneaker is mid-print when the tax date (January 1) arrives, it may be classified as "Work-in-Progress" (WIP). While WIP is often taxable, some jurisdictions have specific exemptions or different valuation methods for goods in transit or specific manufacturing stages.
*   **Trigger:** If your production cycle exceeds 24 hours such that significant value sits on printers on January 1, your taxable value could spike beyond raw material costs.
*   **Rule of Thumb:** If >10% of your monthly revenue is tied up in machines mid-print on Jan 1, document the exact stage of completion to argue for lower valuation.
*   **Step this week:** If your projected material cost exceeds $20,000, consider Y: contact your county appraisal district immediately to request the specific "Business Personal Property Rendition" form and verify if a "Freeport" exemption (goods in transit) applies to any materials shipped directly to customers, though this rarely covers stored raw filament.
