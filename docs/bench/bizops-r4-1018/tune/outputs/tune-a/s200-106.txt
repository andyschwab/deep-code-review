**The Call**
Do not book the $50,000 immediately; you must spread (amortize) the revenue evenly over the 12-month service period. Recognizing it all upfront would violate accrual accounting principles, artificially inflate your January performance, and mislead investors regarding your true Monthly Recurring Revenue (MRR) and run-rate.

**The Numbers**
Under accrual accounting, revenue is recognized when earned (service delivered), not when cash is received.
*   **Total Contract Value:** $50,000 (Cash received Jan 1).
*   **Service Period:** 12 months.
*   **Monthly Revenue Recognition:** $50,000 / 12 = **$4,166.67 per month**.

*Journal Entry Logic:*
1.  **January 1 (Cash Receipt):** Debit Cash $50,000; Credit **Deferred Revenue** (Liability) $50,000.
    *   *Result:* Your balance sheet shows a liability (obligation to serve), not income.
2.  **January 31 (Month End):** Debit Deferred Revenue $4,166.67; Credit **Revenue** $4,166.67.
    *   *Result:* Your P&L shows $4,166.67 in revenue for January.
3.  **Repeat:** Perform step 2 at the end of every subsequent month until the balance reaches zero in December.

*Assumption:* This calculation assumes the contract has no cancellation clauses requiring immediate refund upon termination, which would alter the liability profile.

**What Flips It, and the Trigger**
The only input that changes this treatment is the **delivery model**. If the contract were for a one-time, delivered asset (e.g., a perpetual software license with no ongoing support or updates), immediate recognition might be permissible. However, since you deliver workshops and coaching continuously, the "performance obligation" is satisfied over time.
*   **Trigger:** If your audit team identifies any portion of the $50,000 booked as immediate revenue, you must reclassify it to Deferred Revenue before the audit closes.
*   **Rule of Thumb:** If the customer can cancel and receive a pro-rated refund, you strictly cannot recognize revenue faster than the service is provided.
*   **Action Step:** If your current ledger shows $50,000 in January revenue, consider creating a reversing entry this week to move $45,833.33 to a Deferred Revenue liability account.

**Routing Note**
While the principle of amortization is standard under US GAAP (ASC 606) and IFRS (IFRS 15), the specific tax treatment of deferred revenue varies by jurisdiction; some tax regimes require you to pay taxes on cash received upfront even if you haven't recognized it for book purposes. Bring your specific state/country tax code and the signed contract to a CPA to determine if you owe tax on the full $50,000 in Year 1 despite spreading the book revenue.
