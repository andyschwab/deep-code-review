**The Call**
Use your specific historical data (2% failure rate) as the base case for your financial model, but present a sensitivity analysis showing the impact of the industry average (5%). Investors view company-specific empirical data as more credible than generic benchmarks, even with a limited history, provided you disclose the sample size and assumptions. Setting reserves at the industry average (5%) without evidence of higher risk in your specific product line unnecessarily depresses your valuation and signals a lack of confidence in your quality control.

**The Numbers**
*Warranty Reserve Formula:* Total Units Sold × Estimated Failure Rate × Cost Per Repair

*   **Scenario A (Historical Data - Base Case)**
    *   Inputs: 10,000 units × 2% (0.02) × $100
    *   Calculation: 200 failures × $100
    *   **Total Liability: $20,000**
    *   *Per Unit Impact:* $2.00

*   **Scenario B (Industry Average - Stress Case)**
    *   Inputs: 10,000 units × 5% (0.05) × $100
    *   Calculation: 500 failures × $100
    *   **Total Liability: $50,000**
    *   *Per Unit Impact:* $5.00

*   **ASSUMED:** The $100 repair cost remains constant regardless of volume; the 10,000 units represent the total population for the reserve calculation period.

**What Flips It, and The Trigger**
The deciding factor is **product modification or supply chain changes**. If you change materials, manufacturers, or design features in the next production run, your historical data becomes less predictive.
*   **Trigger:** If you implement a design change or switch suppliers, immediately revert to the industry average (5%) for new units until new failure data is collected (typically 12 months).
*   **Rule of Thumb:** Historical data is statistically significant if the confidence interval around your 2% estimate does not overlap materially with the industry 5% benchmark. With 10,000 units and 200 failures, your data is robust enough to be credible.
*   **This Week's Step:** If your engineering team has planned any component changes for the next batch, consider modeling the new batch at the 5% rate while keeping the legacy inventory at 2%.

**Accounting Principles & Investor Credibility**
The guiding principle is **GAAP ASC 450 (Contingencies)**, which requires losses to be accrued when they are *probable* and *reasonably estimable*. "Probable" relies on the best available evidence. For a hardware startup, your own failure data is the "best available evidence," superior to broad industry averages which may include lower-quality competitors. Investors prefer a model grounded in your actual operational metrics, accompanied by a clear "what-if" scenario for higher failure rates. Over-reserving based on fear looks like poor unit economics understanding; under-reserving looks like negligence. The middle path is: **Base your P&L on your 2% reality, but show the 5% scenario in your risk factors.**
