**The Call:** A contract clause alone is insufficient to avoid money transmitter classification; regulators prioritize functional control over contractual disclaimers. You must technically architect your system to ensure you possess zero ability to initiate, authorize, or alter the movement of funds or the authoritative state of balances, rather than relying on legal text to define your role.

**What Flips It, and The Trigger:**
The determining factor is **functional custody**. If your database schema or API allows your internal admin keys (or a compromised server) to directly credit/debit user balances in a way that a customer treats as the "source of truth" for settling payments, you are likely facilitating transmission.
*   **Trigger:** If a customer can instruct your API to "move" value from User A to User B, or if your system is the ledger of record for final settlement, you cross the line.
*   **Rule of Thumb:** Data hosting records *what happened*; money transmitters *make it happen*.
*   **Actionable Step:** If your current architecture allows any write-access to balance fields that triggers a downstream financial action, **consider** implementing a "read-only" mirror architecture where the authoritative balance lives solely on the customer's core banking ledger, and LedgerCloud only stores historical snapshots for analytics.

**Routing Note:**
Money transmitter laws are fact-specific and vary significantly between New York (BitLicense/Broad Money Transmitter), California (DFPI), and Texas (DFF). The determination of whether "storing balance snapshots" constitutes maintaining a "stored value" instrument or facilitating transmission is a binding legal conclusion. You must engage a fintech regulatory attorney in each jurisdiction to review your specific data flow diagrams and API specifications before signing contracts or finalizing architecture. Do not rely on general rules for this specific architectural design.
