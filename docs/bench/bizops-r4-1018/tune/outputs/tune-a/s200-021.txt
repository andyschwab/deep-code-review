**The Call**
Do not use the phrase "Certified Compostable" on packaging or marketing materials without holding the actual certificate; launch immediately with a factual, non-certification claim such as "Home Compostable in 6 Months" to capture revenue while you pursue the seal.

**The Numbers**
The decision hinges on the cost of delay versus the risk of regulatory action.
*   **Cost of Waiting:** 4 months delay × $15,000/month burn = **$60,000** in lost operating capital before unit one is sold.
*   **Revenue at Risk:** 5,000 units × $35 price = **$175,000** in potential top-line revenue delayed.
*   **Unit Margin:** $35 (Price) - $12 (Cost) = **$23** contribution per unit.
*   **Break-even on Delay:** You must sell ~2,609 units ($60,000 / $23) just to recover the cash burned during the waiting period.

*Assumption:* We assume your marketing team can pivot messaging instantly and that the "softer claim" does not trigger the same certification requirement (see Tier 2 below).

**What Flips It, and the Trigger**
The single input that changes this call is the **specific wording of your claim**.
*   **Trigger:** If your packaging says "Certified," you are making a factual assertion about a third-party verification that you do not yet possess. This is the line that triggers enforcement.
*   **Rule of Thumb:** In the US, the FTC Green Guides state that unqualified compostability claims require competent and reliable scientific evidence that the product will break down in a home compost environment. However, adding the word "Certified" implies an existing accreditation.
*   **Actionable Step:** If you cannot produce a certificate from an accredited body (like BPI or TUV Austria) by next month, **consider** removing the word "Certified" entirely and relying on your internal testing data to support the phrase "Designed to Home Compost in 6 Months."

**Regulatory Context & Routing Note**
*General Rule (Tier 2):* The Federal Trade Commission (FTC) enforces the "Green Guides." They do not strictly *mandate* third-party certification for all compostability claims, but they mandate that any claim be substantiated by reliable scientific evidence. If you claim "home compostable," you must have proof it breaks down in a home bin, not just an industrial facility. Many states (like California and Washington) have stricter laws requiring specific certifications for certain claims. Using "Certified" without the seal is widely considered deceptive practice.

*Routing Note (Tier 3 - Binding Determination):* Because you are based in Portland, **Oregon's specific consumer protection statutes** and the **FTC's interpretation of "home compostable"** versus "industrial compostable" require a binding legal review before finalizing artwork. Ask counsel: "Does our internal degradation data meet the 'competent and reliable scientific evidence' standard under the FTC Green Guides and Oregon law to support an unqualified 'Home Compostable' claim without third-party certification?"
