**The Call:** Structure your promotion as a post-sale manufacturer rebate; in California and most key markets, you generally collect and remit sales tax on the full $110 selling price, not the net $85, because the rebate is a separate transaction occurring after the sale. Do not attempt to reduce your sales tax base based on the future rebate obligation; the potential cash flow cost of the extra tax is the price of compliance, and attempting to deduct it upfront without specific statutory authority creates audit risk.

**The Numbers:**
*   **Gross Revenue:** 3,000 units × $110 = $330,000 (Note: Your $80,000 projection implies only ~727 units sold; calculations below use your 3,000 unit volume for accuracy, but scale linearly to your actuals).
*   **Rebate Liability:** 3,000 units × $25 (ASSUMED 100% redemption rate for worst-case cash flow) = $75,000.
*   **Net Revenue:** $330,000 - $75,000 = $255,000.
*   **Sales Tax Base (California General Rule):** $330,000 (Full selling price).
*   **Sales Tax Due (Illustrative @ 8.5% avg CA rate):** $330,000 × 0.085 = $28,050.
*   **Tax Impact of Error:** If you incorrectly taxed only the net ($255,000), you would under-collect ~$6,375.
*   **Unit Economics Impact:**
    *   Margin per unit (pre-tax): $110 - $42 = $68.
    *   Effective Margin (assuming 100% rebate redemption): $68 - $25 = $43.
    *   Contribution Margin Ratio: $43 / $110 = 39%.

**What Flips It, and the Trigger:**
The single input that changes the tax treatment is the **legal structure of the discount**. If you change the promotion to an "instant discount" (reducing the point-of-sale price to $85 immediately), the sales tax base drops to $85. However, this flips the marketing dynamic: customers must qualify *at purchase* rather than registering later.
*   **Trigger:** If your redemption rate drops below 40% (customers buying but not mailing in the rebate form), the effective cost of the promotion rises relative to an instant discount, yet the tax burden remains on the full $110.
*   **Rule of Thumb:** If redemption < 50%, a post-sale rebate is financially superior to an instant discount despite the higher tax base, because you retain the unredeemed cash. If redemption > 90%, the administrative burden and tax drag may warrant switching to an instant discount model.
*   **Action:** If your historical redemption data (or pilot test) shows >80% completion, consider switching to an instant discount for the next quarter to lower the taxable base.

**Routing Note:**
Sales tax rules on rebates are highly jurisdiction-specific (e.g., California treats manufacturer rebates differently than retailer coupons; some states allow deductions for specific "energy incentive" rebates if mandated by law). You must consult a California tax attorney or CPA to confirm if your specific "energy incentive" language qualifies for any statutory exemption that overrides the general manufacturer rebate rule. Do not file returns assuming a reduced base without written confirmation of the specific code section allowing it.
