### The Core Distinction: Software vs. Financial Intermediary

The line between providing a software tool and acting as a financial intermediary (Money Services Business or Money Transmitter) generally depends on **custody** and **control** of funds.

**1. Providing a Software Tool (Likely Safe)**
You are likely operating as a pure software provider if:
*   **No Custody:** You never take possession, control, or temporary custody of the customer's crypto assets.
*   **Direct Payment:** The customer sends the audit fee directly to your wallet address from their own wallet. You are simply the recipient of a payment for services rendered, similar to a freelancer accepting Bitcoin for consulting.
*   **No Third-Party Movement:** You are not moving funds on behalf of the user to a third party (e.g., you aren't holding their funds to pay the auditor if you are a marketplace, or swapping tokens for them).

**2. Acting as a Financial Intermediary (High Risk)**
You likely cross the line into Money Transmitter/Money Services Business (MSB) territory if:
*   **Custody:** You hold user funds in a platform wallet before settling the transaction. Even "briefly" touching funds to facilitate a swap or hold them in escrow can trigger licensing.
*   **Exchanging Assets:** If you accept one token (e.g., ETH) and convert it to another (e.g., USDC) to pay your auditors or yourself, you may be acting as an unlicensed currency exchanger.
*   **Transmission:** If the user sends funds to *your* controlled address, and you then forward those funds to a third-party auditor (if your model involves third parties), you are transmitting value. The "facilitation fee" model is risky if the flow is User → ChainDebug → Auditor. If the flow is User → Auditor (with a separate fee to you), the risk is lower.

### Specific Activities That Trigger Obligations

Regulators (including the Monetary Authority of Singapore under the Payment Services Act) typically look for these specific activities:
*   **Account Creation:** Creating accounts where users store value.
*   **Safekeeping:** Holding private keys on behalf of users.
*   **Exchange Services:** Swapping one digital payment token for another.
*   **Transfer Services:** Moving digital payment tokens from one person to another on behalf of others.

If your "facilitation" involves the user sending crypto to a smart contract or address you control, which then automatically splits the funds (sending the bulk to the auditor and the fee to you), this is often viewed as **transmitting** funds. The fact that you touch the asset, even algorithmically, to distribute it, can classify you as an intermediary.

### Potential Exposure

If you are deemed an unlicensed Money Services Business or Money Transmitter:

1.  **Regulatory Enforcement:** You face cease-and-desist orders, forcing an immediate shutdown of payment processing.
2.  **Civil Penalties:** Fines can be substantial, often calculated as a multiple of the transaction volume or a fixed statutory maximum (e.g., up to SGD 1 million or more in Singapore depending on the breach).
3.  **Criminal Liability:** In severe cases, operating an unlicensed money transmission business can lead to criminal charges against founders.
4.  **Banking De-risking:** Traditional banks and fiat on-ramps will terminate your accounts once they identify unlicensed crypto transmission activity.
5.  **Reputational Damage:** Being flagged as non-compliant makes it difficult to partner with auditors, insurers, or enterprise clients.

### Immediate Recommendation

Your legal advisor's warning is critical. If your current flow involves **any** mechanism where ChainDebug controls the funds mid-transaction, you are likely exposed. To mitigate this without expensive licenses:
*   Restructure payments so users pay auditors directly, and auditors pay you a referral fee separately (if legally viable).
*   Or, ensure you are strictly receiving payment for your own services directly into a non-custodial wallet you control, with no movement of third-party funds.

Do not process another transaction until a qualified attorney reviews your specific smart contract logic and fund flow diagrams.
