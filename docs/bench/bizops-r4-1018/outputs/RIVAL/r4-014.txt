**2. Regulatory Distinction: Food vs. Supplement in the UK**
The liability stems from the divergent regulatory frameworks governed by the Food Standards Agency (FSA):
*   **Standard Tea/Food:** Regulated under general food law. Focus is on safety, labeling accuracy (ingredients, allergens), and hygiene. Duty rates are generally lower (often 0–4% for tea/herbs). Claims are restricted to basic nutritional content; no disease prevention or therapeutic claims are permitted.
*   **Dietary Supplements:** Regulated under stricter legislation (retained EU law on food supplements). These products are assumed to have a physiological effect. They require:
    *   Pre-market notification to the FSA.
    *   Strict limits on vitamin/mineral dosages.
    *   Prohibition of unauthorized novel foods (some Asian herbs may fall under "Novel Food" regulations requiring specific authorization).
    *   Higher scrutiny and potentially higher duty rates depending on the specific commodity code.
*   **The Risk:** By labeling as a "supplement," you inadvertently claimed the product meets these stricter safety and notification standards. If the ingredients (e.g., specific turmeric extracts) are not authorized for supplements or exceed safe limits, the goods are non-compliant, triggering penalties for incorrect classification and potential safety violations.

**3. Liability Assessment: Past Imports and Audits**
*   **Audit Risk:** Yes, a classification error on a £15,000 shipment can trigger a compliance audit of your past imports. HMRC typically has the power to examine records going back **four years** (not necessarily five, but close enough to warrant concern) if they suspect a systematic error.
*   **Systemic vs. Isolated:** The severity depends on whether this was a one-off clerical error or a pattern. If all 12 annual containers were misclassified, the liability compounds. If only this shipment was affected, the exposure is limited.
*   **Financial Exposure:** With £200,000 in reserves, a systematic re-classification of £180k+ of annual imports could be devastating if duties differ significantly or if penalties are applied retroactively. However, if the duty rate difference is small (e.g., 4% vs 6%), the primary risk is the penalty, not the duty arrears.

**4. Corrective Steps for Future Shipments (Without Admitting Guilt)**
*   **Obtain a Binding Tariff Information (BTI) Ruling:** Apply to HMRC for a BTI decision on your specific turmeric and ginger blend. This is a legal classification decision that binds HMRC for three years.
    *   *Strategy:* Frame the application as a request for "clarification to ensure future compliance" rather than a confession of past error. State that you are reviewing your portfolio and seek certainty on the correct commodity code for "herbal blends intended for infusion."
*   **Update Documentation:** Until the BTI arrives, classify future shipments conservatively as "Herbal Tea" or "Food Preparation" unless you have explicit authorization for "Supplements." Ensure all commercial invoices and packing lists match this conservative classification.
*   **Internal Review:** Conduct an internal audit of your last 24 months of shipments using a third-party consultant (under legal privilege if possible) to quantify potential exposure before HMRC initiates a formal deep-dive.

**Note:** The "20% penalty" is likely an initial assessment; specialized brokers often reduce this significantly by proving "reasonable care" was taken elsewhere or that the error was not deliberate. Do not ignore the deadline for appealing the penalty notice.
