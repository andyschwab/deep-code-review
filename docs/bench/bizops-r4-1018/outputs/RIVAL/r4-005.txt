Under the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act (FD&C Act), a product is classified as a "drug" if it is intended to diagnose, cure, mitigate, treat, or prevent disease. Your marketing team's proposed phrases cross this legal line definitively:

1.  **"Helps fight off common colds"**: The "common cold" is a recognized disease. Any claim suggesting your product prevents, treats, or mitigates a specific disease automatically classifies the product as an unapproved new drug. Without rigorous scientific substantiation and specific regulatory formatting (see below), this phrase is highly dangerous for a conventional food.

**The Line Between Food and Drug Claims**

To remain compliant as a food manufacturer, you must distinguish between **disease claims** (illegal for food) and **structure-function claims** (permissible with restrictions).

*   **Prohibited (Drug Claims):** Any mention of a disease, symptom of a disease, or class of diseases.
    *   *Avoid:* "Fights colds," "prevents flu," "treats infection," "reduces fever," "boosts immunity to stop viruses."
    *   *Result:* These imply the product alters the body's structure or function to combat a specific pathological condition.

*   **Permissible (Structure-Function Claims for Conventional Food):** These describe the role of a nutrient or dietary ingredient in affecting the *normal* structure or function of the human body, **without** referencing a disease.
    *   *Safe Examples:* "Elderberry is a source of antioxidants," "Echinacea is an herb traditionally used in herbal teas," "Supports overall wellness."
    *   *Critical Restriction:* Even structure-function claims on conventional foods (unlike dietary supplements) cannot imply disease prevention. The FDA is extremely strict that conventional foods cannot claim to affect the immune system's response to pathogens. Therefore, even "supports immune health" is arguably too risky for a baby food puree unless framed strictly around nutrient content (e.g., "Vitamin C contributes to normal immune function").
