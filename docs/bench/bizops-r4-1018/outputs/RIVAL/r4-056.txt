A free tier generally does not violate EU competition laws simply because a company holds a dominant market position. Under EU law (specifically Article 102 TFEU), "predatory pricing" or abuse of dominance requires proving that a dominant firm prices below cost with the specific intent to eliminate competitors and subsequently recoup losses by raising prices later. Since your proposed model involves a free entry tier to monetize advanced enterprise features later (a standard "freemium" model), it typically lacks the "recoupment" element necessary for a predatory pricing claim. Regulators view this as competition on the merits rather than an exclusionary tactic, provided the free tier is a genuine product offering and not a temporary, below-cost sham designed solely to starve rivals.

Regulators primarily assess three factors when evaluating such strategies:
1.  **Dominance Definition:** They first confirm if you hold a "dominant position" (usually >40-50% market share) in a clearly defined relevant market. While you have 80% among European startups, regulators may argue the relevant market is broader (e.g., global orchestration tools), which could dilute your calculated dominance.
2.  **Below-Cost Pricing:** They analyze if the price is below your average variable cost. Offering a software tier at zero marginal cost is often defensible in digital markets if the business model relies on upselling, as the "cost" is covered by the conversion to paid tiers.
3.  **Exclusionary Effect:** They examine if the strategy creates an insurmountable barrier to entry that prevents equally efficient competitors from surviving, regardless of their quality. If your free tier locks users in via proprietary data formats or network effects that make switching impossible, scrutiny increases.

**You must engage an antitrust attorney before rolling out the new pricing page if:**
*   **You plan to make the free tier exclusive or incompatible:** If the free version prevents interoperability with competitors or locks data in a way that makes switching to a rival technically impossible, this moves from pricing strategy to technical foreclosure.
*   **Your internal communications suggest exclusionary intent:** If strategy documents, emails, or meeting notes explicitly state the goal is to "drive competitors out of business" or "kill rival X" rather than "acquire users for upsell," this evidence can be used to prove abusive intent.
*   **You are already under investigation:** If the European Commission or a national authority (like the Bundeskartellamt or ACM) has opened a formal inquiry into your market practices, any pricing change requires legal clearance.
*   **The "Enterprise" features are essential facilities:** If the advanced capabilities you plan to charge for are considered an "essential facility" that competitors need to access to survive in the market, withholding them or bundling them aggressively could trigger abuse claims.
