### Seasonal Profitability Framework for CityGreens

To model your profitability accurately, you must shift from a monthly average view to a **seasonal contribution margin analysis**. Your current flat revenue ($24,000) masks the risk posed by winter energy spikes. Here is the framework to determine your break-even thresholds and evaluate seasonal pricing.

#### 1. Establish Your Baseline Variables
First, isolate your fixed and variable costs outside of energy.
*   **Monthly Revenue:** $24,000 (15 restaurants × $400/week × 4 weeks).
*   **Summer Energy Cost:** $2,200.
*   **Winter Energy Cost:** $3,800.
*   **Energy Variance:** $1,600 (The specific "winter penalty").
*   **Projected Winter Energy ( +10%):** $3,800 × 1.10 = **$4,180**.

*Note: You did not provide your total non-energy operating costs (labor, seeds, packaging, rent). For the calculations below, we will treat these as a single variable called **Non-Energy OpEx**.*

#### 2. The Seasonal Break-Even Formula
Do not use an annual average for energy. Calculate two distinct break-even points: one for summer and one for winter.

**Formula:**
`Required Revenue = Non-Energy OpEx + Seasonal Energy Cost`

**Scenario A: Current Winter Break-Even**
`Required Revenue = Non-Energy OpEx + $3,800`

**Scenario B: Projected Winter Break-Even (+10% Energy Hike)**
`Required Revenue = Non-Energy OpEx + $4,180`

**The Danger Zone:**
If your current `Non-Energy OpEx` is greater than **$19,820**, you are already operating at a loss in winter ($24,000 revenue - $3,800 energy - $19,820+ other costs = negative). If energy rises 10%, your Non-Energy OpEx must be under **$19,820** just to break even.

#### 3. Calculating the Profitability Threshold
To find the exact revenue threshold where you become unprofitable, work backward from your known costs.

1.  Sum all non-energy costs for a month (Labor + Rent + Supplies + etc.).
2.  Add the projected winter energy cost ($4,180).
3.  **Result:** This is your **Winter Survival Number**.
    *   If your revenue drops below this number, you lose money.
    *   Currently, your buffer is: `$24,000 - $4,180 - Non-Energy OpEx`.

**Example Calculation:**
If your Non-Energy OpEx is $18,000:
*   Summer Profit: $24,000 - $2,200 - $18,000 = **$3,800 profit**.
*   Winter Profit (Projected): $24,000 - $4,180 - $18,000 = **$1,820 profit**.
*   *Risk:* If revenue dips just 8% in winter, you hit break-even. If Non-Energy OpEx is actually $20,000, you are already losing $180/month in winter.

#### 4. Evaluating Seasonal Pricing Contracts
You asked whether to negotiate seasonal pricing. Use the **Energy Pass-Through Model** to decide.

**The Argument for Seasonal Pricing:**
Your product value (fresh local greens) remains constant, but your cost to produce fluctuates wildly. Charging a flat rate means your restaurant clients subsidize your winter inefficiencies while you absorb the risk.

**Proposed Structure:**
*   **Base Rate (May–Sept):** Maintain current $400/week.
*   **Winter Surcharge (Oct–Apr):** Add a specific line item or percentage increase to cover the energy variance.
    *   *Calculation:* Total Winter Energy Penalty ($1,600) ÷ 15 restaurants = ~$107 extra per restaurant/month.
    *   *Proposal:* Increase weekly orders by **$25/week** per restaurant during winter months.
    *   *Impact:* This generates an extra $1,500/month, nearly neutralizing the winter energy spike.

**Recommendation:**
Yes, propose a **Seasonal Energy Adjustment Clause**. Frame it transparently: "To guarantee consistent local supply despite rising utility costs, a temporary winter surcharge applies from October to April." This protects your margins without permanently raising prices year-round.
