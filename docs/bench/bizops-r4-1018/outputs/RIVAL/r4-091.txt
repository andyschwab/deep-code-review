The employment status of the creators (contractors vs. employees) does not inherently reduce your valuation multiple, but it creates a binary risk profile: either you own the IP 100% cleanly, or you own nothing. Acquirers do not penalize the *use* of contractors; they penalize *ambiguity* in ownership.

If your contracts with the three external developers included explicit "Work Made for Hire" clauses and unconditional IP assignment agreements transferring all rights to Acme Logic, there is no liability. In this scenario, the IP is treated exactly the same as if your full-time employees built it. The due diligence process will simply verify that these documents exist, are signed, and cover the specific scope of the compression algorithm. If this documentation is perfect, your valuation multiple remains unaffected.

However, if these agreements are missing, vague, or verbal, you face a catastrophic risk. Under US copyright law, contractors retain ownership of their work unless explicitly transferred in writing. An acquirer will view this as a "broken chain of title." They cannot buy what you do not clearly own. This uncertainty can halt a deal entirely or force a significant escrow arrangement where a portion of the purchase price is withheld until the IP is legally secured, effectively lowering your immediate realized value.

Acquirers will specifically investigate three factors during due diligence:
1.  **Chain of Title:** Can you produce signed agreements from day one of the contractors' engagement proving Acme Logic owns the code?
2.  **Scope Consistency:** Do the contracts specifically cover the compression algorithm, or are they generic service agreements that might leave gaps?
3.  **Moral Rights & Jurisdiction:** Since you are in Delaware, ensure the contracts waive any "moral rights" the contractors might claim, which can sometimes persist even after IP transfer depending on the jurisdiction where the work was performed.

**Immediate Legal Steps Required:**
Before entering serious negotiations, you must audit your contractor agreements immediately. If any gap exists where IP assignment is not explicit, you must execute a retroactive IP Assignment Agreement with each of the three contractors. This document must state that they irrevocably assign all rights, title, and interest in the compression algorithm to Acme Logic, effective from the start of their engagement. Do not rely on existing invoices or generic consulting contracts; get a specific assignment for this asset.

Once these documents are signed and stored, the distinction between contractor and employee creation becomes irrelevant to the valuation. The asset is then solidified as company property, allowing you to command the full market multiple based on the technology's value, not its authorship.

Note: Ensure these assignments include representations that the code is original and does not infringe on third-party IP.
Have your legal counsel review the specific language to ensure it satisfies the due diligence standards of typical strategic acquirers in the SaaS space.
