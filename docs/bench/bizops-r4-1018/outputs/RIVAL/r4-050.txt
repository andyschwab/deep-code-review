**IRS Criteria: Dealer vs. Investor**
The distinction relies on the "facts and circumstances" test, where no single factor is decisive, but your specific metrics strongly indicate dealer status:

1.  **Frequency and Continuity:** You sell approximately 15 items monthly (180 annually) with a 25-day hold time. The IRS views regular, continuous, and substantial sales activity as the hallmark of a business. An investor typically holds assets for appreciation over a longer period with infrequent sales. Your 30-day turnover window is characteristic of retail inventory management, not long-term investment.
2.  **Intent at Acquisition:** You buy distressed items specifically to refurbish and resell them for a profit. Investors generally acquire assets to hold for appreciation or income (like dividends/rent) without active modification for immediate resale. Your intent is clearly commercial arbitrage.
3.  **Value-Added Activities:** You actively refinish the furniture. The IRS considers the effort to improve, repair, or market goods for resale as a business operation. Investors typically do not materially alter assets to generate profit.

**Impact of Detailed Logs**
Keeping detailed logs of your repair hours **does not** change your classification from dealer to investor. In fact, extensive logs documenting labor hours often reinforce the argument that you are operating a business by proving the significant time and effort invested in generating income. These logs are valuable for substantiating your business activity and ensuring you deduct all legitimate business expenses (materials, tools, portion of home office, vehicle mileage), which lowers your net taxable income, but they will not exempt you from self-employment tax.

**Tax Implications**
*   **Income Character:** Your $3,000 monthly profit is ordinary income, not capital gains. You cannot utilize lower long-term capital gains tax rates.
*   **Self-Employment Tax:** You must pay Social Security and Medicare taxes on your net profit. On $36,000 annual profit, this adds approximately $5,100 in taxes annually compared to an investor scenario.
*   **Deductibility:** As a business, you can deduct the cost of goods sold ($150 purchase + materials) and operating expenses against your revenue. As an investor, these deductions would be treated differently and potentially limited.
