**Accounting Treatment for EcoCart Jar Deposits**

**1. Initial Recognition: Liability, Not Revenue**
When you collect the $3 deposit, it is **not revenue**. It is a current liability on your balance sheet, typically recorded under an account named "Customer Deposits" or "Refundable Deposits."
*   **Journal Entry:** Debit Cash $3; Credit Customer Deposits (Liability) $3.
*   **Impact on Loans:** While this increases your total liabilities, lenders generally view refundable deposits differently than debt. They are "unearned" obligations that disappear when the jar is returned, not interest-bearing loans. However, a high ratio of liabilities to assets can affect leverage ratios. You must disclose the nature of these liabilities to your lender to ensure they are not misclassified as traditional debt.

**2. Recognizing Unreturned Deposits as Revenue**
You cannot recognize unreturned deposits as revenue immediately upon collection. Revenue is recognized only when the obligation to refund is extinguished. This occurs in two scenarios:
*   **Actual Forfeiture:** The customer explicitly states they will not return the jar, or the jar is returned damaged beyond repair (if your policy states deposits are forfeited in this case).
*   **Breakage (Statutory Escheatment):** If a customer simply never returns the jar, you cannot automatically keep the money forever. Most jurisdictions have "unclaimed property" or escheatment laws. After a specific dormancy period (often 1–5 years, varying by state), unclaimed deposits must be remitted to the state government, not kept as revenue.
    *   *Exception:* Some accounting frameworks allow recognizing "breakage income" proportionally as customers redeem their jars, based on the expected redemption rate (your 85% return rate implies a 15% breakage rate). However, this requires robust historical data and often specific CPA guidance to apply correctly under GAAP.

**3. Modeling Working Capital and Taxes**
*   **Working Capital:** With 1,500 jars at $3, you have $4,500 in cash inflow offset by a $4,500 liability. This is neutral for net working capital (Current Assets - Current Liabilities) but improves immediate cash flow. As you scale to 5,000 jars ($15,000), this cash float grows. If you spend this cash on operations before jars are returned, and redemption spikes, you face a liquidity crunch.
*   **Tax Obligations:** Deposits are generally not taxable income until recognized as revenue (forfeiture/breakage). Sales tax treatment varies; in many states, deposits are not subject to sales tax unless forfeited. You must verify Washington State specific rules regarding sales tax on reusable container deposits.

**4. Financial Statement Presentation**
*   **Balance Sheet:** List under "Current Liabilities" as "Refundable Customer Deposits."
*   **Income Statement:** Only the portion of deposits deemed forfeited or recognized as breakage appears here as "Other Income" or "Revenue," never the initial collection.

**Recommendation on CPA Expertise**
Yes, you should engage a CPA with specific experience in **retail operations and unclaimed property compliance**. Generalist accountants may incorrectly treat these deposits as immediate revenue (inflating tax liability) or fail to set up the necessary tracking for escheatment obligations. Given your goal to secure a loan, having clean, compliant books that clearly distinguish between operational debt and refundable liabilities is critical. Your accountant needs to establish a "breakage policy" aligned with GAAP and Washington state law to determine exactly when and how the 15% non-return rate converts to income.

*Note: This guidance is based on general accounting principles; specific Washington State statutes and GAAP updates must be verified by your licensed CPA.*
*Do not spend the deposit cash reserve; treat it as restricted cash until your breakage policy is formally documented.*
