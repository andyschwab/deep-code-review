For investor reporting, tax compliance, and accurate unit economics, you should **average your storage costs over the entire year** rather than attributing specific monthly fluctuations to units sold in those periods.

Your commercial freezer storage is a **period cost** (overhead) necessary to maintain your inventory's state, not a direct variable cost tied to the production of a specific batch. In food manufacturing and general accounting standards (GAAP), allocating highly volatile seasonal overheads directly to COGS on a monthly basis distorts your margin analysis. It makes your summer performance look artificially weak and your winter performance artificially strong, even though your operational efficiency and sales volume may remain constant. This volatility obscures the true profitability of your core business model from investors.

**Recommended Modeling Approach:**

1.  **Calculate Annualized Overhead:** Sum your projected or actual storage costs for all 12 months. Based on your data, if we assume a simple average of your high ($3,500) and low ($2,100) points as a baseline proxy, your annual estimate is roughly $33,600 (assuming a sinusoidal variation) or simply use the actual billed amounts if known.
2.  **Determine Annual Production Volume:** You produce 5,000 lbs/month, totaling 60,000 lbs annually.
3.  **Compute Blended Unit Cost:** Divide the total annual storage cost by the total annual production volume.
    *   *Example:* If annual storage is ~$33,600 / 60,000 lbs = **$0.56 per lb**.
4.  **Apply Consistently:** Add this blended $0.56/lb to your other variable costs (ingredients, labor, packaging) to establish a standard COGS per unit. Use this standard cost for every month's P&L.

**Why this matters for your price increase decision:**
With $45,000 in the bank, you need clarity, not noise. If you use the "specific attribution" method, you might panic in July thinking your margins have collapsed, potentially triggering an unnecessary price hike that hurts customer relationships. Conversely, you might feel overly confident in January. By smoothing the cost, you see that your true margin at $12/package is stable year-round. If the *blended* margin is too thin to sustain growth or cover the peak cash-flow crunch in summer, then a price increase is justified based on structural economics, not temporary seasonality.

**Compliance Note:**
For tax purposes, the IRS generally allows reasonable allocation methods for overhead. Consistency is key; once you choose an allocation method (like annual averaging), you must apply it consistently year-over-year unless you file for a change in accounting method. Consult your CPA to formalize this "standard cost" system, but averaging is the standard best practice for seasonal storage in manufacturing.

**Cash Flow Warning:**
While averaging helps your *profit* reporting, it does not solve the *cash flow* gap. You will still pay $3,500 in July but only collect revenue based on a $0.56/lb cost assumption. Ensure your $45,000 cash reserve is sufficient to cover the ~$1,400/month extra cash outflow during peak summer months without dipping into operating capital needed for ingredients.
