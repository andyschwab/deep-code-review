**Do not include internal cost savings or infrastructure efficiency metrics in your customer-facing Service Level Agreement (SLA).**

Your proposal to guarantee specific internal cost savings as a performance metric creates significant, unmanageable liability and violates standard software contracting norms. Here is the assessment:

**1. Misalignment of Metrics**
SLAs are designed to guarantee **customer outcomes** (e.g., uptime, response time, data accuracy), not **vendor internal economics**. Your internal compute costs are opaque to the customer and irrelevant to the value they receive. A customer cares if their dependencies are updated correctly and securely, not whether your cloud bill was low. Tying a credit to your internal cost structure introduces a variable you cannot fully control (cloud provider price spikes, architectural changes) into a legal contract, creating a "double jeopardy" scenario where your margins are squeezed by both rising costs and mandatory customer credits.

**2. Legal and Enforceability Risks**
*   **EU Consumer & Contract Law:** In the EU, terms that create a significant imbalance in the parties' rights and obligations to the detriment of the consumer (or smaller business) can be deemed unfair. However, the greater risk here is **ambiguity**. If your internal costs rise due to an AWS/Azure price hike, your SLA technically triggers a credit obligation even if your service performance remains perfect. This creates an uncapped liability loop unrelated to service failure.
*   **Standard Norms:** Standard SaaS SLAs guarantee **Service Availability** (e.g., 99.9% uptime) or **Performance Throughput** (e.g., scan completion within X minutes). They never guarantee the vendor's profit margin or operational efficiency. Deviating from this norm signals immaturity to enterprise legal teams and may cause them to reject the contract entirely during procurement review.

**3. The "Performance Metric" Fallacy**
You mentioned framing this as a performance metric: "if their dependency update time exceeds 2 hours." This is a valid performance metric (speed/latency). However, linking the *credit amount* to your *internal cost savings* ($100 difference) is the error.
*   **Correct Approach:** Guarantee that scans complete within 2 hours. If they don't, provide a standard service credit (e.g., 10% of that month's fee).
*   **Incorrect Approach:** Guarantee that because *you* saved money, *they* get money, contingent on a time threshold. This conflates your P&L with their service levels.

**Recommendation**
1.  **Strip Internal Costs from the SLA:** Rewrite the guarantee to focus solely on the customer outcome: "Guaranteed scan completion within 2 hours."
2.  **Market the Price Drop, Not the Logic:** Aggressively market the new $400 price point as "Passing efficiency savings to you," but keep the *mechanism* of those savings out of the legal text. Marketing copy is flexible; SLAs are binding constraints.
3.  **Consult Counsel:** Yes, you must consult a specialist in EU digital contract law before updating your website or SLA. The EU's Digital Services Act and varying national contract laws require precise language regarding automated decision-making and service guarantees. Do not rely on general templates for this specific cross-border liability.

**Verdict:** The proposed language is dangerous. It binds your legal liability to your internal accounting, creating a precedent that could invalidate your insurance coverage and scare off enterprise counsel. Separate the marketing message (lower price) from the legal guarantee (service speed).
