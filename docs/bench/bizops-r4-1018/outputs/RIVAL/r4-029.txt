### Cash Flow Modeling Framework for BespokeDigital

To prevent overspending deposit money and visualize your liquidity, you must shift from standard accrual accounting to a **13-Week Direct Cash Flow Forecast**. This model tracks actual cash movements week-by-week, explicitly separating the "Cash In" event (deposit) from the "Cash Out" events (fabric/labor) and the final "Cash In" event (delivery payment).

#### 1. The Core Model Structure
Create a spreadsheet with columns representing individual weeks (not months) for the next 26 weeks. Rows should be categorized as follows:

*   **Beginning Cash Balance:** Start with your $60,000.
*   **Inflows - Deposits (50%):** Record $600 per suit in the week the order is placed.
*   **Inflows - Final Payments (50%):** Record $600 per suit in the week of delivery (Order Week + 6).
*   **Outflows - Materials:** Map fabric costs to the specific weeks they are purchased (e.g., Weeks 1–2 of production).
*   **Outflows - Labor:** Map tailor payments to the weeks work is performed (e.g., Weeks 2–5 of production).
*   **Outflows - Fixed OpEx:** Rent, utilities, and admin salaries paid weekly/monthly.
*   **Net Weekly Change:** (Total Inflows) – (Total Outflows).
*   **Ending Cash Balance:** (Beginning Balance) + (Net Weekly Change).

#### 2. Scenario Planning: The "Surge" Test
To answer your specific concern about doubling orders to 40 suits/month, run two parallel scenarios in your model:

*   **Baseline Scenario:** 20 orders/month consistent.
*   **Surge Scenario:** 40 orders starting Month 1.

**What you will likely see:** In the Surge Scenario, your *Beginning Cash* will spike immediately due to 40 x $600 deposits ($24,000 extra inflow). However, your *Outflows* for materials and labor will ramp up steadily over the next 6 weeks. Crucially, the *Final Payments* for these new orders will not appear until Week 7.

If your cumulative outflows during Weeks 1–6 exceed your starting cash plus the new deposits, the model will show a negative **Ending Cash Balance** in those weeks. This negative number represents the exact amount of working capital (or line of credit) required to bridge the gap.

#### 3. Key Variables to Track
To avoid a cash crunch, monitor these four metrics weekly:

1.  **Work-in-Progress (WIP) Liability:** The total value of suits paid for partially (deposits received) but not yet delivered. This is not profit; it is a debt owed in labor and fabric. Never treat this as available profit.
2.  **Cash Conversion Cycle (CCC):** The number of days between paying your tailors/fabric suppliers and receiving the final 50% payment. Your goal is to negotiate terms with suppliers to extend this cycle if possible.
3.  **Burn Rate vs. Deposit Velocity:** Compare your weekly cash outflow rate against the weekly inflow of *new* deposits. If Burn Rate > Deposit Velocity, you are eating into reserves.
4.  **Delivery Cliff:** The specific weeks where few suits are scheduled for delivery (and thus no final payments arrive) while production on new suits is high. These are your danger zones.

#### Decision Rule for Line of Credit
If your "Surge Scenario" shows a negative Ending Cash Balance for more than two consecutive weeks, secure a line of credit *before* the surge begins. The credit limit should equal the deepest negative point in your 6-month forecast plus a 20% safety buffer. Use the line only to cover the timing gap between paying tailors and receiving final payments, paying it down immediately as final payments arrive.

*Note: Update this forecast every Friday with actuals vs. projections to catch delays early. A one-week delay in delivery pushes your final payment inflow out by a week, potentially widening the cash gap.*
