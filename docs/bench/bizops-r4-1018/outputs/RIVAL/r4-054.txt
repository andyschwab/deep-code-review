**General Accounting Principles for SOC 2 Costs**

Based on general accounting principles (US GAAP), the treatment of your $65,000 total SOC 2 cost depends on the specific nature of the expenditure:

1.  **Expensing Immediately (Most Likely for Audit Fees):**
    *   **External Audit Fees ($45,000):** Fees paid to the independent auditor for the examination and report are typically considered period costs. They do not create a separate, saleable asset with a future economic life distinct from the ongoing business. Therefore, these are generally **expensed as incurred** on the income statement.
    *   **Ongoing Compliance:** Costs related to maintaining the certification annually are always expensed.

2.  **Capitalizing (Limited Scope for Implementation):**
    *   **Internal/Implementation Costs ($20,000 CTO time):** You may only capitalize costs directly attributable to *implementing* new controls or software configurations that did not previously exist, provided they create a future economic benefit.
    *   **The Hurdle:** Capitalization requires strict tracking. General planning, training, and maintenance phases must be expensed. If the CTO's time was spent fixing existing gaps to meet a standard rather than building a new distinct asset, it is likely an expense. Furthermore, internal labor costs are often scrutinized heavily by auditors regarding capitalization eligibility.

**Implications for Tax Liability and Financials**

*   **Expensing Approach:** If you expense the full $65,000, you reduce your current year's taxable income by that amount. Given your $1.2M revenue, this provides an immediate tax shield, lowering your current year's tax liability. It also reduces your reported net income for the year.
*   **Capitalizing Approach:** If you capitalize a portion (e.g., the CTO time), you record it as an intangible asset on the balance sheet. You do not get the full tax deduction immediately. Instead, you amortize the cost over the asset's useful life (often 3–5 years for software/security implementations), deducting a small fraction each year. This increases your current year's taxable income (and tax bill) compared to expensing, but smooths expenses over future years.

While the general principles above outline the framework, the specific classification of your CTO's time and the audit fees depends on:
1.  The specific activities logged during the two months (planning vs. development vs. testing).
2.  Your company's specific capitalization policy thresholds.
3.  Current IRS regulations regarding "start-up" vs. "organizational" costs vs. capital improvements.
4.  The risk of audit adjustment if the IRS disagrees with your capitalization stance.

Misclassifying these costs can lead to tax penalties, restated financials, and issues with your own SOC 2 auditors who review your financial controls. Do not finalize your tax filings without written guidance from your CPA firm specific to these line items.
