Based on the facts provided, your product is legally classified as an **over-the-counter (OTC) drug**, not a cosmetic. Under the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act (FD&C Act), the definition of a drug includes articles intended to diagnose, cure, mitigate, treat, or prevent disease, or to affect the structure or function of the body.

Your specific claim of "antibacterial protection" and the assertion that the tea tree oil ingredient "reduces odor-causing bacteria" explicitly claims to affect the biological function of the skin by killing or inhibiting microorganisms. The FDA distinguishes deodorants (cosmetics) from antiperspirants and antibacterial products (drugs) based on **intended use**, which is determined by your labeling and marketing claims, not just the ingredient list.
*   **Cosmetic Deodorant:** Permitted to claim it masks odor or makes the user smell pleasant. It cannot claim to kill bacteria or prevent bacterial growth.
*   **OTC Drug:** Any product claiming to kill bacteria, reduce bacterial count, or provide "antibacterial" effects falls under the OTC Monograph for Antimicrobial Drug Products or the Antiperspirant Monograph (if it also affects sweating).

Because you have made drug claims, you are subject to the following immediate regulatory realities:
1.  **Monograph Compliance:** You must comply with the specific OTC monograph requirements, which include strict testing for safety and efficacy, specific drug fact labeling (Drug Facts panel), and adherence to Current Good Manufacturing Practices (cGMP) for drugs, which are more rigorous than those for cosmetics.
2.  **New Drug Application (NDA):** If your specific formulation or ingredient concentration does not strictly conform to an existing active monograph (many natural antibacterial ingredients do not have established monograph status for this use), your product is considered an unapproved new drug. Selling an unapproved new drug is a violation of federal law.
3.  **Labeling Violation:** Your current labels claiming "antibacterial protection" are misbranded because they lack the required "Drug Facts" panel and because the product has not been approved or cleared under the relevant monograph.

There is no specific concentration threshold of tea tree oil that exempts you from these rules. The trigger is the **claim**. Even trace amounts of an ingredient, if marketed with an antibacterial claim, render the product a drug. Conversely, a high concentration of tea tree oil marketed solely for its scent would remain a cosmetic.
