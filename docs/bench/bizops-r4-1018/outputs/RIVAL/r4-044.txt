**Regulatory Distinction and Risk Assessment**

Selling unfinished wood blanks generally shifts your product classification from a "finished consumer good" to a "component" or "raw material," which can alter regulatory obligations. However, this strategy does not automatically eliminate liability or compliance burdens, particularly if the product is marketed specifically for skateboarding.

**1. Regulatory Agency Guidelines**
You must review guidelines from two primary bodies:
*   **Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC):** The CPSC regulates skateboards under the Consumer Product Safety Improvement Act (CPSIA). Finished skateboards require third-party testing for lead content, phthalates, and mechanical safety (e.g., truck attachment strength), followed by a Children's Product Certificate (CPC) if marketed to those 12 and under.
    *   *The Blank Loophole:* Raw wood blanks *may* be exempt from specific finished-product mechanical testing if they are not "primarily intended" for use by children 12 or under in their sold state. However, if your marketing, packaging, or website explicitly states these blanks are for "skateboard decks," the CPSC may still view them as consumer products subject to safety standards.
*   **Federal Trade Commission (FTC):** You must adhere to the Textile Fiber Products Identification Act if any finishing materials are used, and general labeling laws regarding "Made in USA" claims. Misleading claims that the product is "not a skateboard" to avoid regulation while marketing it as a "skateboard blank" could trigger FTC enforcement for deceptive practices.

**2. Liability and the "Substantial Transformation" Doctrine**
Your concern about customer painting changing the classification is valid but risky.
*   **Manufacturer Liability:** As the creator of the blank, you remain liable for defects in the raw material (e.g., wood delamination, toxic glue, or splintering) regardless of whether the customer paints it. Selling a blank does not indemnify you against product liability lawsuits if the wood fails during use.
*   **Intent Matters:** Courts and regulators look at "foreseeable use." If a reasonable person knows the blank is a skateboard deck, you cannot easily claim it is merely "lumber." If a child uses the blank and is injured because it lacked the structural integrity of a finished deck, your company will likely face negligence claims. The "unfinished" status is a weak defense against personal injury litigation.

**3. Does This Reduce Regulatory Burden?**
*   **Potential Savings:** You may successfully avoid the *specific* cost of third-party mechanical testing and issuing a CPC for the blank, potentially saving the $3,000 annually you cited.
*   **New Risks:** You introduce ambiguity. If the CPSC determines the blanks are "children's products" based on marketing, you could face fines for selling untested goods. Furthermore, you lose quality control over the final product. If a customer paints and rides your blank, then suffers an accident, your brand reputation and legal exposure remain intact.
