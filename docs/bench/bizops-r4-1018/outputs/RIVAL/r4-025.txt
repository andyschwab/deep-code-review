### Legal Risk Assessment: Dynamic Pricing in the EU

Using a dynamic pricing algorithm that reacts to competitor data carries inherent antitrust risks under EU law, regardless of your current revenue (€600k) or market share (2%). There is **no specific "safe harbor" revenue threshold or market share percentage** that automatically exempts a company from scrutiny regarding anti-competitive algorithms. While small market share reduces the *likelihood* of a significant market impact, it does not provide legal immunity if the behavior constitutes a violation.

The primary legal triggers you must avoid are:

**1. Algorithmic Collusion (Tacit Coordination)**
Under Article 101 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union (TFEU), agreements or concerted practices that prevent, restrict, or distort competition are prohibited. The European Commission and national authorities (such as the Bundeskartellamt in Germany) have clarified that collusion can occur via algorithms.
*   **The Risk:** If your algorithm monitors competitors' prices and automatically adjusts yours to match or stabilize them, it can be interpreted as a "concerted practice," even without direct human communication. If multiple competitors use similar software that leads to parallel price movements (e.g., all rising 15% simultaneously during peak season), authorities may infer illegal coordination.
*   **The Trigger:** The mere use of an algorithm that creates a feedback loop with competitors' pricing data can be risky if it results in reduced price competition.

**2. Abuse of Dominance (Article 102 TFEU)**
While less likely given your 2% market share, this article prohibits abusive conduct by dominant firms. Dynamic pricing itself is not abusive, but if a company were dominant, using algorithms to exploit customers (excessive pricing) or exclude competitors (predatory pricing) would be illegal. For VeloLux, the primary concern remains Article 101 (collusion), not dominance.

**3. Unfair Commercial Practices (Consumer Law)**
Beyond antitrust, the EU's Unfair Commercial Practices Directive (UCPD) and the new "Empowering Consumers for the Green Transition" directive scrutinize dynamic pricing.
*   **Transparency:** You must clearly inform consumers if prices are dynamically adjusted. Hiding the fact that prices fluctuate based on demand or data profiles can be deemed misleading.
*   **Personalization:** If the algorithm uses personal data to set individual prices (personalized pricing), strict GDPR compliance and transparency obligations apply.

### Specific Triggers to Avoid

To minimize risk, your algorithm's logic must not:
*   **Explicitly target competitor prices for alignment:** Avoid rules designed to "match" or "undercut by €1" specific competitors automatically. This mimics price-fixing behavior.
*   **Create market-wide price stability:** If your tool contributes to a market environment where prices stop fluctuating competitively and move in lockstep, it invites investigation.
*   **Lack human oversight:** Fully autonomous algorithms that execute price changes without human review are viewed more skeptically. Maintain a "human-in-the-loop" for significant adjustments.

### Conclusion

Your size (€600k revenue, 2% share) makes a full-scale antitrust investigation less probable than for a market leader, but it does not make the *practice* legal if it functions as collusion. The risk lies in the **mechanism** (reacting directly to competitor data to stabilize prices) rather than your size.

**Recommendation:** Configure your algorithm to react only to **internal factors** (your own inventory, your own sales velocity, seasonality) and **aggregate market data** (general demand trends), rather than specific real-time competitor prices. Ensure full transparency on your website regarding how prices are determined.
