**Regulatory Boundary: Cosmetic vs. Drug Classification**

Under current US FDA regulations, the distinction between a cosmetic and a drug is determined strictly by **intended use**, which is derived from your product claims, labeling, and marketing materials—not solely by the ingredients themselves.

1.  **Cosmetic Definition:** Products intended to cleanse, beautify, promote attractiveness, or alter appearance. Your vibrant coloring agents that turn bathwater "deep purple" or "electric blue" generally fall under "altering appearance" or providing a visual experience, which is permissible for cosmetics.
2.  **Drug Definition:** Products intended to diagnose, cure, mitigate, treat, or prevent disease, or to affect the structure or function of the body.

**Impact of Your Specific Claims:**

*   **"Visual Immersion":** This claim describes a sensory/aesthetic experience. It supports a **cosmetic** classification as it relates to the visual alteration of the bathwater and the user's environment, not their physiology.
*   **"Therapeutic Relaxation":** This is the critical risk factor. The term "therapeutic" implies a medical benefit or an effect on the body's function (e.g., reducing stress hormones, treating anxiety). If the FDA interprets "therapeutic relaxation" as a claim to affect the structure or function of the body (the nervous system), your product could be classified as an **unapproved new drug**.
    *   **Does mentioning relaxation change the classification?** Yes, if qualified as "therapeutic." Simple claims like "relaxing scent" or "soaking for relaxation" are often tolerated as general well-being statements. However, explicitly claiming a *therapeutic* effect crosses the line into drug territory because it suggests a physiological change beyond simple aesthetics.

**Compliance Strategy:**
To remain in the cosmetic category, you must scrub all claims suggesting physiological changes or medical benefits. Replace "therapeutic relaxation" with language focused on aesthetics and mood, such as "calming experience," "luxurious soak," or "visually stunning bath." The visual effect (coloring the water) is safe; the implied medical benefit (therapy) is the liability.

**Required Specialist:**

You need to engage a **Regulatory Affairs Consultant specializing in FDA Cosmetics Compliance** (often titled "Cosmetic Regulatory Specialist" or "FDA Compliance Attorney").

*   **Role:** They will review your specific ingredient list (to ensure color additives are FDA-approved for bath use, which is a separate but critical requirement) and audit your marketing copy, packaging, and website text.
*   **Deliverable:** They will provide a "claim substantiation" review, rewriting risky phrases to ensure your "intended use" remains firmly within the cosmetic definition, protecting you from drug classification and the associated $40,000+ approval costs.

*Note: Color additives used in bath bombs must be specifically approved by the FDA for use in the bath; many standard cosmetic colorants are not approved for this specific application regardless of the drug/cosmetic claim issue.*
