With a $3.20 gross profit per unit, you are **not** losing money on the sale itself based *strictly* on the numbers provided ($6.50 - $2.10 - $1.20 = $3.20 positive margin). The risk lies in whether this margin covers your fixed costs and hidden variables.

**Revised Viability Analysis:**

**A. Break-Even Volume for Fixed Overhead**
*   **Monthly Fixed Overhead:** $18,000
*   **Contribution Margin:** $3.20 per unit
*   **Units Needed to Break Even:** 5,625 units ($18,000 / $3.20)
*   **Current Sales:** 4,000 units
*   **Gap:** You are short by 1,625 units (40% increase needed) just to cover overhead, resulting in a monthly operating loss of **$5,200** at current volume.

**B. Realistic Trajectory**
To become profitable, you must increase sales from 4,000 to nearly 6,000 units immediately upon switching. Given a 5% churn rate, you need to acquire ~200 new customers monthly just to maintain volume, let alone grow to the break-even point.

**2. Hidden Cost Factors Eroding Margins**
The $1.20 fee rarely represents the total cost of goods sold (COGS) in a co-manufacturing deal. Watch for these erosions:
*   **Freight & Logistics:** Moving raw materials to the co-manufacturer and finished goods to distributors often adds $0.30–$0.60 per unit.
*   **Minimum Order Quantities (MOQs):** Co-manufacturers often require large batch runs. If you cannot sell the full batch before expiration, waste costs skyrocket.
*   **Slotting & Listing Fees:** Scaling to new stores to hit 5,625 units often requires paying retailers for shelf space, which can cost thousands per SKU.
*   **Payment Terms:** Co-manufacturers often demand net-15 or upfront payment, whereas retailers may pay net-60, creating a cash flow crunch that can halt operations even if unit economics look positive on paper.
*   **Quality Control & Waste:** Pilot kitchens allow for immediate adjustments; co-manufacturers may charge for run-time errors or reject batches that don't meet strict specs, leaving you with unrecoverable material costs.

**Conclusion**
While the unit economics show a positive gross margin of $3.20, your current volume of 4,000 units is insufficient to cover your $18,000 fixed overhead. You need a 41% sales increase immediately to break even. Without a confirmed plan to secure additional retail distribution or raise prices, this transition poses a high risk of cash flow failure due to the gap between current sales and the break-even volume.
