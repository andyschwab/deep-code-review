**General Rules for US Corporations on Foreign Currency Revenue Recognition**

As a US C-Corp, ServerlessFix must adhere to US GAAP (Generally Accepted Accounting Principles), specifically ASC 830 (Foreign Currency Matters). The core principle is that your "functional currency" is the US Dollar (USD) because your primary economic environment (costs, capital, and likely majority revenue) is USD-based. Consequently, all foreign currency transactions must be translated into USD for your financial statements.

**1. Timing of Recognition and Exchange Rates**
You do not wait until conversion to recognize revenue or exchange effects. The process occurs in two stages:
*   **Initial Recognition (Invoicing):** When you issue an invoice in JPY or BRL, you must record the revenue in your USD books immediately using the spot exchange rate on the transaction date (the date the service was performed or the invoice was issued, depending on your specific revenue recognition policy under ASC 606).
*   **Subsequent Measurement (Month-End):** Before you convert the cash, you must revalue any outstanding foreign currency accounts receivable at the end of each reporting period (e.g., month-end) using the current spot rate.

**2. Realizing Gains and Losses**
You must recognize foreign exchange gains or losses **immediately** in your income statement; you do not wait for the cash to be converted to USD.
*   **Unrealized Gains/Losses:** If the exchange rate fluctuates between the invoice date and the balance sheet date, the difference is recorded as an unrealized gain or loss. This hits your P&L immediately.
*   **Realized Gains/Losses:** When the customer actually pays and you convert the funds (or deposit them into a USD account), any difference between the last revalued amount and the final settlement amount is recorded as a realized gain or loss.
*   **Impact:** Both unrealized and realized gains/losses are typically reported in "Other Income/Expense" on your income statement, not as part of gross revenue. This means your net income will fluctuate based on currency markets, even if your operational performance is stable.

**3. Holding Foreign Balances**
Holding balances in JPY or BRL in a foreign bank account does not change the accounting requirement. For your US financial statements, those bank balances must be revalued to USD at the month-end spot rate. The gain or loss from this revaluation is recognized immediately in the P&L.

**4. Thresholds for Consulting a Specialist**
There is no specific statutory dollar threshold in the US tax code or GAAP that triggers a mandatory requirement to hire a specialist. However, you should consult a tax professional specializing in international accounting immediately if:
*   **Complexity vs. Volume:** The administrative burden of tracking daily/monthly exchange rates for hundreds of small transactions outweighs the benefit of local billing.
*   **Repatriation Strategy:** You plan to keep significant cash reserves in JPY or BRL for local operations rather than converting to USD, which may introduce Subpart F income considerations or GILTI (Global Intangible Low-Taxed Income) complexities if you establish foreign subsidiaries later.
*   **Hedging:** You consider using financial instruments (forwards, options) to hedge against currency risk, as hedge accounting under US GAAP is highly complex.
*   **Materiality:** While $23,000 total foreign revenue is currently immaterial for most audit purposes, if this channel grows to exceed **5–10% of your total annual revenue**, the volatility impact on your financial statements becomes material, warranting a formal review of your treasury and accounting policies.

Given your current volume ($23k/month), the primary risk is operational accuracy in tracking rates rather than regulatory non-compliance, but establishing a consistent policy now is critical before scaling.
