**Recommendation: Convert the Debt to Equity Immediately, But Structure It Strategically**

You should absolutely convert the $150,000 loan into equity before approaching Series A investors. Leaving founder debt on the balance sheet is a significant red flag for venture capitalists, not a sign of desperation. It signals financial instability, creates a senior claim on assets that complicates the cap table, and suggests the founders lack confidence in the company's equity value. Investors prefer a clean cap table where all founders are fully aligned through ownership, not creditor relationships.

However, your concern about undervaluing your contribution is valid. Converting at the current implied valuation based on your revenue could indeed shortchange your initial risk. Here is the strategic approach:

**1. The Conversion Mechanism**
Do not simply swap debt for shares at a fixed price today. Instead, convert the debt into a **Safe (Simple Agreement for Future Equity)** or a **Convertible Note** that carries into the Series A round. This instrument should include:
*   **Valuation Cap:** Set a cap that rewards your early risk but remains reasonable for new investors. Given your $360k ARR ($30k MRR) and current burn, a cap between **$4M and $6M** is defensible. This values your early capital at a 2x–4x return multiple relative to the likely Series A pre-money valuation (typically $8M–$12M for a company at your stage seeking $2M).
*   **Discount Rate:** Apply a standard 15–20% discount to the Series A price per share. This is market standard for early risk and avoids the optics of setting an arbitrary low valuation today.

**2. Why This Works**
*   **Cleans the Cap Table:** The debt disappears, replaced by a standard early-stage instrument that investors expect to see.
*   **Fair Valuation:** You secure your upside based on the *future* Series A valuation (which you will drive up with their money), rather than locking in a low valuation today based on current distressed metrics.
*   **Investor Alignment:** It shows you are committed to equity growth, not debt repayment. It removes the awkwardness of you being a creditor who could theoretically call the loan during due diligence.

**3. Addressing Your Fears**
*   **"Desperate Move":** Converting debt is standard procedure. *Not* converting is what looks desperate, as it implies you need the cash back soon or don't believe the equity is worth holding.
*   **"Undervaluing Contribution":** By using a cap/discount rather than a fixed conversion price, you ensure your $150k buys more shares than the new investors' $2M, properly rewarding your early timing without capping the company's potential prematurely.

**Action Plan:**
Draft a Safe with a $5M cap and 20% discount. Present this to your co-founder and legal counsel immediately. When pitching, disclose the conversion as a completed step: "We cleaned up the balance sheet by converting founder debt to equity to align interests for this round." This frames it as a proactive governance decision, not a rescue mission.

*Note: Ensure your interest accrual ($150k principal + ~2 years interest) is included in the conversion amount.*
*Consult a startup attorney to draft the specific instrument terms.*
