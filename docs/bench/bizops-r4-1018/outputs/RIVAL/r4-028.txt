Your proposed sample pack strategy does not constitute predatory pricing and carries negligible legal risk. Predatory pricing laws, both federal (Sherman Act) and state-level unfair competition statutes, are designed to stop dominant companies from intentionally selling below cost to drive competitors out of business and subsequently raise prices to recoup losses. Your situation fails to meet the two fundamental legal prerequisites for a predatory pricing claim:

1.  **Market Power Requirement:** To be liable for predatory pricing, a company generally must possess significant market power (often a monopoly or near-monopoly share) in a specific geographic market. With less than 1% of the spice market, SpiceRoute lacks the capacity to control prices or exclude competition. Regulators and courts do not pursue small businesses for low pricing because a small player cannot "recoup" losses by raising prices later; if you tried to raise prices after driving others out, customers would simply return to your larger competitors.
2.  **Dangerous Probability of Recoupment:** Legal precedent requires a "dangerous probability" that the predator can recoup its investment in below-cost prices. Given your national online presence and tiny market share, you cannot isolate a market long enough to raise prices without losing customers to the thousands of other spice vendors.

Regarding your specific questions on jurisdiction and thresholds:

*   **Which state laws apply?** While you ship nationwide, predatory pricing claims are typically adjudicated where the alleged harm occurs or where the defendant resides. However, because you lack market power, no state's unfair competition laws (including strict ones like California's Unfair Practices Act) would successfully classify this as illegal. These laws often include explicit exemptions for "loss leaders" or promotional sales intended to attract new customers, provided they are not part of a scheme to destroy competition.
*   **Duration and Market Share:** There is no specific "safe harbor" duration or market share threshold written into statutes because the offense itself requires market dominance. Your <1% share effectively immunizes you. The three-month limit further demonstrates a legitimate promotional intent rather than a long-term scheme to distort the market.
*   **Regulatory Factors:** Regulators distinguish between legitimate loss leaders and illegal predation by looking at: (a) the seller's market share, (b) the intent to destroy competition versus attract customers, and (c) the feasibility of recouping losses. Your intent is customer acquisition (a standard marketing practice), your share is insignificant, and recoupment via future monopoly pricing is impossible.
