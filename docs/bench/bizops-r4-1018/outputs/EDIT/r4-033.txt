**The Call**
Capitalize all direct costs (cashews, labor directly involved in aging) and a reasonable portion of overhead (including storage electricity) into your Work-in-Progress (WIP) inventory asset; do not expense these until the cheese is sold. Expensing labor immediately while holding inventory is likely incorrect under standard accrual accounting and will distort your P&L, potentially causing you to understate assets and misstate taxable income.

**The Numbers**
*Formula:* Ending WIP Value = Direct Materials + Direct Labor + Allocated Overhead.
*Your Figures (Q3 Example):*
*   Direct Materials: $22,000
*   Allocated Overhead (given): $15,000
*   Total Reported WIP: $65,000
*   *Implied Direct Labor & Other:* $65,000 - ($22,000 + $15,000) = $28,000.

*Electricity Allocation (Illustrative):*
If monthly storage electricity is $3,000 and the average batch sits for 90 days (approx. 3 months), the electricity cost per full batch is ~$9,000.
*Assumption (ASSUMED):* Your $15,000 allocated overhead already includes a pro-rated share of this electricity. If it does not, you must add the portion of the $3,000/month attributable to the specific units in the aging room at quarter-end to the inventory value.
*Result:* If overhead was under-allocated, your $65,000 figure is too low, meaning you likely expensed too much, reducing your reported profit (and tax) artificially for that quarter, which creates a liability risk upon correction.

**What Flips It, and the Trigger**
The deciding factor is whether your accounting method is **Cash** vs. **Accrual**.
*   *Trigger:* If you are on the Cash method (rare for manufacturers with inventory >$1M gross receipts, but possible for small entities), you might expense costs as paid. However, IRS rules generally require producers to capitalize costs into inventory regardless of cash flow if you maintain inventory accounts.
*   *Rule of Thumb:* If you produce goods for sale, you must capitalize costs. The "flip" occurs if the IRS determines you are improperly expensing production costs, reclassifying them to inventory, and adjusting your taxable income upward.
*   *Step this week:* If your current method expenses aging labor, consider halting that practice immediately and tagging those labor hours to the WIP asset account instead of the P&L expense account.

**Routing Note**
While the general rule (capitalize production costs) is clear under IRS Section 263A (Uniform Capitalization Rules), the specific calculation of "allocated overhead" for a small food producer and the determination of whether your gross receipts allow any simplified inventory methods are binding tax determinations. Bring your specific overhead allocation formula and gross receipt history to a CPA to finalize the Q3 filing and ensure Section 263A compliance.
