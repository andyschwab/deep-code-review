**The Call:** Halt sales and do not distribute the 5,000 printed labels immediately; claiming "antibacterial protection" and citing an active ingredient for that purpose almost certainly reclassifies PureGuard as an Over-the-Counter (OTC) drug under FDA regulations, not a cosmetic. You cannot proceed under cosmetic rules with these specific claims without risking enforcement action (seizure, injunction, or warning letter).

**The Numbers & Regulatory Thresholds:**
The distinction is not mathematical but definitional based on the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act (FD&C Act).
*   **Cosmetic Definition:** Intended to cleanse, beautify, or alter appearance (e.g., "smells good," "reduces wetness" via absorbents).
*   **Drug Definition:** Intended to diagnose, cure, mitigate, treat, or prevent disease, or affect the structure/function of the body (e.g., "kills bacteria," "prevents odor" by stopping bacterial growth).

**Your Specific Triggers:**
1.  **Claim Language:** The phrase "antibacterial protection" is a drug claim. It asserts the product affects the biological function of the skin (killing bacteria) to prevent a condition (odor caused by bacterial decomposition). Cosmetics can claim to "mask odor" or "freshen," but not to stop the biological cause.
2.  **Ingredient Intent:** Marketing tea tree oil specifically as an "antibacterial ingredient" establishes drug intent. Even natural ingredients become drug actives if marketed for therapeutic or prophylactic effects.
3.  **Monograph Applicability:** If classified as a drug, you must comply with an OTC Monograph.
    *   *Antiperspirants:* Have a specific monograph requiring active ingredients like aluminum salts and specific testing for sweat reduction. Tea tree oil is not an approved antiperspirant active.
    *   *Antimicrobial/Skin Protectant:* There are monographs for these, but they require specific active ingredients at defined concentrations and rigorous testing (e.g., challenge testing) to prove efficacy. Tea tree oil is not currently listed as a generally recognized as safe and effective (GRASE) active in the OTC antiperspirant or antimicrobial monographs for this specific use case without new drug application data.

**What Flips It (The Pivot Trigger):**
The single input that changes this from a drug to a cosmetic is the **removal of all disease-mitigating and biological-function claims**.
*   *Current State:* "Antibacterial protection" = Drug.
*   *Required State for Cosmetic:* "Natural deodorant," "Fresh scent," "Neutralizes odor" (via absorption or masking, not killing).
*   *Trigger Value:* If your label, website, or ads mention "bacteria," "germs," "antibacterial," or "preventing odor" (implied biological stop), you are a drug. If you only claim to "smell good" or "mask odor," you are a cosmetic.

Because you have already printed 5,000 labels with non-compliant drug claims, this is a high-stakes compliance event. You must consult an FDA regulatory counsel or a specialized cosmetic/drug compliance attorney before selling.
*   *Exact Question for Counsel:* "Does our specific tea tree oil formulation and 'antibacterial' claim language constitute an unapproved new drug or a misbranded OTC drug under 21 U.S.C. § 301, and what is the safest path to remediate the 5,000 printed labels (e.g., sticker overlay vs. destruction) to avoid an FDA Warning Letter?"
*   *Time Sensitivity:* Immediate. Distributing misbranded drugs invites immediate FDA enforcement.

Do not guess on the concentration thresholds; the claim itself ("antibacterial") triggers the drug status regardless of the percentage of tea tree oil used.
