**The Call**
Your proactive detection does not automatically create a heightened legal duty of care that exposes you to negligence lawsuits for future misses, provided your Terms of Service (ToS) clearly define the service as "best-effort" scanning rather than a guarantee of total security. The client's argument conflates identifying a specific risk with assuming an ongoing insurer-like obligation; in software services, finding one bug does not legally bind you to find all future bugs unless you have explicitly contracted to do so. Your current liability cap (3 months' fees) is a standard and strong defensive layer, but it must be paired with clear language stating that the tool provides "point-in-time" analysis and does not replace comprehensive security auditing.

**The Legal Mechanism & Usual Practice**
*   **Duty of Care:** In most jurisdictions, a duty of care arises from the contract and the reasonable expectations of the service. For security tools, the industry standard (Tier 2 explanation) is that vendors provide a "reasonable effort" to detect known patterns. Courts generally do not impose a duty to be perfect or to catch every future variation of a bug unless the vendor made specific warranties of completeness.
*   **The "Rescue" Doctrine:** The client's fear relies on a misapplication of the "voluntary undertaking" doctrine (often called the rescue doctrine). While starting a rescue can sometimes create a duty to continue it reasonably, in commercial SaaS, this is almost universally contracted away by defining the scope: you are selling a *scanner*, not a *guarantee*.
*   **Liability Caps:** Limiting liability to 3 months of fees is common practice for B2B SaaS. It signals that the service is a utility, not an insurance policy.

**Illustrative Exposure (Hypothetical)**
*   **Scenario:** A client suffers a $500,000 breach due to a missed config.
*   **Without Clear Scope Definition:** If your marketing or contract implies "total protection," a court might ignore the cap, exposing you to the full $500k (minus the cap argument).
*   **With Clear "Best-Effort" Scope:** The court upholds the cap. Your maximum exposure is $3,000 (3 months × $1,000), regardless of the number of clients or bugs found previously.
*   **Assumption:** This assumes your ToS is enforceable in the client's jurisdiction and does not violate local consumer protection laws (unlikely for sophisticated corporate clients).

**What Flips the Call**
The single input that changes this verdict is **specific language in your contract or marketing materials promising "comprehensive," "complete," or "guaranteed" detection.**
*   **Trigger:** If your website or contract says "We catch all misconfigurations" rather than "We scan for known misconfigurations," the duty of care shifts from "reasonable effort" to "strict performance."
*   **Rule of Thumb:** Any absolute promise ("all," "never," "guaranteed") voids the protection of "best-effort" defenses.
*   **Actionable Step:** If your current ToS lacks explicit "point-in-time" and "non-exhaustive" language, consider adding a clause next week stating: "Service provides automated scanning for known patterns at a specific point in time and does not guarantee the absence of all vulnerabilities."

**Routing Note**
The binding determination of whether your specific ToS language holds up in your clients' jurisdictions (especially if they are in the EU, UK, or specific US states like California) is a Tier 3 legal question. You must engage qualified counsel to review your specific Terms of Service and marketing copy to ensure the "best-effort" scope is explicitly defined and the liability cap is enforceable under applicable law.
