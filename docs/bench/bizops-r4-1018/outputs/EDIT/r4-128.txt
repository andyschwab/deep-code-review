**What Flips It, and the Trigger:**
The single input that changes the call is the **specific marketing language** and the **history of the ingredient**.
*   **Trigger:** If your website claims to "treat," "cure," "prevent," or "diagnose" a disease (e.g., "fixes memory loss from Alzheimer's") or if the ingredient lacks a pre-1994 US marketing history, the FDA classifies it as a drug.
*   **Rule of Thumb:** Structure/function claims ("supports focus") are permitted for supplements; disease claims are not.
*   **Action This Week:** If your copy includes any disease-related terms, **consider** stripping them immediately and halting launch until a regulatory attorney reviews the ingredient's NDI status.

**Regulatory Pathways & NDI Requirement:**
*   **Supplement vs. Drug:** A dietary supplement must not be marketed to treat or prevent disease. If an article (ingredient) is studied specifically as a drug (investigational new drug) before being marketed as a supplement, it may be excluded from the supplement definition entirely. Your mention of "European studies for cognitive enhancement" raises a red flag; if those studies were conducted to prove efficacy for a specific condition, the FDA may deem the ingredient an unapproved drug.
*   **NDI Notification:** If any of your five proprietary ingredients were not marketed in the US before October 15, 1994, you **must** file an NDI notification with the FDA 75 days prior to launch. Failure to file renders the product adulterated. Given the "proprietary" nature and European study history, an NDI filing is likely required.
*   **Marketing Language:** Avoid words like "treats," "cures," "prevents," "diagnoses," or references to specific diseases (Alzheimer's, dementia, ADHD). Use "supports," "promotes," or "maintains" paired with normal structure/function (e.g., "supports healthy brain function").
