**What Flips It, and the Trigger:**
The specific trigger is the use of disease-related terminology. Under the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act (FD&C Act), any claim suggesting a product diagnoses, mitigates, treats, cures, or prevents a specific disease (like the "common cold") defines the product as a **drug**.
*   **The Line:**
    *   **Impermissible (Drug Claims):** "Fights colds," "prevents flu," "treats infection," "reduces fever." These imply disease intervention.
    *   **Permissible (Structure-Function Claims):** "Supports immune health," "helps maintain a healthy immune system." These describe the role of a nutrient in affecting normal structure or function.
*   **The Rule:** If you make a structure-function claim on a conventional food, you must include the disclaimer: *"This statement has not been evaluated by the Food and Drug Administration. This product is not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease."*
*   **Decision Threshold:** If your label contains *any* reference to a specific disease state (cold, flu, virus), the product is legally a drug.
*   **This Week's Step:** If your current mockups include "fight off colds," consider halting the print run immediately and rewriting the copy to focus solely on general wellness (e.g., "supports natural defenses") while adding the mandatory FDA disclaimer.

**Routing Note:**
While the distinction between disease claims and structure-function claims is a general rule, the specific application to infant/toddler foods and botanical ingredients (elderberry/echinacea) involves nuanced FDA guidance and potential state-level variations. You must engage a regulatory attorney specializing in FDA food labeling to review your final artwork before production to confirm the claims do not inadvertently cross the drug boundary given your specific ingredient levels and target demographic.
