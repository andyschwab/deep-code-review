**The Call**
Adopt a **weighted annual average** for internal unit economics and investor reporting to prevent seasonal distortion of margins, but maintain **monthly actuals** for your general ledger and tax filings. Do not attribute specific high summer costs only to summer units sold; this artificially depresses summer profitability and inflates winter results, misleading stakeholders about the true steady-state economics of your $12 price point.

**The Numbers**
To model this, you must separate *cash flow timing* (actuals) from *unit cost analysis* (averaged).

*Formula for Annual Average Storage Cost per Month:*
$$(\text{Summer Monthly Cost} \times \text{Summer Months}) + (\text{Winter Monthly Cost} \times \text{Winter Months}) \div 12$$

*Assumptions (Labelled ASSUMED):*
Since you did not specify the exact duration of "summer" vs. "winter" rate hikes, I will assume a standard 4-month peak season (June–Sept) and 8-month off-peak season based on typical utility cycles.
*   **Summer Cost:** $3,500/month (4 months)
*   **Winter Cost:** $2,100/month (8 months)
*   **Production Volume:** 5,000 lbs/month (constant)

*Computed Figures:*
1.  **Total Annual Storage Cost:** $(\$3,500 \times 4) + (\$2,100 \times 8) = \$14,000 + \$16,800 = \$30,800$.
2.  **Average Monthly Storage Cost:** $\$30,800 \div 12 = \$2,567$.
3.  **Storage Cost Per Pound (Averaged):** $\$2,567 \div 5,000 \text{ lbs} = \$0.51/\text{lb}$.
4.  **Storage Cost Per Pound (Summer Actuals):** $\$3,500 \div 5,000 \text{ lbs} = \$0.70/\text{lb}$.
5.  **Storage Cost Per Pound (Winter Actuals):** $\$2,100 \div 5,000 \text{ lbs} = \$0.42/\text{lb}$.

*Result:*
Using actuals, your summer margin appears $\$0.19/\text{lb}$ worse than winter solely due to overhead timing. Using the average, you present a consistent $\$0.51/\text{lb}$ overhead load, revealing that if your base COGS (ingredients + labor) plus $\$0.51$ exceeds your profit target at $\$12$, the business model needs adjustment regardless of the season.

**What Flips It, and the Trigger**
The decision flips if your production volume is **not** constant. If you produce significantly less in the summer (e.g., 3,000 lbs) while costs rise, the per-unit cost spikes drastically, and averaging might hide a capacity utilization crisis.
*   **Trigger:** If summer production volume drops below 4,000 lbs/month while storage costs remain at $3,500, stop averaging immediately.
*   **Step:** This week, overlay your last 12 months of production volume against storage costs. If the correlation is negative (costs up, volume down), model a "stress case" unit economics sheet using peak costs and low volume to test your $45,000 runway survival.
