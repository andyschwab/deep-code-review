**The Call**
Treat the $3 jar deposits as current liabilities on your balance sheet, not revenue, until the jar is returned or the obligation to refund is legally extinguished. Do not recognize unreturned deposits as income immediately based solely on an 85% return rate; doing so risks misstating your financials and could trigger scrutiny during loan underwriting. **The Numbers**
*Formula:* Total Deposit Liability = (Active Jars × Deposit Amount).
*Current State:* 1,500 jars × $3 = **$4,500** recorded as a liability (e.g., "Customer Deposits").
*Target State:* 5,000 jars × $3 = **$15,000** projected liability.

*Breakage (Unreturned Jars) Analysis:*
Assumption: You aim to recognize revenue only on jars statistically unlikely to be returned ("breakage").
*   **Low Case (Conservative):** Recognize $0 until legal expiration. Liability remains $15,000. Cash impact: Neutral (cash held matches liability).
*   **Base Case (Statistical):** 15% non-return rate on 5,000 jars = 750 jars. Potential revenue = 750 × $3 = **$2,250**.
    *   *Constraint:* You cannot book this $2,250 immediately. It is recognized over time or upon legal forfeiture.
*   **High Case (Aggressive/Non-Compliant):** Booking 15% immediately. Revenue +$2,250; Liability reduced to $12,750.
    *   *Risk:* This inflates income prematurely and understates liabilities, potentially misleading lenders regarding your true obligation.

*Working Capital Impact:*
With $60,000 cash assets and a max $15,000 liability, your net working capital remains strong ($45,000+). The liability does not reduce cash; it restricts the *use* of that cash until refunds are paid or breakage is recognized. Lenders typically view deposit liabilities as low-risk if backed by cash, but misclassification as revenue is a red flag.

**What Flips It, and the Trigger**
The single input that changes the accounting treatment is the **statute of limitations for unclaimed property in Washington**.
