**The Call:** Capitalize all direct costs (cashews, labor directly involved in aging) and a reasonable portion of overhead (including storage electricity) into your Work-in-Progress (WIP) inventory asset; do not expense these until the cheese is sold. This matches your costs to your revenue, preventing the artificial profit spikes and tax overpayments you are seeing on your P&L.

**The Numbers:**
Under standard accrual accounting (GAAP), inventory value includes all costs necessary to bring the asset to its present condition and location.
*Formula:* `WIP Value = Direct Materials + Direct Labor + Allocated Overhead`
*Your Figures (Q3 Example):*
*   Direct Materials: $22,000
*   Allocated Overhead (given): $15,000
*   **Missing Input (Direct Labor):** You stated the remainder of the $65,000 is labor/time.
    *   *Calculation:* $65,000 (Total) - $22,000 (Mat) - $15,000 (OH) = **$28,000 (ASSUMED Direct Labor)**.
*   **Electricity Check:** Your $3,000/month storage cost ($9,000/quarter) should be part of the $15,000 overhead allocation. If it was expensed separately, your WIP is understated by ~$9,000, and your taxable income was overstated by that same amount.

*Result:* Your $65,000 ending inventory balance appears correctly structured *if* the $15,000 overhead already includes the pro-rated electricity. If you expensed the electricity monthly instead of allocating it, you must reclassify it into inventory.

**What Flips It, and the Trigger:**
The single input that changes this treatment is the **nature of the labor**. If the labor is administrative or sales-related, it must be expensed immediately (Period Cost), not capitalized. Only labor *directly* touching the product or supervising the aging process is capitalized.
*Trigger:* Review your payroll ledger. If >90% of the $28,000 labor is production staff, keep the current capitalization. If significant portions are sales/admin, reclassify those specific wages to expense.
*Rule of Thumb:* "Would this cost exist if we paused production tomorrow?" If yes (e.g., rent, basic security), it's often overhead; if no (e.g., cheesemakers, aging room monitors), it's direct.
*Step this week:* If your overhead allocation rate is arbitrary, consider switching to an activity-based driver (e.g., machine hours or aging days) to allocate the $3,000 electricity more precisely to the specific batches in the room.

**Routing Note:**
While capitalizing production costs is the standard rule, the specific method for allocating overhead (e.g., normal capacity vs. actual capacity) and any special IRS provisions for agricultural producers (like uniform capitalization rules under IRC §263A) require a binding determination. Bring your Q3 cost ledger to your CPA and ask: "Does our current overhead allocation rate satisfy IRC §263A uniform capitalization requirements for our specific aging process, and are we eligible for any small-business taxpayer exceptions?"
