**The Call:** Calculate your TAM using a bottom-up approach based on annual *new unit demand* rather than total population or consumable market value, treating retail and direct channels as distribution methods for the same end-user to avoid double-counting.

**The Numbers:**
For durable goods, TAM represents the maximum annual revenue if you captured 100% of the *replacement and new adopter* market, not the total population. The standard formula is:
`TAM = (Addressable Units Sold Annually) × (Average Selling Price)`

Using your figures:
*   **Total US Population:** 330 million
*   **Target Segment (20%):** 66 million people
*   **Price Point:** $35

The missing input is **Annual Purchase Frequency**. Unlike bottled water (consumed daily), a reusable bottle is bought once every few years.
*   *Assumption (Low):* Replacement every 5 years (0.2 units/year/person).
    *   Units: 66M × 0.2 = 13.2M units.
    *   TAM: 13.2M × $35 = **$462M**.
*   *Assumption (Base):* Replacement every 3 years + gift buying (0.4 units/year/person).
    *   Units: 66M × 0.4 = 26.4M units.
    *   TAM: 26.4M × $35 = **$924M**.
*   *Assumption (High):* High churn/fashion cycles (0.6 units/year/person).
    *   Units: 66M × 0.6 = 39.6M units.
    *   TAM: 39.6M × $35 = **$1.386B**.

**Distribution Channel Logic:**
Do not add "Direct Potential" + "Retail Potential." Your TAM is defined by the *end customer*, not the shelf. Whether a customer buys from Target or your website, they are one unit of demand. Your distribution strategy determines your *Serviceable Obtainable Market* (SOM)—how much of that TAM you can actually reach—not the size of the TAM itself.

**What Flips It, and the Trigger:**
The **replacement cycle length** is the single variable that changes the verdict. If your product lasts 10 years, your TAM shrinks by half compared to a 5-year cycle.
*   *Trigger:* If customer surveys indicate a replacement intent of <4 years, your TAM exceeds $1B; if >6 years, it drops below $400M.
*   *Action:* This week, survey 50 current owners asking, "When did you buy your previous reusable bottle?" to establish a data-driven replacement rate rather than guessing.
