**What Flips It, and the Trigger:**
The single input that changes the outcome is the **specific wording of your marketing claims** and the **history of the specific ingredient**.
*   **The Trigger:** If your website or label claims to "treat," "cure," "prevent," or "diagnose" a disease (e.g., "prevents Alzheimer's") or implies a therapeutic effect supported by the European studies you mentioned, the product is legally a drug under the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act (FD&C Act).
*   **Rule of Thumb:** Structure claims strictly as "supports memory" or "promotes cognitive function" (structure/function claims) and avoid any reference to disease states or clinical outcomes.
*   **This Week's Step:** If you have draft copy, stop all design/printing and engage a regulatory attorney immediately to redact any language that crosses the line from "supplement" to "drug."

**Regulatory Pathways & Filing Requirements:**
*   **Supplement vs. Drug:** A dietary supplement must be intended to supplement the diet and cannot claim to treat disease. If an article (ingredient) has been investigated as a new drug for which substantial clinical investigations have been instituted and made public, it may be excluded from the supplement definition. Your mention of European studies raises this exact "drug exclusion" flag.
*   **New Dietary Ingredient (NDI) Notification:** If any of your five proprietary ingredients were not marketed in the US before October 15, 1994, you **must** file an NDI notification with the FDA 75 days before launch. Failure to file renders the product adulterated. Given the "proprietary" nature and European origin, an NDI filing is highly likely required.
*   **Who to Hire:** You need a regulatory attorney or consultant specializing in **FDA dietary supplement law** (not general IP or corporate counsel). Look for firms with specific experience in "NDI notifications" and "structure/function claim substantiation."
