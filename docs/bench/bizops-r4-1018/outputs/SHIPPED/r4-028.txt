**The Call**
Proceed with the sample pack promotion; your strategy carries negligible legal risk of a predatory pricing claim because you lack the market power to recoup losses, which is the essential legal element of predation.

**The Numbers**
Predatory pricing analysis relies on two economic tests: (1) selling below cost, and (2) a dangerous probability of recouping those losses through monopoly power later.
*   **Your Price:** $5.00 per pack.
*   **Your Cost (COGS):** $7.50 per pack.
*   **Loss per Unit:** $2.50.
*   **Monthly Volume (Estimated):** ~2,315 standard jars ($22k / $9.50). Even if sample packs double your volume to 4,600 units, your monthly "predatory" loss is ~$11,500.
*   **Market Share:** <1% (User provided).
*   **Recoupment Feasibility:** **Zero.** To recoup a $34,500 total campaign loss (3 months), you would need to raise prices significantly above competitive levels without losing customers. With <1% share and thousands of competing spice brands, you cannot force the market to accept higher prices.

**What Flips It, and the Trigger**
The single input that changes this call is **Market Power**. Predatory pricing claims generally fail unless the defendant has sufficient market share (often >30-50% depending on the circuit) to drive competitors out and then raise prices.
*   **Trigger:** If your market share exceeds 30% in a specific geographic niche or product category, reconsider.
*   **Rule of Thumb:** Regulators and courts view "loss leaders" (selling below cost to attract customers) as legitimate competition for small players. They only view it as "predatory" when a dominant player uses deep pockets to starve out rivals.
*   **Step this week:** Document the business justification internally as "customer acquisition cost" (CAC) rather than "undercutting competitors." If your CAC for a new customer is normally $15, spending $2.50 to acquire them via a sample is financially sound, not legally suspect.

**Routing Note**
While federal law (Sherman Act) and most state laws require proof of recoupment (which you cannot satisfy), a few states (e.g., California, Texas) have specific statutes prohibiting sales below cost *with the intent* to harm competition. Since your intent is clearly acquisition and you lack market power, a formal legal opinion is likely unnecessary unless a competitor threatens suit. If you receive a cease-and-desist letter, route it immediately to an antitrust attorney to cite the "meeting competition" or "promotional" defenses specific to that state's statute.
