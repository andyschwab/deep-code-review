**The Numbers & Regulatory Thresholds:**
The distinction between a cosmetic and a drug is not determined by ingredient concentration thresholds in this context, but by **intended use** as defined by your claims and labeling.
*   **Cosmetic Definition:** Articles intended to be rubbed, poured, or sprayed on the human body for cleansing, beautifying, promoting attractiveness, or altering appearance (e.g., standard deodorants that mask odor).
*   **Drug Definition:** Articles intended for use in the diagnosis, cure, mitigation, treatment, or prevention of disease, or to affect the structure or any function of the body (e.g., antiperspirants that stop sweating or deodorants that kill bacteria).

**Your Specific Trigger:**
*   **Claim:** "Antibacterial protection" and "reduces odor-causing bacteria."
*   **Effect:** These claims assert that the product affects a function of the body (killing bacteria) rather than merely masking odor.
*   **Result:** Under the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act, this makes PureGuard an **Over-the-Counter (OTC) Drug**.
*   **Required Compliance:** You must comply with the **OTC Monograph for Antimicrobial Drug Products** (or obtain a New Drug Application approval, which is cost-prohibitive for startups). This monograph dictates:
    1.  **Approved Active Ingredients:** Tea tree oil is generally *not* an accepted active ingredient in the current OTC Antimicrobial Monograph for consumer antiseptic washes or leave-on products without specific new data.
    2.  **Testing:** You must perform specific efficacy testing (e.g., time-kill studies) to prove the product works as claimed.
    3.  **Labeling:** Labels must include a "Drug Facts" panel, not just cosmetic ingredient lists. The current "cosmetic" labels are legally misbranded if sold with drug claims.

**What Flips It, and The Trigger:**
*   **The Flip Input:** The removal of all "antibacterial," "kills bacteria," or "germ-reducing" claims from your marketing and packaging.
*   **The Trigger Value:** If you reposition the claim strictly to "masks odor" or "freshens" (cosmetic claims) and remove references to the mechanism of action (killing bacteria), the product reverts to cosmetic status.
*   **Decision Threshold:** If the label says "antibacterial," it is a drug. If it says "deodorant" (implying odor masking only), it is a cosmetic.
*   **Immediate Step:** If you cannot validate tea tree oil against the OTC Monograph requirements immediately, consider Y: Quarantine the 5,000 labels, revise the copy to remove all antimicrobial claims (focusing solely on scent/odor masking), and reprint before distribution.
