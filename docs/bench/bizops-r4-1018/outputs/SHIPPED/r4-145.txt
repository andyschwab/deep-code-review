**The Analysis:**
In the United Kingdom, software that provides active triage advice (categorizing symptoms into urgency levels) is typically classified as a Medical Device under the UK Medical Devices Regulations 2002 (as amended), specifically falling under Software as a Medical Device (SaMD) guidance issued by the MHRA. Because your AI interprets symptoms to drive clinical decision-making ("when to seek care"), it is not merely providing general wellness information; it is performing a medical function.

Standard Technology Errors & Omissions (E&O) policies generally cover software failures, bugs, or downtime, but they explicitly exclude "professional liability" or "medical malpractice" arising from the provision of medical advice or services. Your legal advisor is correct: if your algorithm fails to flag a high-urgency symptom and a user suffers harm, a plaintiff would argue you assumed a duty of care by offering diagnostic triage. In this scenario, a standard disclaimer ("this is not medical advice") often fails as a defense because the product's actual function contradicts the warning. Courts and regulators look at the reasonable reliance of the user; if the app tells a user their symptoms are "low urgency," the user relies on that assessment regardless of the fine print.

Consequently, your current insurance setup is likely inadequate. The specific financial risk is not just the cost of a lawsuit, but the potential denial of coverage by your existing carrier when a claim is filed, leaving you personally liable for defense costs and damages. The "practice of medicine" argument in the UK is less about licensing individual developers and more about the regulatory status of the device and the liability associated with its intended purpose.
