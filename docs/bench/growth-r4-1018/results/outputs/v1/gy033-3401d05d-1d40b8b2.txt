**Interpretation of Conflicting Signals**
The divergence between open rates and revenue indicates that while fewer emails reduce fatigue (higher opens), they critically fail to maintain top-of-funnel awareness required for our specific product category (artisanal kits). In a four-week window, a €4,200 gap suggests the purchase cycle for our core customers is shorter than the frequency reduction allows. The "awareness decay" in Group B appears immediate, whereas the benefit to sender reputation (if any) has not yet translated to monetary value.

**Decision Framework**
We cannot treat the Day 28 result as a final "win" for either side without addressing the lag hypothesis, but we also cannot ignore the realized loss.
1.  **Revenue Priority:** The primary metric is Total Conversion Value. Group A won decisively here.
2.  **Lag Verification:** To validate the "longer cycle" theory, we must analyze the *time-to-conversion* distribution for both groups. If Group B's conversions are merely shifted by 3–5 days, the cumulative revenue would converge by week 6. If the gap remains static or widens, the frequency reduction permanently suppresses demand.
3.  **Guardrail Check:** Open rate is a secondary metric. A win on engagement that loses money is a failed experiment.

**Recommendation & Next Steps**
**Decision:** Proceed with **Group A (4 emails/week)** for the immediate Q4 budget allocation to secure holiday revenue. The risk of under-sourcing demand during the peak season outweighs the unproven potential of long-tail conversion from lower frequency.

The data shows a clear short-term revenue penalty for reduced frequency. Unless the lag analysis proves a definitive delayed conversion spike within 48 hours, the safe financial decision is to maintain current frequency.
