### Risk Assessment: MediConnect Triage Module Rollout

**Executive Summary**
Extrapolating the Ohio pilot results to the full 40-hospital network carries high risk due to unverified behavioral causality and demographic mismatch. The observed 20% efficiency gain and 8-point satisfaction drop are currently **correlated outcomes**, not proven causal mechanisms. Approving a full rollout without resolving whether speed gains stem from workflow optimization or the omission of essential care steps poses a direct patient safety and liability risk.

**Key Risks in Extrapolation**

1.  **Demographic Validity Gap (Urban vs. Rural)**
    The pilot sample (100% urban) is statistically non-representative of the target population (majority rural). Rural hospitals often face different resource constraints, patient acuity profiles, and connectivity issues.
    *   **Risk:** The "speed gain" observed in well-resourced urban centers may vanish in rural settings where doctors spend more time compensating for lack of support staff or technology, potentially exacerbating the satisfaction dip if the module forces a rigid workflow unsuited to rural realities.

2.  **Unverified Causality (Efficiency vs. Safety)**
    The assumption that doctors are "skipping non-essential questions" is currently a hypothesis, not a measured fact.
    *   **Risk:** If doctors are skipping *clinically relevant* questions to achieve speed, the 20% time reduction represents a degradation of care quality, not an improvement. The 8-point satisfaction drop may indicate patients feel unheard or rushed, signaling a fundamental flaw in the triage logic rather than a temporary learning curve.

3.  **Sample Size and Statistical Power**
    With only 150 doctors across three sites, the data lacks the power to detect low-frequency but high-severity adverse events or to segment results by doctor experience level.
    *   **Risk:** The aggregate 8-point drop may hide severe dissatisfaction in specific sub-groups (e.g., elderly patients or complex cases) that will scale linearly across 40 hospitals, leading to widespread contract churn or regulatory scrutiny.

**Critical Questions to Answer Before Rollout**

To distinguish between a "learning curve" and a "fundamental flaw," the following questions must be answered using data, not assumptions:

*   **What specific fields are being skipped?** We need an event-level audit comparing completion rates of every triage question in the pilot vs. baseline. If "non-essential" fields are undefined, we cannot verify if critical clinical data is being lost.
*   **Does the satisfaction drop correlate with specific patient cohorts?** Is the 8-point drop uniform, or is it driven by specific demographics (e.g., age, condition severity) that are more prevalent in rural networks?
*   **Is the time reduction sustained after the novelty effect wears off?** Pilot participants often show temporary efficiency spikes due to heightened attention. Without a control group or a longer timeline, the 20% gain may regress to the mean upon full rollout.
*   **How does rural infrastructure impact module performance?** Have we tested the module's latency and usability in low-bandwidth environments typical of the target rural hospitals?

**Recommendation**
Do not approve the full rollout yet. The current data supports a **targeted expansion** to 2–3 rural hospitals specifically to test the demographic hypothesis and instrument the "skipped questions" theory. Treat the current 20% gain as **UNVERIFIED** for the broader network until the causal link between skipped fields and time savings is proven.

*Note: This assessment relies solely on the provided pilot figures; no external benchmarks for "acceptable" satisfaction drops were used as they vary by context.*
*Next Step: Instrument specific field-completion events in the next pilot phase to validate the skipping hypothesis.*
