**Recommendation: Optimize for Gross Merchandise Value (GMV), not Transaction Volume.**

For a two-sided marketplace like SecondHand Stockholm, GMV is the superior North Star because it inherently balances the tension between buyer demand and seller liquidity. Transaction volume is a vanity metric in this context; your data already proves it can rise while unit economics deteriorate (volume +30%, price -15%). Optimizing for volume alone incentivizes low-value transactions that may fail to cover operational costs or satisfy sellers, directly threatening the supply side.

**The Argument for Investors:**
1.  **Seller Liquidity Guardrail:** Your hypothesis identifies a critical risk: if prices drop too low, sellers exit. GMV captures price sensitivity immediately. If volume rises but GMV flattens or drops, it signals that you are attracting buyers who devalue the inventory, warning of impending seller churn. Volume masks this decay; GMV exposes it.
2.  **Seasonal Reality:** In the Swedish market, Q4 winter coats drive high-value transactions. A volume-only target might encourage pushing cheap summer accessories in Q4 to hit unit counts, diluting the high-margin seasonal opportunity. GMV aligns incentives with the natural revenue peaks of the fashion calendar.
3.  **Sustainable Growth:** In a saturated Nordic market, acquiring low-intent buyers for cheap items increases support and logistics load without proportional revenue. GMV ensures that growth correlates with actual economic value exchanged, protecting the unit economics required for long-term viability.

**Addressing the Data Gap:**
You noted a lack of data correlating price drops to seller churn. Until this is instrumented, GMV acts as the leading indicator. A divergence where Volume ↑ and GMV → (or ↓) is the specific signal that seller liquidity is eroding. Do not wait for churn data to act; treat a decoupling of volume and GMV as the early warning system for supply shortage.
