### Executive Summary: Dynamic Routing Pilot Analysis

The rapid decay from a 14.5% reduction to a 1.8% increase in fuel consumption suggests the initial gain was likely a **statistical anomaly driven by the "Novelty Effect" and favorable early weather**, rather than a sustained algorithmic improvement. The subsequent reversal indicates that under stress (blizzard conditions), the algorithm's rigid optimization likely conflicted with real-time driver judgment, leading to inefficiencies when drivers overridden the system or when the system routed trucks into sub-optimal conditions.

The pattern follows a classic "regression to the mean" followed by "negative adaptation." The first seven days likely coincided with stable ice conditions where the algorithm's theoretical efficiency held true. Once environmental variables shifted (blizzards), the algorithm lacked the dynamic input to adjust, causing drivers to lose confidence and manually override routes. These overrides often result in "thrashing"—frequent route recalculations or hesitant driving patterns that consume more fuel than a steady, experienced human-driven route.

### Recommended Operational Data Investigation

To isolate the cause before the Tuesday deadline, you must triangulate **environmental context**, **human behavior**, and **system logic**. Investigate these specific data points immediately:

1.  **Weather-Adjusted Performance Correlation**
    *   **Action:** Overlay daily fuel consumption data with localized weather reports (wind speed, visibility, temperature) for the specific routes taken during the pilot.
    *   **Goal:** Determine if the decay correlates strictly with the onset of blizzard conditions. If fuel efficiency dropped only on days with >40km/h winds, the algorithm likely fails to weight weather risks heavily enough.

2.  **Override Frequency and Timing**
    *   **Action:** Extract logs showing exactly when and where drivers manually overridden the suggested route. Cross-reference these timestamps with fuel spikes.
    *   **Goal:** Validate your assumption. If overrides cluster around the decay period (Days 8–14) and specifically precede fuel inefficiencies, the issue is human-machine friction. If overrides are low but fuel is still high, the algorithm itself is routing poorly in bad weather.

3.  **Route Deviation vs. Idle Time**
    *   **Action:** Compare "planned route distance" vs. "actual distance traveled" and "engine idle time" for the treatment group versus the control group.
    *   **Goal:** Distinguish between *routing errors* (algorithm sending trucks too far) and *operational hesitation* (drivers stopping frequently due to lack of trust in the system). High idle time suggests driver uncertainty; high distance suggests algorithmic failure.

4.  **Driver Segmentation**
    *   **Action:** Break down fuel data by individual driver. Identify if specific experienced drivers maintained efficiency while others struggled.
    *   **Goal:** Determine if the issue is training-related. If veteran drivers succeeded where rookies failed, the rollout may require a phased training program rather than a software fix.

### Strategic Recommendation for COO

**Do not roll out to the entire fleet of 200 vehicles by next Tuesday.** The risk of increasing fuel costs across the whole fleet during the critical pre-thaw window is too high given the negative trend in the final week of the pilot.

Instead, recommend a **"Hybrid Mode" extension**:
*   Limit the software to **fair-weather conditions only** (hard-coded weather thresholds).
*   Run a targeted 2-week follow-up pilot with **mandatory override logging** to quantify the "trust gap."
*   Proceed with full rollout only after the algorithm is tuned to defer to human judgment during severe weather events.

*Note: The initial 14.5% gain should be treated as a "best-case scenario" benchmark for ideal conditions, not an average expectation. Immediate access to driver logbooks is essential to confirm the override hypothesis.*
