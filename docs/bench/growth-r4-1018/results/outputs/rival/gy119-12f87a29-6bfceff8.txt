### Executive Summary: ArcticLine Pricing Experiment Analysis

**Recommendation:** Do not roll out the 12% discount permanently. The current strategy destroys value. Immediately revert to standard pricing for Q4 while conducting a targeted 8% discount test on a specific segment before any broader implementation.

#### 1. Interpretation of Conflicting Signals

The conflict between rising volume and falling margins is not a data error; it is a mathematical certainty driven by your unit economics. The experiment successfully stimulated demand but failed to preserve profitability.

**The Volume-Margin Trade-off**
Your 28% volume increase was insufficient to offset the 12% price reduction because industrial refrigeration units likely have high fixed costs and significant variable costs (materials, warranty reserves). When you cut price by 12%, you removed 12% of revenue from *every* unit sold, not just the incremental ones.

To maintain the same total gross profit dollar amount after a 12% price cut, you would have needed a volume increase of approximately **55-60%** (depending on your exact initial margin structure), not 28%. Since you only achieved 28%, the total profit pool shrank drastically, reflected in the margin drop from 34% to 21%.

**The "Mix Shift" Hypothesis**
Your assumption is correct: the discount altered the customer mix.
*   **Control Group (North):** Purchased by established clients with higher lifetime value (LTV) and standard warranty terms.
*   **Test Group (South):** Attracted price-sensitive smaller businesses. These customers not only bought at a lower price but likely negotiated extended warranties, further increasing your cost of goods sold (COGS) and depressing the margin beyond the simple price cut.

The 21% margin suggests that for every €100 of revenue generated in the south, you retained only €21, compared to €34 in the north. In absolute terms, unless your total revenue more than doubled (which it didn't; it rose ~28%), your total gross profit dollars declined.

#### 2. Scenario Analysis: The 8% Discount Question

You asked if an 8% discount would have balanced volume and margin. While we cannot know the exact elasticity without running the test, we can apply logical bounds:

*   **The Breakeven Point:** To break even on profit dollars with an 8% price cut, you typically need a volume lift of roughly **30-35%** (assuming similar cost structures).
*   **The Likely Outcome:** An 8% discount is less attractive than 12%. It is highly probable the volume lift would have been lower than 28% (perhaps 15-20%).
*   **The Risk:** If an 8% discount yields only a 15% volume increase, you still lose total profit dollars, though perhaps less severely than the 12% scenario. However, if the "bad mix" (smaller businesses demanding extra warranties) persists even at 8%, the margin erosion remains a structural issue unrelated to the discount depth.

#### 3. Strategic Recommendation for Q4

**Immediate Action: Revert to Standard Pricing**
Continuing the 12% discount into Q4 will compound the loss of gross profit dollars. The current trajectory sacrifices long-term brand value and profitability for short-term unit volume that does not pay for itself.

**Next Steps: Targeted Testing**
Before abandoning discounts entirely, isolate the variable causing the margin bleed:
1.  **Segment the Offer:** Test an 8% discount *only* for existing customers or specific industries with historically high LTV, excluding new, small-scale buyers.
2.  **Decouple Warranty:** Offer the price discount with *standard* warranty terms only. Make extended warranties a paid add-on. This tests if the volume lift exists without the associated cost burden.
3.  **Measure Profit per Unit, Not Just Margin %:** Focus the CFO's dashboard on "Total Gross Profit Dollars" generated per region, not just the percentage margin. A lower margin % is acceptable only if the total profit dollar amount increases significantly.

**Conclusion for Stakeholders**
The experiment proved that price sensitivity exists in the southern region, but the current offer structure attracts unprofitable revenue. We must stop the bleed immediately and redesign the offer to ensure incremental volume contributes positively to the bottom line.
