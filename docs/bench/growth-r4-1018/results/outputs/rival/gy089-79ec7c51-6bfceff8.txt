### Hypotheses for the Conversion Rate Discrepancy

The divergence between increased add-to-basket actions (+3.5%) and flat purchase conversion (2.1%) suggests the UI change successfully reduced friction for *intent* but failed to address barriers to *completion*. The following hypotheses explain why revenue has not moved:

1.  **The "Window Shopper" Inflation Hypothesis:** The prominent button encourages low-intent users to add items to the basket as a "saving" mechanism without genuine purchase intent. These users inflate the top-of-funnel metric but drop off immediately upon seeing the total cost (shipping/taxes) in the cart, leaving the final conversion rate unchanged.
2.  **The Downstream Friction Hypothesis:** Your assumption is correct; the button size is irrelevant if downstream friction (unexpected shipping costs, lack of preferred payment methods, or mandatory account creation) remains the primary blocker. The UI change merely moved users faster to the point where they ultimately abandon.
3.  **The Basket Bloat Hypothesis:** Users may be adding more items per session but not completing checkout due to "sticker shock" when the cumulative total is revealed. This increases add-to-basket counts but not transaction volume.
4.  **The Technical/Performance Hypothesis:** The larger button or associated script may have inadvertently increased page load times on mobile devices, causing latency-sensitive users to abandon the session after clicking but before the cart page fully renders.

### Proposed Experiments to Isolate the Cause

To validate these hypotheses before the August 15 review, execute the following targeted experiments:

**Experiment A: Cart Page Intervention (Tests Downstream Friction)**
*   **Hypothesis:** If downstream friction is the blocker, addressing it immediately in the cart will convert the additional traffic generated by the new button.
*   **Variant:** On the cart page, display a prominent "Free Shipping Threshold" progress bar and offer guest checkout by default for users arriving from the PDP.
*   **Metric:** Cart-to-Purchase conversion rate.
*   **Success:** If this variant lifts overall conversion above 2.1%, the issue is downstream friction, not the quality of the add-to-basket traffic.

**Experiment B: The "Intent" Filter (Tests Window Shopper Hypothesis)**
*   **Hypothesis:** If the button is attracting low-intent users, adding a micro-commitment step will filter them out without hurting high-intent buyers.
*   **Variant:** Change the button behavior to open a quick modal confirming size/color before adding to the basket, rather than an immediate add.
*   **Metric:** Add-to-basket rate and subsequent Purchase Conversion Rate.
*   **Success:** If the add-to-basket rate drops but the *conversion rate* of those who do add increases significantly, the original button was indeed inflating metrics with low-quality clicks.

**Experiment C: Segment-Specific Analysis (Non-Test Diagnostic)**
*   **Action:** Immediately segment the last 10 days of data by device (Mobile vs. Desktop) and Traffic Source (Paid vs. Organic).
*   **Goal:** Determine if the 3.5% lift is isolated to a specific segment (e.g., mobile paid traffic) that historically has lower conversion quality. This requires no dev time and can be done instantly in GA4.

**Recommendation:** Prioritize **Experiment A**. Since you already have increased traffic entering the cart, fixing the leak there offers the highest potential ROI. If Experiment A fails to move the needle, the issue is likely the quality of traffic (Hypothesis 1), requiring a redesign of the button interaction (Experiment B).
