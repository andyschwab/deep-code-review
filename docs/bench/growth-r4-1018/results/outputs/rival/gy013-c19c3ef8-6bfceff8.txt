**Recommendation: Do Not Roll Out Based on Current Data**

The observed 25% lift in click-through rate (CTR) is statistically confounded and cannot be attributed to the design change alone. Because the treatment group contained a 40% overrepresentation of the 18–24 demographic—a segment with inherently different engagement patterns than your core 35–50 buyer persona—the result is a mixture of design effect and demographic skew. Rolling out globally based on this data risks optimizing for clicks from non-buyers while potentially alienating your primary revenue drivers.

**Immediate Analytical Actions**

To salvage insights from this experiment, you must isolate the design effect from the demographic noise using one of the following two approaches:

1.  **Segmented Analysis (Recommended for Speed)**
    Restrict your analysis to the overlapping population where the groups are comparable. Specifically, compare Treatment vs. Control *only* within the 35–50 age bracket.
    *   If the lift disappears or becomes negative in this segment, the design likely appeals only to younger users and may harm core conversion.
    *   If the lift persists in the 35–50 segment, the design has universal appeal, and the aggregate 25% was simply amplified by the younger skew.
    *   *Note:* This reduces your sample size and statistical power, so check confidence intervals carefully. A wide interval including zero means the data is inconclusive for your core persona.

2.  **Statistical Adjustment (Post-Stratification)**
    If you need an aggregate estimate, re-weight the treatment group data to match the demographic distribution of the control group (and your actual user base).
    *   Calculate the CTR for each age bucket (e.g., 18–24, 25–34, 35–50, 50+) separately for both groups.
    *   Apply the control group's demographic weights to the treatment group's bucketed CTRs to generate a "demographically adjusted" treatment CTR.
    *   Compare this adjusted CTR against the control's actual CTR. This simulates what the result would have been had the randomization been balanced.

**Strategic Guidance for Stakeholders**

Present the findings to the Product Director and UX team with this narrative: "The initial 25% lift is driven largely by a demographic imbalance rather than pure design efficacy. Our core buyers (35–50) require a separate evaluation."

*   **If the 35–50 segment shows no lift:** The design is not ready for global rollout. It may increase low-value traffic while diluting high-value user experience. Recommend iterating on the design to better resonate with the core persona before re-testing.
*   **If the 35–50 segment shows positive lift:** You have validation for the core business. You can proceed with a cautious rollout, monitoring purchase conversion rates closely, as CTR does not equal revenue.

**Next Steps**
Do not attempt to "fix" the p-value of the aggregate data; the experiment protocol was breached. Treat this as a learning opportunity about randomization checks. Plan a follow-up test with strict stratified randomization to ensure age demographics are balanced across variants before launch.

*Always validate CTR lifts against downstream conversion and revenue metrics before full implementation, especially when demographic shifts are detected.*
