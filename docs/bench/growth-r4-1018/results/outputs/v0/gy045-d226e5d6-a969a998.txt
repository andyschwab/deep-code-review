**Recommendation: Optimize for Gross Merchandise Value (GMV), not Transaction Volume.**

For a two-sided marketplace like SecondHand Stockholm, GMV is the superior North Star because it inherently balances the tension between buyer demand and seller liquidity. Transaction volume is a vanity metric in this context; it rose 30% (50k to 65k units) while GMV effectively stagnated or declined slightly (from 22.5M SEK to 24.7M SEK, a mere 10% rise despite the volume surge). This divergence signals that you are acquiring low-value transactions that do not compensate sellers adequately, directly threatening the supply side.

**The Argument for Investors:**
1.  **Seller Liquidity is the Constraint:** In a saturated Nordic market, supply (quality used fashion) is the scarce resource, not demand. If average order value (AOV) drops from 450 to 380 SEK, sellers receive less net value per listing. Without data on seller churn, we must treat the falling AOV as a leading indicator of supply risk. Optimizing for volume incentivizes further price erosion, potentially causing high-quality sellers to exit, creating a "lemon market" where only low-value items remain.
2.  **GMV Aligns Incentives:** GMV (Price × Volume) forces the product team to improve both conversion and price realization. To grow GMV, you must either increase volume *without* crashing prices, or increase AOV. This prevents the "race to the bottom" that volume-only targets encourage.
3.  **Seasonality & Quality:** Winter coats drive Q4 revenue. These are high-AOV items. A volume-centric model might inadvertently deprioritize these complex, high-value listings in favor of easy, low-value summer accessories to hit unit targets. GMV ensures the strategy respects the seasonal mix of high-value inventory.

**Immediate Risks & Unknowns:**
You stated you lack data on seller churn correlated with price drops. Therefore, any claim that "sellers are leaving" is currently **UNVERIFIED**. You cannot assert the magnitude of the supply leak yet. However, the *risk* is proven by the falling AOV. The argument to investors is not that sellers *are* leaving, but that the current metric (volume) blindfolds the company to the *probability* of them leaving.

**This Week's Steps:**
1.  **Instrument the "Seller Retention" Question:** Define and track a cohort metric: "Percentage of sellers who list a second item within 60 days, segmented by the AOV of their first sale." This directly tests the hypothesis that low prices drive churn. Do not track all seller events; only this specific question.
2.  **Re-baseline the Dashboard:** Replace "Monthly Transaction Volume" with "Monthly GMV" as the primary headline metric for the investor deck. Add "Average Order Value" as the critical input metric.
3.  **Run a Guardrailed Experiment:** If you plan to stimulate volume, run an A/B test where the primary metric is GMV lift and the **guardrail** is seller retention rate. Do not peek at results before the pre-committed sample size is reached; a early spike in volume with a lagging drop in seller return rates is a false win.
