Given your constraints—specifically the lack of historical data and the high stakes involving student grades and disciplinary records—your pre-launch safety checklist must prioritize defensive guardrails over performance optimization. Since you cannot rely on statistical validation from past cases, you must rely on rigorous procedural controls and synthetic stress-testing.

### 1. Bias Mitigation in Language Interpretation
Without historical data to reveal systemic biases, you must proactively test for them using your synthetic dataset. The model may inadvertently favor articulate students or penalize non-native speakers, or it might misinterpret emotional distress as aggression.

*   **Synthetic Adversarial Testing:** Expand your synthetic examples to include specific edge cases:
    *   **Linguistic Diversity:** Include appeals written with varying levels of English proficiency, slang, and non-standard grammar to ensure the scorer evaluates the *argument*, not the writing style.
    *   **Emotional Variance:** Create examples where students are angry, distressed, or overly formal. Verify the system does not penalize high-emotion language if the core argument is valid.
    *   **Faculty Tone:** Simulate faculty comments that range from supportive to curt or sarcastic. Ensure the system does not blindly side with the faculty member's tone over the evidence.
*   **Blind Evaluation Protocol:** Configure the system to redact or ignore demographic markers (names, major, year) before analysis begins. If your synthetic data includes these, verify they are stripped prior to the model's inference step.
*   **Counterfactual Testing:** For every synthetic appeal, create a mirrored version where the student's gender or background context is swapped (if applicable in the text). If the suggested outcome changes, flag the model for retraining or restriction.

### 2. Transparency and Explainability
Students and the review board must understand *why* a suggestion was made. A "black box" recommendation will undermine trust and likely fail academic governance standards.

*   **Evidence Mapping:** The system must not just output a score adjustment; it must cite the specific sentences from the student's appeal and the faculty's response that drove the decision.
    *   *Requirement:* Every suggestion must include a "Reasoning Block" linking the outcome to specific input text segments.
*   **Confidence Scoring:** The system should output a confidence level (e.g., Low, Medium, High) based on the clarity of the argument and the strength of the evidence.
    *   *Rule:* Any suggestion with "Low" confidence automatically triggers a full human review, regardless of the recommended outcome.
*   **Plain Language Summaries:** Avoid technical jargon in the output presented to students. The explanation should read: "The system identified a discrepancy between the rubric criteria and the graded section," rather than referencing internal model weights or probabilities.
*   **Audit Logging:** Every interaction must be logged immutably. This includes the raw input, the system's internal reasoning trace, the final suggestion, and the ultimate human decision. This is crucial for future retrospective analysis once real data becomes available.

### 3. Mandatory Human-in-the-Loop (HITL) Protocols
Automation should assist, not decide, especially when academic standing is at risk. Given the lack of real-world validation, your human oversight must be absolute for critical cases.

*   **Hard Stops for High-Stakes Cases:** Implement a logical gate that prevents the system from finalizing any suggestion involving:
    *   A grade that results in a failure (F, 0.0, or below passing threshold).
    *   Any appeal mentioning disciplinary action, academic dishonesty, or misconduct.
    *   Cases where the suggested adjustment changes the final course grade by more than one letter grade.
    *   *Protocol:* In these instances, the system acts only as a drafting tool for the board; it cannot generate a "suggested outcome" visible to the student until a human has explicitly approved the reasoning.
*   **The "Second Pair of Eyes" Rule:** For the first semester of deployment, require two human reviewers for any case where the system's suggestion contradicts the original faculty grade. One reviewer should be blind to the system's suggestion to prevent automation bias.
*   **Override Justification:** If a human reviewer rejects the system's suggestion, require a mandatory short-text field explaining why. This data will be your gold mine for building better synthetic tests and eventually training on real patterns.
*   **Student Recourse:** Clearly communicate to students that the output is a *recommendation*. Provide a distinct, easy-to-access mechanism for students to request a purely human review if they feel the automated analysis misunderstood their context.

### Final Deployment Gate
Before going live, conduct a "Tabletop Exercise" with your team and a few faculty members. Walk through five synthetic scenarios (one clear error, one ambiguous case, one high-stakes failure, one bias trap, and one standard appeal). If the team cannot unanimously agree on the system's behavior and the HITL intervention points for these five cases, do not launch. Your lack of historical data means your process must be your primary safety mechanism.
