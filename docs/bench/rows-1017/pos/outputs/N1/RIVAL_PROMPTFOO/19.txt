Given the high stakes of a health-tech launch and the specific constraint regarding older patients with osteoporosis, your pre-launch safety evaluation must prioritize rigorous retrospective validation over broad clinical trials. Since you cannot extend the timeline, you must maximize the signal from existing data and simulate edge cases where the model is most likely to fail.

### Pre-Launch Safety Evaluation Plan

Your primary goal is to quantify performance gaps specifically for the underrepresented demographic before any live deployment. Do not rely on aggregate accuracy metrics, which can hide catastrophic failures in minority subgroups.

**1. Stratified Retrospective Analysis**
You must immediately segment your existing validation dataset by age, bone density status (if available), and fracture type. Since you lack diverse data for older patients, you need to artificially stress-test this gap:
*   **Synthetic Data Augmentation:** Collaborate with a medical imaging specialist to apply realistic transformations to existing X-rays that mimic osteoporotic characteristics (e.g., reduced bone density, increased cortical porosity). Use these synthetic cases to create a dedicated "high-risk" test set.
*   **Subgroup Metric Isolation:** Calculate sensitivity (recall) and specificity separately for the "older/osteoporotic" cohort versus the general population. A drop in sensitivity here indicates a risk of missed diagnoses, which is the critical failure mode for a triage tool.
*   **Threshold Tuning:** Analyze the confidence scores your model assigns to these difficult cases. You may need to lower the threshold for flagging potential fractures in older patients to prioritize sensitivity over specificity, accepting more false positives to ensure fewer missed breaks.

**2. Adversarial and Edge Case Testing**
Simulate the conditions where the model will struggle most. Create a "challenge set" comprising:
*   Images with low contrast or poor positioning, common in emergency settings with elderly patients who may have difficulty standing still.
*   Cases with comorbidities that present visual noise similar to fractures (e.g., old healed fractures, calcifications).
*   Run the model against this set to identify specific visual patterns that trigger false negatives. Document these failure modes explicitly for the radiographers.

**3. Human-in-the-Loop Simulation**
Before going live, conduct a "shadow mode" study using historical cases where the ground truth is known but hidden from the reviewers.
*   Have your radiographers review cases both with and without the AI's suggestions.
*   Measure if the AI's presence improves detection in standard cases but inadvertently causes "automation bias," leading experts to miss fractures in the difficult osteoporotic cases because the AI failed to flag them.
*   If the tool reduces human performance in the high-risk group, you must reconsider the launch or implement stricter guardrails.

### Post-Launch Monitoring Protocols

Once deployed, your monitoring must shift from static validation to dynamic safety surveillance. The goal is to detect drift or systematic errors in real-time before they impact patient outcomes.

**1. Discrepancy Tracking System**
Implement a mandatory feedback loop within the radiographer's workflow:
*   **Override Logging:** Every time a radiographer disagrees with the AI (either ignoring a flag or finding a fracture the AI missed), log the case ID, the AI's confidence score, and the final diagnosis.
*   **Delayed Ground Truth:** Link the tool to final radiology reports or surgical confirmations. If a patient flagged as "negative" by the AI is later diagnosed with a fracture, trigger an immediate alert to the engineering team.

**2. Demographic Performance Dashboards**
Build real-time dashboards that break down error rates by demographic slices (age, gender, facility).
*   Set automated thresholds for alerts. For example, if the false negative rate for patients over 75 exceeds a specific percentage (e.g., 5%) over a rolling 7-day window, the system should automatically notify the safety officer.
*   Monitor the ratio of false positives to true positives. A spike in false alarms can lead to alert fatigue, causing staff to ignore legitimate warnings.

**3. Scheduled Safety Audits**
Given the two-month constraint, plan for a formal safety review at 30, 60, and 90 days post-launch.
*   Re-evaluate the model's performance on the "older/osteoporotic" cohort using real-world data accumulated during the first month.
*   If the real-world data confirms the training gap results in unacceptable miss rates, have a pre-approved contingency plan ready. This could involve temporarily increasing the sensitivity threshold for older patients or reverting to a human-only workflow for that demographic until the model can be retrained.

By focusing your limited time on targeted stress testing of the known weakness and establishing robust, automated monitoring for that specific demographic, you can mitigate the most severe risks while adhering to your launch timeline.
