Launching an unsupervised math chatbot for children aged eight to twelve carries significant responsibility. Without adult oversight, the bot becomes the sole authority on logic and encouragement, meaning any drift in reasoning or tone can directly impact a child's confidence and understanding. Given your one-month timeline and budget constraints, you cannot rely on external experts alone; you must build rigorous internal guardrails immediately.

The primary risks fall into three categories: pedagogical accuracy, age-appropriate interaction, and logical consistency.

**1. Pedagogical Accuracy and Misconceptions**
The greatest danger is not just giving a wrong answer, but explaining *why* a wrong answer is right, or using a method that conflicts with school curricula.
*   **Methodology Drift:** Schools teach specific algorithms (e.g., "partial quotients" for division or "number bonds" for addition). If your bot uses a different adult-centric method, it may confuse the student and cause friction with teachers.
*   **Reinforcing Errors:** If a child makes a common misconception error (e.g., thinking $0.5 > 0.25$ because $5 > 25$), the bot must identify the specific logical flaw. Generic corrections like "Try again" do not help; the bot must address the root misunderstanding.
*   **Hallucinated Rules:** LLMs can invent math rules that sound plausible but are false. This is catastrophic for learning.

**2. Age-Appropriateness and Tone**
Children aged 8–12 have varying reading levels and emotional resilience.
*   **Cognitive Load:** Explanations must be broken into small, digestible steps. Large blocks of text or complex vocabulary will cause disengagement.
*   **Encouragement vs. Pressure:** The bot must distinguish between a "growth mindset" encouragement ("Let's look at where the slip happened") and toxic positivity ("You're so smart, just try harder!"), which can make struggling students feel inadequate.
*   **Ambiguity Handling:** Children often ask vague questions. The bot must ask clarifying questions rather than guessing, which could lead to irrelevant explanations.

**3. Logical Consistency**
In multi-turn dialogues, the bot must remember previous steps. If it contradicts itself halfway through a solution, it destroys trust and teaches that math is arbitrary.

### A Framework for Pre-Launch Testing

Since you cannot hire psychologists, your team of six must act as the proxy for students, teachers, and curriculum experts. Adopt a "Red Team" approach focused on education rather than security.

**Phase 1: Curriculum Mapping (Days 1–3)**
Select the top three math standards for grades 3–6 in your target region (e.g., Common Core or national equivalents).
*   List the specific methods required for fractions, long division, and decimals.
*   Create a "Golden Set" of 20 problems that represent these standards, including the *exact* steps and terminology expected in classrooms.
*   Define "Forbidden Outputs": List phrases or methods the bot must never use (e.g., advanced algebraic notation for an 8-year-old).

**Phase 2: The "Six-Hat" Internal Review (Days 4–10)**
Assign each team member a specific persona to test the bot against the Golden Set. Do not let them test as engineers; they must role-play strictly.
*   **The Struggling Student:** Intentionally makes common mistakes to see if the bot identifies the specific misconception.
*   **The Confused Student:** Asks vague or off-topic questions to test clarity and boundary setting.
*   **The Curriculum Police:** Checks if the bot's step-by-step method matches the school-required algorithm exactly.
*   **The Skeptic:** Tries to trick the bot into hallucinating rules or contradicting itself.
*   **The Emotional Observer:** Rates every response for tone, ensuring it is supportive but not patronizing.
*   **The Parent:** Reviews the transcript to see if they would feel comfortable leaving their child alone with this conversation.

**Phase 3: Automated Consistency Checks (Days 11–20)**
Use your existing engineering skills to create deterministic tests.
*   **Step Verification:** Break down solutions into atomic steps. Write scripts that verify the mathematical validity of each intermediate step, not just the final answer.
*   **Vocabulary Scanner:** Run all outputs through a filter that flags words above a 6th-grade reading level or jargon not defined in the prompt.
*   **Contradiction Detection:** Feed the bot's last three responses back into a separate checker instance to flag logical inconsistencies.

**Phase 4: Limited "Friends and Family" Pilot (Days 21–30)**
Before public release, run a closed beta with 10–15 families.
*   Provide parents with a simple feedback form: "Did your child understand the explanation?" and "Did the bot ever seem confused?"
*   Record sessions (with consent) to review where children got stuck or frustrated.
*   Treat any instance of confusion as a critical bug requiring an immediate prompt update.

**Critical Safety Guardrails**
*   **Hard Stop on Non-Math:** Configure the system to politely refuse topics outside mathematics and homework support.
*   **Uncertainty Protocol:** If the bot's confidence in a calculation drops below a high threshold, it must admit uncertainty and suggest reviewing the step, rather than guessing.
*   **No "Do It For Me" Mode:** Ensure the bot is tuned to guide, not just provide answers, to prevent academic dishonesty and ensure learning occurs.

Your constraint of time and budget means you must prioritize depth over breadth. Perfect the bot's handling of fractions and basic algebra before expanding to other topics. It is safer to launch with a narrow, flawless scope than a broad, error-prone one.
