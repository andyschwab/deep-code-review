Based on your constraints and the sensitive nature of automated decision-making for financial aid, here is a focused review of your validation approach and a strategy for applicant communication.

### Reviewing Validation Logs Without Extensive Simulation

Since you cannot run new simulations or extensive stress tests before the deadline, your existing validation logs are your primary evidence source. You must treat these logs not just as error reports, but as a forensic record of how the model behaves across different demographics.

Focus your manual review on three specific patterns within the logs:

*   **Score Distribution by Proxy Variables:** Even without explicit demographic data, look for correlations between low scores and specific legacy data formats, zip codes, or high school types. If students from specific legacy data sources consistently receive lower scores, this indicates a potential bias introduced by data formatting rather than student merit.
*   **Confidence Intervals vs. Binary Decisions:** Identify cases where the model's confidence score was low (e.g., between 40% and 60%) but the system still rendered a hard "reject" decision. These are your highest-risk false negatives.
*   **Error Patterns in Essay Parsing:** Check logs for instances where the system failed to parse essays due to non-standard formatting, dialect variations, or file types common in under-resourced schools. A "parse error" leading to an automatic rejection is a critical fairness failure.

Do not attempt to statistically prove the model is perfect. Instead, use the logs to identify where the model is *uncertain*. This uncertainty defines the scope of your manual review layer.

### Communication Strategy for Non-Selected Applicants

Transparency is legally and ethically required, but explaining a complex scoring algorithm to a disappointed high school senior requires clarity, not technical jargon. Your communication must balance honesty about the automation with empathy for the applicant.

**Core Principles for Notification:**

*   **Avoid "Black Box" Language:** Do not say "the algorithm decided." Instead, explain that the system evaluated specific, stated criteria (e.g., GPA thresholds, essay keyword relevance, extracurricular consistency).
*   **Acknowledge Limitations:** Explicitly state that the automated tool is a preliminary sorter, not a final judgment on their potential or worth.
*   **Provide an Immediate Path Forward:** Every rejection letter must include clear instructions on how to request a human review.

**Drafting the Message Structure:**

1.  **The Decision:** Clearly state they were not selected in this initial automated round.
2.  **The "Why" (Generalized):** Explain that the system prioritizes applications that most closely match the specific weighting of academic metrics and essay themes defined for this year's grant. Avoid giving a specific score breakdown that might encourage gaming the system.
3.  **The Human Safety Net:** Inform them that a team of human reviewers is currently assessing borderline cases and that they have the right to request their application be included in this manual pool.
4.  **Timeline:** Give a specific date by which they will hear back if they opt for a manual review.

### Implementing the Manual Review Layer

Given your ten-day window, you cannot manually review every application. You must triage based on the insights from your log review.

*   **Target the "Borderline":** Configure your manual review queue to automatically include any application where the automated score fell within the low-confidence range identified in your logs.
*   **Prioritize Data Anomalies:** Flag any application where the legacy data format required conversion or where the essay parsing log showed warnings. These are the most likely candidates for false negatives.
*   **Reviewer Guidelines:** Equip your eight team members with a simple rubric that overrides the automated score. Instruct them to look specifically for context that the AI misses, such as non-traditional extracurriculars, overcoming adversity mentioned in essays, or grade improvements that a static GPA snapshot ignores.

### Final Recommendations

With only ten days remaining, do not try to fix the legacy data formats or retrain the model. Focus entirely on the **human-in-the-loop** process. Your goal is not to make the automated scorer perfect, but to ensure its errors do not result in permanent denial of aid.

By openly communicating that the automated decision is provisional and by actively funneling uncertain cases to human reviewers, you protect the foundation from liability and, more importantly, ensure that deserving students from diverse backgrounds are not filtered out by technical limitations. The narrative to applicants should be: "The computer sorted quickly, but humans decide fairly."
