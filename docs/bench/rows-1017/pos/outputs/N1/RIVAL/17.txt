Launching an unsupervised math chatbot for children aged eight to twelve carries significant responsibility. Without teacher oversight or access to child psychologists, your safety plan must rely on rigorous internal controls, clear boundaries, and community-based testing. The primary risks are not just technical errors, but the potential to erode a child's confidence or cement mathematical misconceptions that are difficult to unlearn later.

**Key Risks to Address**

*   **Pedagogical Drift:** The bot might provide the correct final answer but use a non-standard or confusing method that conflicts with what students are learning in school, causing frustration.
*   **Confabulation (Hallucination):** Generative models can invent mathematical rules or facts with high confidence. For a child, a confident lie is more damaging than a silent error.
*   **Emotional Impact:** An unsupervised bot might fail to recognize when a student is frustrated, potentially using encouragement that feels patronizing or ignoring signs of distress.
*   **Age-Inappropriate Content:** The bot could inadvertently introduce complex vocabulary, abstract concepts, or even unsafe topics if the conversation drifts away from math.
*   **Over-Reliance:** Students might use the tool to get answers without understanding the underlying logic, defeating the educational purpose.

**Pre-Launch Safety Plan**

Since you cannot hire external experts, you must institutionalize safety within your development cycle using the following framework:

1.  **Define Strict Guardrails**
    *   Limit the bot's scope strictly to the specific math curriculum for grades 3–6. Explicitly instruct the system to refuse questions outside this domain.
    *   Implement a "Socratic Method" constraint: The bot should guide students with questions rather than providing full step-by-step solutions immediately, encouraging active thinking.
    *   Hard-code safety responses for emotional cues. If a user expresses frustration (e.g., "I'm stupid," "This is too hard"), the bot must switch to a predefined, empathetic script and suggest taking a break, rather than trying to "solve" the emotion with generated text.

2.  **Curriculum Alignment Framework**
    *   Create a "Golden Dataset" of 100–200 common math problems sourced directly from public school standards (such as Common Core or your local equivalent).
    *   For each problem, document the *expected* teaching method and key vocabulary.
    *   Run your bot against this dataset before every update. Do not just check if the answer is right; manually review the *explanation* to ensure it matches the approved teaching method.
    *   Flag any deviation where the bot uses advanced shortcuts or terminology not yet introduced to that age group.

3.  **Community-Based "Red Teaming"**
    *   Since you lack psychologists, recruit a diverse group of parents and teachers from your network to act as beta testers.
    *   Provide them with specific scenarios to try: "Try to make the bot confused," "Ask a question slightly above your grade level," or "Pretend you are very angry about a wrong answer."
    *   Ask teachers specifically to evaluate if the explanations match classroom instruction. Their feedback is a proxy for professional pedagogical review.

4.  **Transparency and Parental Controls**
    *   Clearly state on the interface that this is an AI assistant, not a human tutor.
    *   Implement a feature where a summary of the session (topics covered, errors made) can be emailed to parents. This creates a safety loop, allowing adults to spot if the bot gave strange advice after the fact.
    *   Include a prominent "Report a Problem" button in the chat interface so users can instantly flag confusing or weird responses.

5.  **Incident Response Protocol**
    *   Establish a rapid response process for when a bad output is reported. If a user flags a hallucination or confusing explanation, that specific interaction pattern must be added to your "blocklist" or fine-tuning dataset immediately.
    *   Prepare a communication template to apologize and correct the record if a widespread error is discovered post-launch.

Given your one-month timeline, prioritize the "Golden Dataset" creation and the recruitment of teacher-beta testers immediately. These two steps offer the highest return on investment for ensuring accuracy and age-appropriateness without a large budget. Remember, for this age group, admitting "I don't know" is safer than guessing wrong. Configure your system to default to humility when confidence scores are low.
