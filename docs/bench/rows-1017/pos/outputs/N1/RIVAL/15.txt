This system presents extreme risk because it removes human verification from life-safety decisions while relying on imprecise location data. Before launch, you must treat the "border zone" uncertainty not as a minor technical glitch, but as a primary failure mode that could cost lives. Your evaluation plan must focus on worst-case scenarios where the algorithm's confidence exceeds its actual accuracy.

You should simulate the following specific high-stress scenarios to test your messaging logic:

**1. The "Straddling" Boundary Event**
Simulate a fire front moving directly across a neighborhood boundary where your legacy mapping data is known to be fuzzy (e.g., ±200 meters).
*   **Test:** Does the system send "Evacuate" to one side of the street and "Shelter" to the other?
*   **Safety Goal:** The logic must default to the most protective action (Evacuate) when location confidence drops below a specific threshold. If the system cannot distinguish between Zone A and Zone B, both must receive the evacuation order. Ambiguity must always resolve toward safety, not precision.

**2. The Rapid Escalation Lag**
Simulate a scenario where fire spread models update every 15 minutes, but wind conditions change the fire trajectory in 2 minutes.
*   **Test:** Measure the time delta between the model update and the text dispatch. More critically, test what happens to residents in the path during that gap.
*   **Safety Goal:** Ensure the system does not send a "Shelter" message that becomes obsolete before the resident reads it. You need a logic gate that suppresses "Shelter" instructions if the fire proximity is within a critical time-to-impact window, regardless of the current model snapshot.

**3. The Conflicting Instruction Loop**
Simulate a resident moving through a border zone while the fire shifts, triggering multiple model updates.
*   **Test:** Does the resident receive an "Evacuate" text, followed immediately by a "Shelter" text, then another "Evacuate"?
*   **Safety Goal:** Evaluate the system's ability to suppress contradictory follow-ups. Once an "Evacuate" order is sent to a specific geographic cluster, the system should lock that status to "Evacuate" for a defined duration unless a specific "All Clear" protocol is triggered. Conflicting messages cause paralysis; the system must prioritize message consistency over real-time fluctuation.

**4. The System Overload During Peak Demand**
Simulate the dry season's worst day: multiple fires igniting simultaneously across the county.
*   **Test:** Does the message queue delay urgent alerts for low-priority updates? Does the system fail to segment neighborhoods correctly under load, sending blanket county-wide alerts that dilute the urgency for those truly in danger?
*   **Safety Goal:** Verify that high-severity evacuation triggers bypass standard queuing protocols. The system must degrade gracefully; if it cannot target precisely, it must expand the warning radius rather than delay the message.

**5. The "False Negative" Stress Test**
Intentionally feed the system slightly outdated or slightly inaccurate map data that suggests a neighborhood is safe when it is actually threatened.
*   **Test:** Does the system withhold an evacuation order based on this flawed input?
*   **Safety Goal:** This is your most critical test. You must establish a "human-in-the-loop" override or a mandatory "pre-send" review for any scenario where the model's confidence score is low. Given your legacy tool constraints, fully automated dispatch is likely unsafe.

**Immediate Recommendations for Your Plan:**

*   **Implement Confidence Thresholds:** Do not allow the system to send "Shelter" instructions if location confidence is below 95%. Default to "Evacuate" or "Prepare to Evacuate" in uncertain zones.
*   **Mandate Human Verification for Border Zones:** Even with a two-month deadline, you cannot automate decisions for areas where your data is known to be weak. Route all border-zone decisions to a human operator for final approval before sending.
*   **Create a "Message Recall" or "Correction" Protocol:** If the system sends a conflicting message, have an automated follow-up ready that explicitly states: "IGNORE PREVIOUS MESSAGE. NEW ORDER: [Action]."
*   **Conduct Tabletop Exercises:** Walk through these scenarios with your seven-person team using printed maps and manual timers. Do not rely solely on digital simulations; verify that your team can manually override the system if it behaves erratically.

Your legacy mapping tools are a single point of failure. Until they are upgraded, your automation logic must be conservative, biased heavily toward over-warning, and strictly limited in areas of known data uncertainty.
