Your plan to launch an unsupervised math chatbot for children aged 8–12 without a human-in-the-loop gate or specialized child safety expertise presents a critical failure point. This feature falls under **unsafe automation of high-stakes actions**: providing educational guidance to minors without oversight is irreversible in its potential to instill lasting misconceptions or erode confidence. You cannot "test" your way out of this design flaw before launch; the risk requires a structural control, not just a review.

### Immediate Harm Inventory
Before writing a single test case, you must acknowledge these specific risks inherent to your current design:
*   **Hallucination surfaced as fact:** The bot may generate incorrect math steps with absolute authority. For a child, an AI error looks like a textbook truth, reinforcing misconceptions that are difficult to unlearn.
*   **Over-reliance and automation bias:** Without a teacher or parent in the loop, children may accept incorrect logic simply because it comes from a computer, bypassing their own critical thinking.
*   **Missing AI disclosure:** Children in this age group often struggle to distinguish between human and machine intent. If the bot mimics a tutor too closely without clear boundaries, it creates a deceptive pattern of trust.
*   **Age-inappropriate interaction:** Without child psychologists, you risk the bot using language, examples, or encouragement styles that are confusing, patronizing, or inadvertently distressing to an 8-year-old versus a 12-year-old.
*   **Unsafe unsupervised automation:** The core failure is the lack of a human gate. Allowing an AI to act as the sole educator for minors without a mechanism for human review of edge cases or failures is the highest-risk element of your product.

### Measurement Plan (Do Not Fabricate Metrics)
You cannot claim your bot is "accurate" or "safe" without evidence. Since you cannot hire experts immediately, you must adopt a **zero-tolerance stance on unverified claims**.
*   **Adversarial Testing:** Create a "red team" of parents and teachers (even volunteers) to probe the bot with common student misconceptions and tricky curriculum edge cases.
*   **Control Groups:** For every math concept, define a "known-good" set of standard solutions and a "known-bad" set of common errors. The bot must never validate the "known-bad" inputs.
*   **Subgroup Analysis:** Test responses separately for the lower end (age 8) and upper end (age 12) of your range. A response suitable for a 12-year-old may be incomprehensible to an 8-year-old.
*   **Stop Conditions:** Define a clear threshold for launch blocking. For example: "If the bot confidently validates a known misconception in >0% of test cases, launch is halted." Do not invent a "99% accuracy" metric; if you haven't tested a specific concept, mark it as **UNVERIFIED**.

### Managing Residual Risk: The Human Gate
Your current design of "entirely without teacher supervision" is the primary hazard. You must implement a **human-in-the-loop gate** for high-stakes interactions:
*   **Uncertainty Tiers:** Never display raw confidence percentages (e.g., "87% sure"). Instead, use defined coarse tiers like "Confirmed Step," "Double-Check This," or "Ask a Teacher." If the bot's internal confidence drops below a strict threshold, it must default to asking the student to consult a human rather than guessing.
*   **Explicit Disclosure:** The interface must clearly state, "I am an AI helper, not a teacher. Always check your work with a grown-up." This is a product safety control, regardless of legal requirements.
*   **Reversibility:** Ensure students can easily flag confusing answers. These flags must route to a human reviewer (even a founder) before the bot continues similar dialogues.

### Required Artifacts
You must produce a **Model Card** before launch. This document should list:
*   **Intended Use:** "Supplementary math practice for ages 8–12," explicitly stating it is *not* a replacement for teachers.
*   **Limitations:** Clearly list topics the bot is not trained on or where it frequently struggles.
*   **Data Provenance:** Where did the math logic come from? Is it aligned with specific curriculum standards?
*   **Owner:** Name the specific team member responsible for reviewing flagged errors.

### Critical Next Steps
1.  **Pause the "unsupervised" launch plan.** The risk of harming a child's education is too high to automate fully at this stage.
2.  **Route legal and regulatory questions to counsel.** Regulations regarding automated decisions affecting minors (such as the EU AI Act or COPPA) are strict. Do not assume you are exempt; have a lawyer confirm your obligations.
3.  **Adopt a "Human-in-the-Loop" MVP.** Instead of full automation, consider a model where the bot drafts explanations but requires a quick human approval for new topics, or limits the session length to ensure human oversight remains part of the workflow.

Do not ship a feature that claims to teach children while lacking the safeguards to prevent it from teaching them wrong. The cost of a delayed launch is far lower than the cost of eroding trust with your youngest users.
