Before launching the automated grading appeal responder, you must treat the system's suggestions as high-stakes outputs that can directly alter a student's academic record and future. Because you lack historical data and rely on synthetic examples, your risk of undetected bias and hallucination is elevated. Do not certify the system as "safe" or "unbiased"; instead, document the residual risks and implement strict controls.

### 1. Harm Inventory
You must explicitly name and record the worst-case scenarios for your specific context. An empty inventory is a failure.
*   **Bias and Unfair Outcomes:** The model may systematically disadvantage non-native English speakers, students using specific dialects, or those whose argumentation styles differ from the synthetic training data.
*   **Hallucination as Fact:** The system might invent faculty comments, misquote university policy, or fabricate precedents to support a suggested grade change, presenting these fabrications with false authority.
*   **Over-reliance:** The review board might accept the AI's suggestion without independent verification because the interface presents the output as a definitive recommendation rather than a probabilistic draft.
*   **Missing Disclosure:** Students may believe a human has already reviewed their case if the automated nature of the initial analysis is not clearly stated.
*   **Unsafe Automation:** Automatically applying a grade change or initiating disciplinary action without human confirmation is a critical failure mode.

### 2. Measurement and Evaluation Plan
Since you cannot claim accuracy without real-world data, you must design a runnable evaluation plan to execute before full deployment.
*   **Adversarial Testing:** Create a "red team" probe set using edge cases: appeals written in broken English, appeals citing non-existent policies, and appeals with emotional but logically flawed arguments.
*   **Control Groups:** For every test, define a "known-bad" input (e.g., a nonsensical appeal that should be rejected) and a "known-good" input (a clear, policy-backed appeal that should be upheld).
*   **Subgroup Analysis:** Even with synthetic data, vary the demographic markers in your test prompts to check for disparate impact. If the model consistently downgrades appeals from specific demographic profiles, flag this as **UNVERIFIED** risk until proven otherwise.
*   **Stop Conditions:** Define a failure threshold. For example, if the system hallucinates a policy citation in more than 0% of high-stakes cases, or if it fails to flag a clearly invalid appeal in the "known-bad" set, the launch must be blocked.
*   **Transparency Artifacts:** Draft a **Model Card** for internal and board use. It must state:
    *   **Intended Use:** Drafting suggestions for grade appeals only.
    *   **Limitations:** Trained on limited synthetic data; high risk of hallucination regarding specific course policies.
    *   **Performance:** Explicitly mark bias and accuracy metrics as **UNVERIFIED** due to data gaps, rather than inventing a percentage.

### 3. Human-in-the-Loop (HITL) Protocols
Given the high stakes of failing grades and disciplinary actions, full automation is prohibited.
*   **Mandatory Gate:** No suggestion involving a failing grade (F, D-, etc.) or any disciplinary referral may be finalized or communicated to a student without explicit human approval from the academic review board.
*   **Uncertainty Display:** Do not show the board a raw confidence score (e.g., "87% confident"). Instead, use defined coarse tiers such as **"Corroborated"** (matches policy text exactly), **"Single-Source"** (based on one interpretation), or **"Unverified"** (requires manual policy check).
*   **Override Mechanism:** The interface must make it easier for a human to reject or edit the AI's suggestion than to accept it blindly. The default state of any generated suggestion should be "Pending Review."

### 4. Disclosure and Governance
*   **User Notification:** Clearly disclose to students that their appeal is initially analyzed by an AI tool to assist the board, ensuring they understand a human makes the final decision.
*   **Legal Routing:** You asked about mandatory disclosures and regulated decisions. Whether this system triggers specific legal requirements under education privacy laws or emerging AI regulations (like the EU AI Act if applicable to your jurisdiction) is a legal determination. Route this question immediately to university counsel; do not assume compliance based on this checklist.
*   **Ownership:** Assign a specific team member as the owner of this feature. They are responsible for re-evaluating the harm inventory if the model, the data, or the university grading policies change.

Do not launch until the HITL gate is technically enforced for all high-stakes outcomes and the Model Card honestly reflects the limitations of your synthetic data.
