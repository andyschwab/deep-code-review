Do not publicly announce the vulnerability or your fix in the issue tracker. Disclosing specific details about a race condition in an abandoned project without a patch available to the general public violates responsible disclosure principles and unnecessarily exposes your firm and other users to immediate exploitation.

Instead, execute the following containment and mitigation strategy:

1.  **Isolate and Stabilize Internally**: Immediately apply your fix to a private fork of the crate within your organization's repository. Update your application's dependency configuration (e.g., `Cargo.toml`) to point to this private fork using a Git URL or a local path. This secures your production environment without relying on the upstream maintainer.

2.  **Conduct Independent Verification**: Since you cannot rely on the original maintainer's review, treat your internal fix as a critical security patch. Perform rigorous stress testing specifically targeting the >500 thread scenario to confirm the race condition is resolved. Document these results thoroughly for your internal audit logs.

3.  **Silent Forking**: Proceed with maintaining the project internally as a silent fork. Do not publish this fork to public registries (like crates.io) yet, as this could confuse the ecosystem or imply an official endorsement that doesn't exist. Use this private version strictly for your firm's needs.

4.  **Responsible Disclosure Attempt (Optional)**: If you feel an ethical obligation to attempt one final contact before considering the project definitively dead, send a single, non-technical message to the maintainer stating only: "We have identified a critical stability issue affecting production systems. We have a working mitigation internally but prefer to merge upstream. If we do not hear back within 14 days, we will assume the project is abandoned and proceed with a community fork." Do not include technical details, code, or proof-of-concept exploits in this message.

5.  **Community Fork (Last Resort)**: If the maintainer remains unresponsive after your final attempt, you may eventually publish your fork under a new name (e.g., `ironclad-network-crate`) to prevent community fragmentation. When you do this, clearly state in the README that this is a community-maintained fork of the original abandoned project, citing the lack of response as the reason for the fork. Only release this once your internal testing is complete and the fix is proven stable.

This approach prioritizes the security of your production system and the broader user base by preventing vulnerability exposure while respecting the original author's silence through non-intrusive final contact attempts.

Note: Public pressure tactics often backfire in open source, leading to project burnout or hostile responses; silent forking is the standard, accepted practice for abandoned critical dependencies.
