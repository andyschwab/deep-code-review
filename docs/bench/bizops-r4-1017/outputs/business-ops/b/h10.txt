### Lane A: Revenue Forecasting Model (Arithmetic Application)

Because your volume is driven by external court schedules rather than steady demand, a standard linear forecast will fail. You should apply a **probabilistic scenario model** using your own historical data slots.

**The Approach:**
Instead of predicting one number, calculate three distinct monthly revenue figures based on your observed fluctuation ($90k–$140k) and case count (40 cases/mo avg).

1.  **Define Your Inputs (Slots to Fill):**
    *   **Base Case:** Use your 12-month average case count (e.g., 40) × Average Case Value ($3,000).
    *   **Downside Case:** Use your lowest observed monthly volume (e.g., 30 cases, derived from the $90k low) × $3,000.
    *   **Upside Case:** Use your highest observed monthly volume (e.g., 47 cases, derived from the $140k high) × $3,000.
    *   **Probability Weighting:** Assign a likelihood to each based on past frequency (e.g., 20% Downside, 50% Base, 30% Upside).

2.  **The Formula:**
    `Expected Monthly Revenue = (Downside Rev × %Prob) + (Base Rev × %Prob) + (Upside Rev × %Prob)`

3.  **Cash Flow Projection:**
    `Net Cash Flow = Expected Monthly Revenue − Fixed Overhead ($25,000) − Variable Translator Costs`
    *Note: Since translators are paid per project, treat their cost as a variable margin deduction, not fixed overhead.*

**Decision Aid:**
*   **Read:** If your "Downside" scenario results in negative cash flow, your fixed overhead of $25k is too high for your volatility profile.
*   **Flip Input:** The variable that most changes your outcome is **case volume variance**. Reducing the gap between your high and low months (e.g., by diversifying into non-court corporate legal work) stabilizes the model more than raising prices.
*   **Threshold Rule of Thumb:** If your cash reserve cannot cover 3 months of the "Downside" net burn, prioritize building liquidity over expansion.
*   **Next Step:** This week, map your last 24 months of case counts against the local court's published holiday and fiscal-year-end calendars to identify recurring seasonal dips, then plug those specific low-month counts into your "Downside" slot.

### Lane R: Contractual Standards & Payment Terms (Regulation-Dependent)

**Regime:** Illinois Contract Law and Local Court Rules (Cook County/Circuit Court of Cook County).

**General Rule:** There is no universal Illinois statute mandating specific payment terms for private deposition translation vendors. Payment terms are typically governed by the **Master Services Agreement (MSA)** between your agency and the law firms or court reporting agencies you serve. However, local court rules sometimes dictate when a court reporter must be paid, which can flow down to you if you are subcontracted.

**Routing Required:**
Determining whether you can legally enforce shorter payment terms (e.g., Net 15 vs. Net 45) or leverage specific Illinois prompt-payment acts requires a review of your existing contracts and the specific nature of your clients (private firms vs. court-appointed).

*   **Action:** Consult an Illinois-licensed attorney specializing in commercial contracts or legal vendor services.
*   **Question to Bring:** "Given our MSA templates and the specific court reporting agencies we subcontract for in Cook County, what contractual clauses can we insert to align our translator payout schedule with our client receipt schedule, and are there any Illinois prompt-payment statutes applicable to our specific vendor classification?"

**Note:** Do not change payment terms based on general industry "standards" without legal review, as this could breach existing MSAs.
Your variable cost structure (paying translators per project) is your strongest hedge; ensure your client terms are not significantly slower than your vendor terms.
