*   **Break-Even Volume Analysis:**

    *   `(New Price - New COGS) × New Volume = Old Profit Total`

        *   **Decision Rule:** If your original margin was thin, a 15% volume drop might be catastrophic. If your original margin was healthy, the extra $0.20/unit acts as a buffer.

### Strategic Decision Aid

You lack the specific input for **Price Elasticity of Demand** (how much volume drops per dollar of price increase). Without this data, any prediction of "will we lose 15%?" is a guess.

**Recommended Next Steps (Apply, Don't Direct):**

1.  **Test Elasticity Before Committing:** Do not roll out a global price hike or recipe change immediately.
    *   *Action:* Run a Van Westendorp price sensitivity survey with your existing customer list. Ask: "At what price would this jar be too expensive?" and "At what price would you question the quality?"
    *   *Alternative:* A/B test the $14.50 price on a small segment (e.g., one retailer or online channel) for 30 days. Measure the actual volume drop.

2.  **Calculate Your "Walk-Away" Volume:**
    *   Determine the exact volume at $14.50 where your total profit equals your current total profit.
    *   Formula: `Required Volume = (Current Total Profit) / ($14.50 - (Old COGS + $1.80))`.
    *   If the required volume is lower than your conservative estimate of post-hike sales, the price increase is mathematically safer than reformulation.

3.  **Brand Risk Assessment:**

    *   *Rule of Thumb:* In premium food segments, recipe degradation often causes higher churn than moderate price increases ($2.00 on a $12.50 item is a 16% increase, which is noticeable but often acceptable for imported goods).

**Immediate Mitigation Strategy:**
Present the price increase as a "Supply Chain Surcharge" temporarily while monitoring volume. This allows you to revert if the volume drop exceeds your calculated break-even point, whereas recipe changes are often permanent and damaging to brand equity.
