### Lane A: Arithmetic & Structural Analysis

**1. Current Liability Baseline (6-Month Window)**
*   **Volume:** 120 repairs/month × 6 months = 720 active repairs in any rolling window.
*   **Follow-up Frequency:** 3% of 720 = ~22 incidents per rolling 6-month period.
*   **Cost Assumption:** You noted "rare part replacements" cost up to 2,000 CHF, but did not specify if the 3% follow-ups are all catastrophic or mostly minor labor.
    *   *Scenario A (Conservative):* All 22 incidents require max parts (2,000 CHF). Liability = 44,000 CHF.
    *   *Scenario B (Likely):* Most are minor adjustments; only a fraction are catastrophic. If only 10% of follow-ups are catastrophic (2.2 incidents), liability = 4,400 CHF + minor labor costs.
*   **Current Reserve:** 40,000 CHF. This covers Scenario A fully, leaving 0 CHF liquid (violating your 25k requirement). It vastly over-covers Scenario B.

**2. Proposed One-Year Warranty Projection**
Extending from 6 to 12 months theoretically doubles the exposure window, but failure rates for luxury repairs often skew early (infant mortality) or very late (wear). Without data, we must assume a linear risk increase for safety.
*   **Projected Volume:** 120 repairs × 12 months = 1,440 active repairs.
*   **Projected Incidents (Linear):** 3% of 1,440 = ~43 incidents.
*   **Reserve Required per Job (to maintain 25k liquidity):**
    *   Total Capital: 40,000 CHF.
    *   Target Liquid: 25,000 CHF.
    *   Max Allowable Reserve: 15,000 CHF.
    *   **Max Reserve per Active Job:** 15,000 CHF ÷ 1,440 jobs = **~10.42 CHF per job**.
    *   **Max Reserve per New Sale:** 15,000 CHF ÷ 120 monthly jobs = **125 CHF per repair**.

**Analysis:** If you set aside 125 CHF per new repair, you hit your liquidity limit immediately. If the actual cost of failures exceeds this (e.g., two catastrophic 2,000 CHF failures in a month = 4,000 CHF), you breach your 25k liquidity floor unless you have incoming cash flow to cover the gap instantly.

**3. Tiered vs. Flat Fee Strategy**
Given the high variance in component costs (minor adjustment vs. 2,000 CHF part), a **Tiered Warranty Model** is mathematically superior to a Flat Fee for capital efficiency.

*   **Flat Fee Risk:** To cover a potential 2,000 CHF loss on every job without knowing the failure rate, you must price the warranty high enough to absorb the worst case, tying up excessive capital or pricing yourself out of the market.
*   **Tiered Approach (Applied Structure):**
    *   **Tier 1 (Labor & Minor Parts):** Included in the base price or a low fee (e.g., 50 CHF). Covers the 3% historical follow-up rate assuming non-catastrophic causes. This keeps reserves low.
    *   **Tier 2 (Catastrophic Component Failure):** Optional add-on or strictly limited coverage. Requires a higher premium (e.g., 150–200 CHF) specifically earmarked for the "rare" 2,000 CHF events.
    *   **Benefit:** You only collect the high reserve amount from customers who opt-in or for specific high-risk movements, rather than hoarding cash against every single job.

**Decision Aid:**
*   **Read:** Your current 40k reserve is insufficient for a 1-year full warranty if catastrophic failures exceed 7–8 incidents annually while maintaining 25k liquidity.
*   **Flip Input:** The break-even variable is the **frequency of catastrophic failure**. If >0.6% of all repairs result in a 2,000 CHF part replacement, the 125 CHF/month reserve cap fails.
*   **Threshold:** If you cannot isolate catastrophic risk via tiers, do not extend to 1 year until you instrument data to separate "adjustment" follow-ups from "part replacement" follow-ups.
