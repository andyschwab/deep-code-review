**The Call:** Do not raise prices to $14.50 immediately; the required volume retention (85%) is highly risky given unknown price sensitivity. Instead, implement a "Good-Better-Best" packaging strategy to introduce a reformulated, lower-cost option while keeping the premium line intact, protecting both margin and brand reputation.

**The Numbers:**
*Current Baseline:*
Revenue = $12.50 × 50,000 = $625,000.
Current COGS Increase = $1.80 × 50,000 = $90,000 total annual hit.

*Option A: Price Increase to $14.50*
New Margin per Unit = ($14.50 - [Old COGS + $1.80]).
To maintain current total gross profit dollars, you must solve for the minimum volume ($V$) where:
$V \times ($14.50 - COGS_{new}) = 50,000 \times ($12.50 - COGS_{old})$.
Without your exact current COGS, we use the break-even volume shift rule of thumb: A 16% price increase ($2.00/$12.50) generally requires retaining ~86-88% of volume to match profit if margins are thin, or slightly less if margins are thick.
**Assumption (UNVERIFIED):** If your current gross margin is 40% ($5.00/unit), raising price to $14.50 (new margin $5.20) requires selling 48,077 units (96% retention) to match profit. If margins are 20% ($2.50/unit), you need 45,454 units (91% retention).
**Risk:** You asked if losing >15% volume is likely. In specialty food, a 16% price hike often triggers >20% volume loss unless brand loyalty is extreme. If volume drops 15% (to 42,500 units), you likely lose total profit dollars compared to absorbing some cost.

*Option B: Reformulation*
Cost Savings = $1.80 avoided per unit.
**Assumption (UNVERIFIED):** Brand damage cost. If reformulation causes a 10% volume drop due to quality perception, the loss is 5,000 units × $12.50 = $62,500 in revenue, plus lost future LTV.
**Result:** Reformulation saves $90,000 in immediate tariffs but risks a recurring revenue cliff if quality perception shifts.

**What Flips It, and the Trigger:**
The flip input is **Price Elasticity of Demand**.
**Trigger:** If a blind taste test of the reformulated product scores within 5% of the original, choose reformulation for the base SKU. If the score drops >10%, do not reformulate the flagship product.
**Rule of Thumb:** Never change the recipe of a "best-seller" without explicit customer validation; brand trust in specialty foods is fragile.
**Step this week:** Run a Van Westendorp price sensitivity survey with your top 100 retailers/consumers asking at what price the $14.50 jar becomes "too expensive." Simultaneously, produce a small batch of the reformulated mix for blind testing against the original.
