**Decision Framework & Next Steps**

You are operating with a missing input: **Price Elasticity of Demand**. You cannot solve this equation without it.

1.  **Do Not Guess the Price:** Do not set the price at $14.50 yet. The "one input that flips the call" is the actual churn rate at that specific price point.
2.  **Run a Van Westendorp Survey:** This is a low-cost method to determine price sensitivity. Ask your existing customers four questions:
    *   At what price is the jar too expensive to consider?
    *   At what price is it a bargain?
    *   At what price is it getting expensive, but you'd still buy it?
    *   At what price is it so cheap you doubt the quality?
    *   *Action:* Email this to 500 loyal customers this week. The intersection of these curves will tell you if $14.50 is within the "acceptable" range or if it triggers mass abandonment.
3.  **A/B Test (If possible):** If you have direct-to-consumer channels, test the $14.50 price on 10% of traffic for two weeks. Measure the conversion rate drop. If conversion drops >15%, the market rejects the hike.

**Strategic Recommendation**
Absorb the $1.80 cost temporarily (reducing margin) while running the survey. If the survey indicates customers will accept up to $13.50 but not $14.50, raise the price to $13.50 and find $0.80 in other efficiencies (packaging, logistics) rather than ruining the recipe. Reformulation should be the last resort, as changing the core ingredient of a "specialty" product often violates the value proposition entirely.
