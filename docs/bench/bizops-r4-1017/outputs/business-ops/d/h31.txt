**The Arithmetic of the Constraint**
Your cash flow gap is driven by the delay between paying for labor/overhead and receiving revenue. Currently, with two-week deliveries, your cash conversion cycle is tight. With three-month (12-week) deliveries, you must pay staff and overhead for 12 weeks before a single new boat enters production and eventually generates revenue.

*   **Weekly Burn Rate Estimate:**
    *   **Payroll:** Assuming 12 skilled builders represent your primary cost. If fully burdened labor averages $1,200/week/builder (a conservative estimate for skilled Portland trades), weekly payroll is $14,400.
    *   **Overhead:** Rent, utilities, insurance, and admin. Without your specific number, we must model this. Let $O$ = Weekly Overhead.
    *   **Total Weekly Burn:** $14,400 + O$.

*   **The Gap Calculation:**
    *   You need to cover 12 weeks of waiting time.
    *   **Capital Needed:** $12 \times (14,400 + O)$.
    *   **Current Cash:** $150,000.
    *   **Shortfall:** $[12 \times (14,400 + O)] - 150,000$.

If your overhead is even modest (e.g., $10,000/week), your total weekly burn is $24,400. Over 12 weeks, you need $292,800. Your shortfall is ~$142,800. If overhead is higher, the hole deepens. This assumes you stop production entirely during the wait; if you continue building from existing stock while waiting for the next shipment, the timing shifts, but the total cash required to bridge the gap until *new* sales cash arrives remains dictated by the 12-week delay in replenishing the pipeline.

**Variables to Adjust in Projections**
1.  **Inventory Days:** Increase from ~28 days to ~90+ days. This directly reduces your Cash Conversion Cycle efficiency.
2.  **Cash Runway:** Recalculate runway as `Current Cash / Weekly Burn`. Under the new model, this will likely drop below 6 months without funding.
3.  **Production Start Date:** Push revenue recognition out by 12 weeks in your forecast.
4.  **Safety Stock Requirement:** You may need to order larger batches less frequently, spiking cash outflows periodically.

**Liquidity vs. Solvency Trigger**
This becomes a **solvency issue** (liabilities > assets) if the cost of borrowing (interest on the line of credit) plus the lost margin from reduced production volume exceeds your equity buffer.
*   **Liquidity Squeeze:** You have the assets (future sales) but not the cash timing. Fixable with a line of credit.
*   **Solvency Trigger:** If you cannot secure the credit, you miss payroll, lose key builders (who cannot be replaced easily), and fail to deliver on deposits. The business value collapses because the "skilled builder" asset walks out the door. The trigger point is the day you cannot make payroll.

**Key Risks for the Bank Presentation**
When requesting the larger line of credit, frame these specific risks to show you understand the exposure:
1.  **Human Capital Attrition:** The primary risk is not material cost, but the inability to retain 12 specialized builders during a production halt. They will find work elsewhere.

3.  **Supply Chain Concentration:** Reliance on a single delivery window creates a binary failure point; propose diversifying suppliers as a mitigation strategy funded by the credit line.
4.  **Inventory Valuation:** While you hold more inventory (raw materials), it is illiquid. The bank must understand that raw wood/resin is collateral, but only if the labor exists to convert it.
