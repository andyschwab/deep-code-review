You should implement a **cumulative preferred return with a catch-up mechanism** distributed on a **fund-level waterfall** rather than deal-by-deal, as this balances the asynchronous cash flows while protecting early capital without requiring you to hold excessive idle cash.

In film funds with lumpy, territory-by-territory revenue, the standard approach is to aggregate all inflows and outflows at the fund level before calculating individual investor distributions. This prevents the inequity where early investors in a quick-turnaround film are paid out while investors in a slow-burn documentary wait years. Your structure should follow this logic:

1.  **Return of Capital:** 100% of net proceeds go to investors pro-rata until every investor has received back 100% of their committed capital. Given your £2m raise across ten angels, if you receive a £150k distribution, it is split among all investors based on their ownership percentage, not allocated solely to the specific film's backers. This ensures no one is "left behind" if their specific film delays.
2.  **Preferred Return (Hurdle):** Once capital is returned, subsequent distributions pay a preferred return (e.g., 6–8% simple or compound annual interest) to investors until that hurdle is met. This compensates early capital for the time value of money without needing to freeze cash reserves.
3.  **Catch-Up:** If you have different vintages of investment (investors joining at different times), a "catch-up" clause ensures that once the preferred return is paid to early investors, the next tranche of profits goes disproportionately to later investors until their internal rate of return (IRR) aligns with the early group.
4.  **Carried Interest:** Only after capital and preferred returns are fully satisfied does the fund manager (you) participate in the profits (typically 20%).

Regarding your concern about idle capital: You do not need to hold cash physically to equalize returns. The equalization happens via the **accounting waterfall**. You distribute cash as it arrives according to the priority list above. If a later investor is "behind" in cumulative returns, the formula directs the next available pound to them until they catch up. This keeps capital working rather than sitting in a non-interest-bearing account.

On the tax front for UK investors: Distributing profits as they arise under a standard partnership or Limited Partnership (LP) structure generally creates a "flow-through" tax event, which is efficient. However, introducing complex preferred returns or catch-up mechanisms can sometimes trigger unintended tax interpretations regarding whether payments are income or capital gains. While the mechanism described is standard industry practice, the specific tax treatment of the "catch-up" portion for UK residents depends on the fund's legal vehicle (e.g., Limited Partnership vs. Limited Company) and the specific wording of the Limited Partnership Agreement (LPA).
