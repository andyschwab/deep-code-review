Do not move your cold storage to Southeast Asia based on current information; the potential fine exposure (up to €60,000 per incident under GDPR, plus reputational damage) and the complexity of establishing a lawful transfer mechanism for sensitive legal data likely outweigh the €13,000 monthly savings. While no law explicitly bans all overseas storage, the default rule under the EU GDPR is that personal data cannot leave the European Economic Area (EEA) unless specific, rigorous conditions are met.

**The Regime and General Rule**
Your operation falls squarely under the **General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR)**, enforced by authorities in Germany, France, and Italy. Legal case files containing client names, case details, or communications constitute "personal data." The GDPR prohibits transferring this data to "third countries" (like those in Southeast Asia) that lack an EU "adequacy decision." Most Southeast Asian nations do not have this status.

**Why Encryption and Archiving Are Not Automatic Exemptions**
You asked if encryption or "cold" status creates an exemption. Generally, they do not:
1.  **Encryption:** While encrypting data is a required security measure, it does not legally negate the act of "transfer." If you hold the keys in the EU, it strengthens your security posture, but regulators still view the physical location of the data bits as a transfer requiring a legal basis.
2.  **Archived Data:** There is no blanket exemption for "cold" or rarely accessed data. If the data identifies a living person, the GDPR applies regardless of access frequency. The only narrow exception is if the data is truly anonymized (irreversibly stripped of all identifiers), but legal case files rarely meet this standard as they must remain linked to specific clients for future reference.

**Lawful Transfer Mechanisms (The Hard Part)**
To move data legally without an adequacy decision, you must implement one of the following, all of which require legal validation:
*   **Standard Contractual Clauses (SCCs):** You must sign EU-approved contracts with the Southeast Asian provider. However, following the *Schrems II* ruling, signing the paper is not enough. You must perform a "Transfer Impact Assessment" to prove the local laws of the Southeast Asian country do not undermine those clauses (e.g., can their government access your data?).
*   **Binding Corporate Rules:** Too complex and costly for a company of your size.

**Risk Calculation**
*   **Savings:** €13,000/month = €156,000/year.
*   **Exposure:** Fines can reach 4% of global annual turnover (€60,000 based on last year's revenue) or €20 million, whichever is higher. More critically, a violation can lead to an order to stop processing, effectively shutting down your service.
*   **The Verdict:** The savings are attractive, but the cost of performing a valid Transfer Impact Assessment and implementing supplementary technical measures often exceeds the hosting savings for mid-sized firms. Furthermore, if the Southeast Asian jurisdiction has broad state-access laws, no technical measure may be sufficient to make the transfer legal.

**Next Steps Before Any Decision**
Do not sign a contract with the overseas provider yet. You must engage a qualified EU data privacy counsel to:
1.  Confirm if your specific data sets can be anonymized (removing them from GDPR scope).
2.  Draft and validate the necessary Standard Contractual Clauses.
3.  Perform the mandatory Transfer Impact Assessment for the specific destination country.
